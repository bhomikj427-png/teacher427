# 07 — Effect-Size Honesty (How to Claim the Evidence)

The most important methodological finding of the whole project. It changes *how strongly we
believe and state the numbers*, not *which techniques we use*.

## The problem

Published effect sizes in education/psychology are **systematically inflated** by publication bias
(positive results get published; nulls don't) and questionable research practices.

**Headline comparison** (Neglect of publication bias, *PLOS ONE* 2021): ~**800 education
meta-analyses average d ≈ 0.40** ("almost everything works"), but **pre-registered large RCTs
average d ≈ 0.06**. In psychology broadly, **multi-lab replications run ~⅓** the meta-analytic
size. Most meta-analyses don't correct for this and **overestimate** both the evidence for, and
size of, effects.

## What it means for every number in this folder

Retrieval g≈0.5–0.7, self-explanation g=0.55, interleaving g=0.42, the Mayer d's up to 1.67,
PS-I g=0.36 — all come from the kind of meta-analysis prone to inflation. **Treat them as
optimistic upper bounds, not expected real-world gains.** The biggest numbers are the *most*
likely to shrink (note: Bloom's mythic 2σ tutoring → ~0.37 SD in modern RCTs is this exact
phenomenon).

## What to do instead (the operating rules)

1. **Rank by robustness, not magnitude.** A smaller effect that replicates in pre-registered
   *classroom* RCTs beats a larger lab-only effect. This is why **retrieval practice and
   spacing** sit at the top — they survived independent reviews (Weinstein 2018, EEF), a hostile
   transfer analysis (Pan & Rickard), *and* multi-year classroom programs (Agarwal).
2. **Expect modest gains and still pursue them.** A true d ≈ 0.2–0.4 sustained across a whole
   course is genuinely valuable and **compounds**.
3. **Distrust any single large effect size.** Prefer "this replicates, including in classrooms"
   over quoting one impressive d.
4. **Prefer techniques with both lab and field evidence** when choosing what to lean on.

## The claim rule for the engine

> State techniques as **"well-supported and worth doing,"** never as **"guaranteed to produce
> effect size X."** The honest sell is **robustness and replication**, not magnitude. When a
> learner (or the user) asks "does this work?", the truthful answer is: *"the best-evidenced
> methods reliably help, by amounts that are real but usually smaller than headline studies
> claim — and we chose them precisely because they keep working when tested hard."*

This humility is not a weakness of the engine — it's what separates it from the confident
pop-pedagogy it was built to avoid.

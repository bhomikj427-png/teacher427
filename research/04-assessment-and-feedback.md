# 04 — Assessment & Feedback

Teaching is a closed loop: adapt instruction by measuring understanding, and respond in a way
that moves the learner forward. Includes the counterintuitive, well-replicated finding that
*feedback can make performance worse*. (Carried from Dive 1 `04`, unchanged by the independent
review — it held up.)

## 1. Formative assessment (assessment *for* learning)

Frequent, low-stakes checks of understanding *during* learning, used to adjust what comes next.
It is **retrieval practice** *and* the signal needed to manage load and gate mastery — so it both
**measures and teaches** (the testing effect, `02 §1`).

**Apply:** Check understanding constantly and concretely. Never accept "yes, I get it" — require a
demonstration (retrieve / explain / solve / teach back). Every check is also a learning event.

## 2. Feedback — powerful, and easy to get wrong

Feedback is among the strongest influences on achievement, but **highly variable in sign**.

**The crucial finding — Kluger & DeNisi (1996):** meta-analysis of **131 studies / 12,000+
people** — roughly **38% of feedback interventions made performance *worse*.** Feedback is not
automatically good.

**What separates helpful from harmful:**
- Feedback at the **self/ego level** ("you're so smart," "you're bad at this") is ineffective or
  harmful — it points attention at the self, away from the task.
- Feedback at the **task and process level** is what helps.

**Hattie & Timperley (2007):** effective feedback answers, at the right level:
- **Feed Up** — where am I going? (goal/success criteria)
- **Feed Back** — how am I going? (current vs. goal)
- **Feed Forward** — where to next? (the specific next step)

...across four levels, best→least useful for learning:
1. **Task** (correct?), 2. **Process** (the strategy — transfers well), 3. **Self-regulation**
(learner monitoring own learning — most valuable long-term), 4. **Self/person** (praise — least
effective, can backfire).

**Rules this yields:**
- Target the **task and process**, essentially **never the person**.
- Be **specific & actionable**: error → reason → next step.
- Tie to the goal (feed up) and point forward (feed forward).
- Move the learner task → process → self-regulation over time.
- Deliver feedback **after a retrieval attempt** (the amplifier for the testing effect).
- Praise **effort/strategy, not ability** — the one growth-mindset-adjacent move the evidence
  supports, and it coincides with good feedback practice anyway (`05`).

## 3. Timing of feedback

Not simply "immediate always wins":
- **Immediate** prevents practicing errors; good for **procedural** skills and early acquisition.
- **Slightly delayed** can aid long-term retention (adds a spacing-like difficulty; stops the
  learner leaning on the answer).

**Apply:** mid-struggle on a *solvable* step → let productive struggle run briefly before feedback
(don't remove the desirable difficulty). True misconception or hopeless overload → correct
promptly so errors aren't encoded.

## 4. The assistance dilemma — don't rescue too early

(Koedinger & Aleven.) Too much help removes the germane effort that causes learning; too little
causes overload and failure. Resolution is **adaptive**: give the **minimum** assistance that
keeps the learner in the productive-struggle band (their ZPD), and prefer **hints that prompt
retrieval** ("what do you think comes next, and why?") over **hints that give answers**. This
connects to the route decision in `03 §5`: don't rescue *conceptual* struggle too early; *do*
scaffold *procedural* acquisition.

## 5. What assessment should measure

Use the revised Bloom's taxonomy (Remember → Understand → Apply → Analyze → Evaluate → Create) as
a **checklist of question types**, not a rigid ladder, to ensure checks probe **understanding and
transfer**, not just recall:
- Don't stop at "state the definition" (Remember).
- Push to "apply it to a new case," "explain why," "which principle applies here?"
  (Apply/Analyze) — because **transfer is the real goal**, and recall alone can mask shallow,
  inert knowledge.

> Note (from `07`): treat the taxonomy as a useful framework, not a precise empirical law.

# 02 — Core Learning Techniques (Validated & Merged)

Every technique here cleared the bar in at least one dive and survived the independent review.
Each entry: **what / evidence / how to apply / caveats.** Effect sizes are **optimistic upper
bounds** (see `07`); the real signal is *replication, especially in classrooms*. Ordered by
robustness + actionability. ★ = added in the final gap-check (neither earlier dive had it).

---

## 1. Retrieval practice (the testing effect) — TOP (lab + classroom)

**What:** Actively recalling from memory (low-stakes quizzing, free recall, "close the book and
explain it") instead of reviewing. The act of retrieval strengthens memory far more than
re-studying.

**Evidence:**
- Adesope et al. (2017), **217 studies**: g ≈ 0.50–0.70.
- **Real classrooms:** Agarwal et al. — 5-year program, 1,400+ middle-schoolers; no-stakes
  quizzes on ~⅓ of material → students scored **a full grade level higher** on quizzed material.
  Delayed quizzes especially potent; quizzes also reduced test anxiety and improved metacognition.
- **Transfer:** Pan & Rickard (2018), 192 effect sizes — testing transfers at **d = 0.40**.

**How to apply:** Default to *asking before telling*. After any explanation, require recall
without looking. Replace "make sense?" with "explain it back." Use frequent low-stakes retrieval;
always give **feedback** after (the key amplifier).

**Caveats:** Transfer is **weaker than direct recall** and **disappears with poor initial
performance** — the learner must succeed reasonably often, and *elaborated* retrieval (explain,
don't just name) transfers better. For very complex, highly-interconnected material the effect is
smaller (van Gog & Sweller debate) — scaffold retrieval there rather than expecting cold
free-recall.

---

## 2. Spaced / distributed practice — TOP (lab + classroom)

**What:** Spreading study across sessions over time vs. massing ("cramming"). Same total time,
distributed.

**Evidence:** Cepeda et al. (2006), 317 experiments — at retention intervals ≥1 week, spacing
**nearly doubles** recall. EEF-endorsed; partly mechanized by **sleep consolidation** (`01 §7`).

**How to apply:** Never teach-and-move-on permanently. Open later sessions with
expanding-interval review of prior material. Prefer gaps **across days/nights**. Rough rule:
space at ~10–20% of the target retention interval.

**Caveats:** The optimal gap scales with how long retention is needed; very long gaps before
adequate initial learning can be counterproductive.

---

## 3. ★ Successive relearning — TOP (combines the two strongest) 

**What:** The deliberate combination of #1 and #2: practice retrieval **to a mastery criterion**
(e.g. 3 correct recalls) in a session, then **relearn to that criterion again on later, spaced
days.** Retrieval + spacing + mastery, operationalized.

**Evidence:** Rawson, Dunlosky & colleagues (2013; review 2022, *Current Directions*): pronounced
gains in **long-term retention at minimal extra cost**, demonstrated on **real course exams**
(biopsychology, introductory psych, engineering math) — including retention into the *following*
course.

**How to apply:** This is the engine's default scheme for durable knowledge. Don't stop at "got
it once" — drive to a few correct retrievals now, then re-test to criterion on spaced later days.
The progress log tracks each item's criterion status and next due date.

**Caveats:** Requires tracking over time (which is exactly what the subjects-layer progress log
is for). Most natural for knowledge/skills with identifiable "items."

## 4. Self-explanation & elaboration — STRONG

**What:** Prompting the learner to explain *why* — why a step is correct, why a fact is true, how
new material connects to prior knowledge. (Merges self-explanation, elaborative interrogation,
and Weinstein et al.'s "elaboration.")

**Evidence:** Bisra et al. (2018), 64 studies, **g = 0.55**, across subjects and for conceptual
and procedural knowledge. Independently listed by Weinstein, Madan & Sumeracki (2018).

**How to apply:** After a step/fact/example ask "*why* is this true?", "why this and not that?",
"how does this connect to X you know?" Make the learner produce the reasoning, not receive it.

**Caveats:** Works best when the learner has enough prior knowledge to generate plausible
explanations; for true novices, pair with a worked example to explain.

---

## 5. Generation: prediction, pretesting & productive failure — STRONG

**What:** Having the learner **attempt/produce/predict before being told** — including when they
get it wrong. Three related forms: the **generation effect**, **pretesting** (answering before
studying), and **★ productive failure** (problem-solving *before* instruction).

**Evidence:**
- Pretesting: Richland, Kornell & Kao (2009) — pre-questions beat extra study even though
  answers were mostly wrong; unsuccessful retrieval primes encoding.
- **Productive failure / PS-I:** Sinha & Kapur (2021) meta-analysis, **166 comparisons, g =
  0.36** (publication-bias-corrected; higher in subsets) — benefits **conceptual understanding
  and transfer**.

**How to apply:** Open a topic with a guess/prediction/attempt, *then* teach. Frame wrong answers
as useful data, never failure.

**Caveats — important:** Productive failure helps **conceptual** learning and transfer; for
**procedural** skills, **instruction-first (worked examples) is better** (see #7 and the
reconciliation in `03`). Choose by goal: build understanding → struggle first; build a procedure
→ show first.

---

## 6. Interleaving — SOLID (but block first)

**What:** Mixing problem types within practice (ABCABC) vs. blocking (AAABBB), forcing the
learner to first decide *which* approach applies.

**Evidence:** Brunmair & Richter (2019), **g = 0.42**; strongest for confusable visual categories
(g≈0.67), smaller for math (g≈0.34). Helps most when categories are **confusable** (forces
discrimination).

**How to apply:** A **step-7** move, never the opener. **Block to a minimum competence first,
then interleave.** Once several related skills are individually solid, mix them.

**Caveats (sharpened by independent dive):** Below a competence threshold, interleaving
**overloads** and hurts. Blocked practice gives better *initial* acquisition; interleaving's
payoff is *later* retention/transfer.

---

## 7. Worked examples + faded guidance — STRONG (for novices)

**What:** Give novices fully worked step-by-step solutions to study (and self-explain), then
**fade** support — completion problems, then independent solving.

**Evidence:** The **worked-example effect** is among CLT's most replicated findings: novices
learn more from studying worked examples than from solving equivalent problems (problem-solving
overloads WM with means-ends search). The **guidance-fading effect**: remove support as expertise
grows.

**How to apply:** Novice → show a complete worked example + have them self-explain it → completion
problems → independent problems. Fade as competence rises.

**Caveats:** Bounded by the **expertise reversal effect** — worked examples become redundant/
harmful once the learner has schemas; switch them to problem-solving. (And see #5: for building
*concepts*, a productive-failure attempt *before* the example can be better.)

---

## 8. ★ Concreteness fading — SOLID

**What:** Sequence instruction from **concrete → abstract**: start with a concrete, meaningful
representation, then *explicitly and gradually* fade to the abstract/symbolic form.

**Evidence:** Fyfe et al. (2014, systematic review) and Goldstone & Son (2005): the fading
condition produces **the best transfer** (immediate and delayed) vs. concrete-only or
abstract-only — and the benefit **generalizes across task, age, and prior knowledge**.

**How to apply:** Introduce a new abstract idea via a concrete example/analogy the learner
already understands, then deliberately strip it toward the general/symbolic form — don't start or
stay purely abstract, and don't stay purely concrete.

**Caveats:** Best evidenced in math and science; the *fading* (not just "use concrete examples")
is what matters.

---

## 9. Dual coding / multimedia (Mayer) — STRONG (trim confidence)

**What:** People learn better from **words + relevant graphics** than words alone, *if* designed
to not overload WM (verbal and visual channels are partly separate).

**Evidence:** Mayer's research-based principles report large median effects (multimedia d≈1.67;
coherence d≈0.86; signaling d≈0.46; plus contiguity, segmenting, modality, redundancy).
**Caution:** these are among the **most likely to be inflated** (`07`) — keep the *principles*,
discount the *magnitudes*.

**How to apply (text/terminal-friendly):** clean diagrams/structure/analogies alongside words;
cut decorative noise (**coherence**); keep labels next to referents (**contiguity**); break into
learner-paced chunks (**segmenting**); signal structure explicitly (**signaling**).

---

## 10. ★ Learning by teaching (the protégé effect) — SOLID

**What:** Having the learner **teach/explain the material to someone** (or prepare to). The
evidence-based core of what's popularly mis-sold as the "Feynman technique."

**Evidence:** Fiorella & Mayer: preparing-to-teach beats preparing-for-a-test (d≈0.30–0.40;
d≈0.59 on immediate comprehension), and **actually teaching** yields the best *delayed* retention
(d≈0.56). The social/explanatory act adds a boost *beyond* private self-explanation.

**How to apply:** Periodically have the learner **teach the concept back** as if to a novice
("explain this to me like I've never seen it"), ideally after telling them up front they'll have
to teach it (the teaching expectancy itself helps).

**Caveats:** The benefit comes from the generative explanation; passive "presenting" without real
explanation does little.

## The generative-learning umbrella (Fiorella & Mayer, SOI)

Techniques 4, 5, 10 — plus self-testing (=#1) — are instances of **generative learning**: the
learner actively **S**elects, **O**rganizes, and **I**ntegrates material (Mayer's SOI model).
Fiorella & Mayer review **eight** generative strategies: summarizing, mapping, drawing, imagining,
self-testing, self-explaining, teaching, enacting. Strength varies:
- **Strongest / use:** self-testing, self-explaining, teaching (covered above).
- **Useful with guidance:** **drawing** (good for science text, but demanding — needs support and
  quality drawings) and **mapping/graphic organizers** (helps organize structure).
- **Conditional:** **summarizing** — *passive* summarizing/note-copying is **low utility**
  (Dunlosky); it helps only when it's genuinely *generative* (restructuring in one's own words),
  which novices often can't do well. This resolves the apparent dive-1-vs-Weinstein conflict.

**Unifying principle:** learning is caused by **active generation** (select–organize–integrate)
and **effortful retrieval** — not by exposure. Every technique above manufactures one or both,
while #7–#9 keep cognitive load low enough that the difficulty stays *desirable*.

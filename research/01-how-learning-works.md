# 01 — How Learning Works (Cognitive Architecture)

Every technique in `02` is a consequence of how memory is built. Learn this and the techniques
become *derivable* rather than a list. (Merged from Dive 1 `01`, with the independent dive's
hedges and the newly-verified sleep/consolidation mechanism added.)

## 1. Working memory vs. long-term memory

**Working memory (WM)** — where conscious thought happens. Severely limited: ~**4 ± 1** novel
elements at once (Cowan; the older "7 ± 2" overcounts for genuinely new items), decaying in
seconds. This bottleneck is the single biggest constraint on learning; overload it and learning
stops.

**Long-term memory (LTM)** — effectively unlimited and durable. Expertise *is* large, organized
LTM. Crucially LTM **expands WM's effective capacity** via **schemas**: an expert sees one chunk
where a novice sees twenty elements.

> **Goal of teaching:** build organized schemas in LTM without overloading the fragile WM
> bottleneck on the way.

## 2. Cognitive Load Theory — useful framework, with one honest caveat

Total WM load has three sources:
- **Intrinsic** — inherent difficulty (interacting elements). Reduce by *sequencing*, not by
  dumbing down.
- **Extraneous** — load from poor presentation (clutter, split attention, redundancy). Wasted;
  minimize ruthlessly.
- **Germane** — the productive effort of building schemas. Spend freed capacity here.

**The caveat (from the independent dive):** the **"germane load" construct became
unfalsifiable** — any result could be relabeled post-hoc (drop = "extraneous," gain = "germane")
with no independent measure. **Sweller himself dropped germane load as a separate category.** So:
use CLT's *actionable, measurable* claims — minimize extraneous load, sequence intrinsic load,
respect WM limits — but treat CLT as a **design heuristic, not a precise predictive law**, and
don't invoke "germane load" as an explanation.

## 3. The expertise reversal effect (and aptitude–treatment interactions)

What helps a novice can **harm** an expert. Worked examples and heavy guidance help novices (no
schemas yet) but become redundant/harmful for experts (guidance conflicts with existing schemas).

The independent dive deepened this into a general rule: **the same manipulation helps some
learners and harms others** (aptitude–treatment interactions). Two moderators dominate:
- **Prior knowledge** — drives the expertise reversal.
- **Working-memory capacity** — **lower-WMC learners benefit *more* from worked examples** and
  are more easily overloaded by unguided difficulty.

Poorly-fitted difficulty doesn't just fail — it can "emotionally upset and de-motivate."

> **Implication:** *diagnose, then adapt* is not optional polish — it is load-bearing. A fixed
> difficulty for all learners is wrong by construction.

## 4. Storage strength vs. retrieval strength (Bjork & Bjork)

A memory has two independent strengths:
- **Storage strength** — how deeply learned; essentially only grows.
- **Retrieval strength** — how accessible *right now*; rises and falls fast.

Conditions that make retrieval feel **easy now** (massing, rereading) build little durable
storage; conditions that make it feel **hard now** (spacing, testing, interleaving) build storage
strength. **Performance during practice is a misleading indicator of learning** — independently
confirmed by Soderstrom & Bjork (2015): manipulations can even have *opposite* effects on
performance vs. learning. This is the engine behind "desirable difficulties."

## 5. Desirable difficulties (Bjork) — calibrated, not maximal

Difficulties that *enhance* long-term learning: retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving,
varying conditions, and generation (producing before being shown). They slow apparent progress
while increasing durable learning and transfer.

**They are still difficulties.** Independent evidence: they only help **above a minimum
competence threshold** — interleaving and unguided struggle *overload* learners below it. The
difficulty must sit in the learner's reachable band (hence the ~80% success target, `03`).

## 6. The fluency illusion

Rereading and watching an expert make material feel *familiar and fluent*; learners misread this
as mastery and stop early. This is the most common self-sabotage in learning, and why subjective
confidence is a poor guide and the engine trusts **retrieval evidence**, not "yes, I get it."

## 7. Forgetting is the default — and sleep consolidates

Ebbinghaus: new material decays rapidly without reinforcement. Forgetting is normal memory
operation. The fix is **retrieval at expanding intervals** (spaced repetition; Leitner / SM-2),
each successful effortful recall flattening the curve.

**Sleep & consolidation (newly added, robust evidence):** sleep — especially slow-wave sleep,
with slow oscillations and spindles — **actively consolidates** newly formed declarative and
motor memories. Retention is reliably better after sleep than after equivalent waking. This is a
major reason **spacing across nights beats cramming**: the gaps aren't just "less forgetting,"
they include offline consolidation.
- *Honest limits:* the teacher can't control the learner's sleep, and "sleep then learn next
  day" benefits are more complex than "sleep fixes everything." Actionable takeaways: prefer
  spaced sessions **across days/nights**, not just within one sitting; don't promise miracles.

# 04 — Assessment & Feedback

Teaching is a closed loop: you cannot adapt instruction without measuring understanding, and
measurement is worthless without a response that moves the learner forward. This file covers
both halves — and the critical, counterintuitive finding that *feedback can make performance
worse*.

---

## 1. Formative assessment (assessment *for* learning)

**What:** Frequent, low-stakes checking of understanding *during* learning, used to adjust
what happens next — as opposed to summative assessment (grading *after*).

**Why it works:** It is retrieval practice (`02 §1`) *and* it gives the teacher the signal
needed to manage cognitive load and gate mastery (`03 §3`). The testing effect means assessment
is not just measurement — the act of being assessed *itself teaches*.

**How to apply:** Check understanding constantly and concretely. Never accept "yes I get it."
Make the learner *demonstrate* it by retrieving, explaining, or solving. Every check is both a
measurement and a learning event.

---

## 2. The power — and danger — of feedback

Feedback is one of the most powerful influences on achievement, but its effect is **highly
variable in sign and size**.

**The crucial finding (Kluger & DeNisi, 1996):** a meta-analysis of **131 studies / 12,000+
people** found that roughly **38% of feedback interventions made performance *worse*.**
Feedback is not automatically good.

**What determines whether feedback helps or hurts:**
- Feedback directed at the **self / ego** ("you're so smart," "you're bad at this") tends to be
  **ineffective or harmful** — it directs attention to the self and away from the task.
- Feedback directed at the **task and the process** ("this step is wrong because…", "try this
  strategy") is what helps.

**Hattie & Timperley (2007), "The Power of Feedback":** effective feedback answers three
questions, at the right level:
- **Feed Up:** Where am I going? (the goal / success criteria)
- **Feed Back:** How am I going? (current performance vs. goal)
- **Feed Forward:** Where to next? (the specific next step)

...operating at up to four levels, in rough order of usefulness for learning:
1. **Task level** — is it correct? (useful, but doesn't transfer far on its own)
2. **Process level** — the strategy/method behind the task (highly valuable; transfers)
3. **Self-regulation level** — the learner monitoring and directing their own learning (most
   valuable for autonomy)
4. **Self / personal level** — praise of the person ("good job!") — **least effective; can be
   counterproductive.**

**How to apply (the rules this yields):**
- Give feedback on the **task and process**, essentially never on the **person**.
- Be **specific and actionable** — name the error, the reason, and the next step.
- Tie it to the **goal** (feed up) and point to the **next move** (feed forward).
- Aim to move the learner from task → process → self-regulation over time.
- Provide it **after a retrieval attempt** (this is the feedback moderator that amplifies the
  testing effect — `02 §1`).

---

## 3. Timing of feedback

The evidence is nuanced rather than "immediate always wins":
- **Immediate** feedback prevents errors from being practiced and is good for procedural skills
  and early acquisition.
- **Slightly delayed** feedback can benefit long-term retention (it adds a spacing-like
  difficulty and avoids the learner leaning on the answer).

**How to apply:** For a learner mid-struggle on a solvable step, let the productive struggle run
briefly before feedback (don't rescue too early — that removes the desirable difficulty). For
outright misconceptions or when the learner is stuck with no path forward, correct promptly so
errors aren't encoded.

---

## 4. Don't rescue too early — the assistance dilemma

There is a genuine tension (the "assistance dilemma," Koedinger & Aleven): too much help
removes the germane effort that causes learning; too little help causes overload and failure.
The resolution is **adaptive**: give the *minimum* assistance that keeps the learner in the
productive-struggle band (their ZPD), and prefer **hints that prompt retrieval** ("what do you
think comes next, and why?") over **hints that give answers**.

---

## 5. What assessment should measure

Bloom's taxonomy (revised: Remember → Understand → Apply → Analyze → Evaluate → Create) is a
useful *ladder of cognitive demand*. Use it to ensure checks probe **understanding and
transfer**, not just recall:

- Don't stop at "can you state the definition?" (Remember).
- Push to "apply it to a new case," "explain why," "spot which principle applies here"
  (Apply/Analyze) — because **transfer** is the real goal, and recall alone can hide a shallow,
  inert understanding.

> Treat the taxonomy as a checklist of question types, **not** as a rigid sequence you must
> climb in order. Its empirical status is as a useful framework, not a precise law.

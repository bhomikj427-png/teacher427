# 02 — Evidence-Based Learning Techniques

Each technique below states: **what it is**, **the evidence** (study, scale, effect size),
**how to apply it**, and **the caveats**. Ordered roughly by strength and breadth of support.
Effect sizes use Cohen's *d* / Hedges' *g* (≈0.2 small, ≈0.5 medium, ≈0.8 large).

---

## 1. Retrieval practice (the testing effect) — STRONGEST

**What:** Actively recalling information from memory (low-stakes quizzing, free recall,
flashcards, "close the book and explain it") rather than reviewing it. The *act of retrieval*
itself strengthens memory far more than re-studying.

**Evidence:**
- Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017), meta-analysis of **217 studies**: medium-to-large
  effect, **g ≈ 0.50–0.61** (some summaries report up to ~0.70).
- Roediger & Karpicke (2006) is the foundational demonstration: testing beats restudying for
  long-term retention, even though restudying *feels* more effective short-term.
- Rated **high utility** by Dunlosky et al. (2013).

**Moderators (what makes it work better):**
- **Feedback** after retrieval amplifies the effect (corrects errors, confirms successes).
- **Retrievability** — items must be at least somewhat retrievable; pure failure with no
  feedback helps little.
- Format matters; matching practice format to the eventual test format helps.

**How to apply:** Default to *asking* before *telling*. After any explanation, require the
learner to recall/reconstruct it without looking. Use frequent low-stakes quizzes. Replace
"does that make sense?" (which invites a fluent "yes") with "explain it back to me."

---

## 2. Spaced / distributed practice — STRONGEST

**What:** Spreading study across multiple sessions over time rather than massing it into one
block ("cramming"). Same total study time, distributed differently.

**Evidence:**
- Cepeda et al. (2006), meta-analysis: **184 articles, 317 experiments, 839 assessments.**
  At retention intervals of a week or more, spaced practice **nearly doubles** recall vs.
  massed practice.
- Rated **high utility** by Dunlosky et al. (2013).

**Key finding — the optimal gap scales with how long you need to remember:** the longer the
retention interval (when you'll be tested / need it), the longer the optimal inter-study
interval. Rough rule of thumb from the literature: space at roughly 10–20% of the target
retention interval (remember for a week → review ~1 day later; for a month → ~1 week later).

**How to apply:** Never teach-and-move-on permanently. Schedule **expanding-interval review**
of previously learned material at the start of later sessions. This is the basis of spaced
repetition systems (Leitner boxes, SM-2/Anki). Cramming is explicitly the anti-pattern.

---

## 3. Self-explanation — STRONG

**What:** Prompting the learner to explain *to themselves* — why a step is correct, how a new
fact connects to what they know, what a worked example is doing and why.

**Evidence:**
- Bisra et al. (2018), meta-analysis: **69 effect sizes from 64 studies (~6,000 participants)**,
  overall **g = 0.55**. Works across subject areas and for both conceptual and procedural
  knowledge. The authors rate it as powerful as mastery learning or peer tutoring.

**How to apply:** After presenting a step/example, ask "*why* is this true?", "why this step
and not another?", "how does this connect to X you already know?" Make the learner produce the
reasoning rather than receive it.

---

## 4. Elaborative interrogation — SOLID

**What:** Generating explanations for *why* a stated fact is true ("Why would that be the
case?"). A close cousin of self-explanation, focused on factual material.

**Evidence:** Rated **moderate utility** by Dunlosky et al. (2013) — reliable benefits,
somewhat narrower conditions than retrieval/spacing. Works best when learners have enough
prior knowledge to generate plausible explanations.

**How to apply:** When introducing facts, repeatedly ask "why?" and have the learner reason
toward the answer before confirming.

---

## 5. Pretesting / the generation effect — SOLID

**What:** Attempting to answer questions *before* studying the material — even when you get
them wrong. Also: generating an answer before being shown it.

**Evidence:**
- Richland, Kornell & Kao (2009), 5 experiments: a **pretesting effect** — being tested on
  embedded concepts *before* reading outperformed extra study time, even though pretest
  answers were largely wrong. Unsuccessful retrieval attempts enhance later learning.
- Part of the broader "errorful generation" literature: productive failure primes the mind to
  encode the correct answer when it arrives.

**How to apply:** Open a new topic by asking the learner to guess/predict/attempt *first*,
then teach. Frame wrong answers as useful, not as failure. (Pairs with a no-penalty-for-errors
tone — see motivation file.)

---

## 6. Interleaving — SOLID

**What:** Mixing different problem types or categories within a practice session (ABCABC)
rather than blocking one type at a time (AAABBBCCC).

**Evidence:**
- Brunmair & Richter (2019), meta-analysis in *Psychological Bulletin*: overall **g = 0.42**.
  Strongest for **visual/inductive category learning** (g ≈ 0.67), still positive but smaller
  for **mathematics** (g ≈ 0.34).
- **Key moderator:** interleaving helps most when categories are **confusable** (high
  between-category similarity) because it forces the learner to *discriminate* — to notice
  which kind of problem this is and which method applies. It can *hurt* when items within a
  category are highly similar.

**How to apply:** Once several related skills/types are introduced, mix them in practice so the
learner must first decide *which* approach to use — not just execute a pre-announced one.
Don't interleave too early (before each type is individually understood).

---

## 7. Worked examples + faded guidance — STRONG (for novices)

**What:** Giving novices fully worked step-by-step solutions to study, then gradually removing
steps for them to complete (completion problems), until they solve independently.

**Evidence:**
- The **worked-example effect** is one of cognitive load theory's most replicated findings:
  novices learn more from studying worked examples than from solving equivalent problems
  (problem-solving overloads WM with means-ends search, leaving no capacity for schema-building).
- The **guidance-fading effect**: support should be removed as expertise grows.
- Bounded by the **expertise reversal effect** (see `01`): worked examples become redundant /
  harmful for learners who already have schemas — switch them to problem-solving.

**How to apply:** For a novice, *show a complete worked example and have them self-explain it*
before asking them to solve. Then use completion problems (some steps filled, some blank).
Then independent problems. Fade as competence rises.

---

## 8. Multimedia / dual-coding principles (Mayer) — STRONG

**What:** People learn better from **words + relevant graphics** than words alone, *if* the
presentation is designed to not overload WM. Verbal and visual channels are partly separate
(dual coding), so using both well increases effective capacity.

**Evidence (Mayer's research-based principles, median effect sizes):**
- **Multimedia principle** (words + graphics > words alone): **d ≈ 1.67**
- **Temporal contiguity** (present words & pictures simultaneously, not sequentially): d ≈ 1.30
- **Redundancy** (don't add on-screen text duplicating narration): d ≈ 0.87
- **Coherence** (exclude extraneous material — "seductive details" hurt): d ≈ 0.86 (23/23 tests)
- **Spatial contiguity** (place labels next to what they describe): d ≈ 0.79
- **Segmenting** (break into learner-paced chunks): d ≈ 0.70
- **Modality** (narrate graphics rather than captioning): d ≈ 0.72
- **Signaling** (cue the essential structure): d ≈ 0.46

**How to apply (text/terminal-friendly translation):** Use clean diagrams, structure, and
analogies alongside verbal explanation; cut decorative noise; keep related ideas adjacent;
break complex material into learner-paced steps; signal structure explicitly ("there are three
parts; here's the first").

---

## The unifying principle

Techniques 1, 2, 5, 6 are all forms of **desirable difficulty** (make retrieval effortful).
Techniques 3, 4 force **active generation of meaning**. Techniques 7, 8 **manage cognitive
load** so the difficulty stays *desirable* and not crushing. None of them make learning feel
easier in the moment — that is precisely why they work, and why learners avoid them.

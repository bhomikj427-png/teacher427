# 03 — Effect-Size Inflation (The Most Important Caveat)

This is the single most consequential finding of the independent dive. It does not change *which*
techniques to use, but it changes *how strongly we should believe the numbers* — and it's the
kind of thing a fluent-but-uncritical first pass tends to omit.

## The problem

Published effect sizes in education and psychology are **systematically inflated** by publication
bias (positive results get published; null results don't) and questionable research practices.

**The headline comparison (Neglect of publication bias, PLOS ONE 2021):**

> Field-wide synthesis of **~800 education meta-analyses** yields an average effect of **d ≈
> 0.40** — "almost everything works." But **pre-registered, large-scale RCTs average just d ≈
> 0.06.**

And in psychology more broadly: **large-scale multi-lab replications produce effects ~one-third
the size** of the meta-analyses that estimated them. Meta-analyses that don't correct for
publication bias (most of them) **overestimate** both the evidence for, and the size of, effects.

## What this means for the numbers in `../research/`

Every effect size the first dive quoted — retrieval g ≈ 0.5–0.7, self-explanation g = 0.55,
interleaving g = 0.42, the Mayer d's up to 1.67 — comes from exactly the kind of meta-analysis
that tends to run inflated. **Treat them as optimistic upper bounds, not expected real-world
gains.**

This is *not* a reason to abandon the techniques. It's a reason to:

1. **Rank by robustness, not by headline magnitude.** A technique with a *smaller* effect that
   replicates in pre-registered classroom RCTs (retrieval practice, spacing) is worth more than
   a technique with a *larger* lab effect that hasn't been stress-tested.
2. **Expect modest real gains** and still pursue them — a true d ≈ 0.2–0.4 sustained across a
   whole course of study is genuinely valuable and compounds.
3. **Distrust any single large effect size.** The biggest numbers (e.g. some Mayer principle
   d's, Bloom's original 2σ) are the *most* likely to shrink. Note the parallel from the first
   dive: Bloom's mythic 2σ tutoring effect shrank to ~0.37 SD in modern RCTs (`../research/03
   §4`) — that *is* this phenomenon in action, and the first dive already caught that one.

## Why the engine still stands

The two techniques the engine leans on hardest — **retrieval practice and spacing** — are
precisely the two that survived:
- independent reviews (Weinstein 2018, EEF),
- a hostile transfer analysis (Pan & Rickard 2018, d = 0.40),
- and **multi-year real classroom programs** (Agarwal, +1 grade level on quizzed material).

These have cleared the bar that inflation-prone effects fail. So the inflation caveat **trims our
confidence in the magnitudes but not in the choices.** The engine should *teach* with these
methods and *speak* about their power with calibrated humility.

## The rule this adds

> **Claim techniques as "well-supported and worth doing," not as "guaranteed to produce effect
> size X."** When stating evidence, prefer "this replicates, including in classrooms" over
> quoting a single impressive d. The honest sell is robustness, not magnitude.

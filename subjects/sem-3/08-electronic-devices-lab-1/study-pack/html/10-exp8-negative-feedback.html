Exp 8 · Effect of negative feedback (removing the emitter bypass capacitor)

What you already have: from Exp 6, CE bypasses RE, and gain ≈ RC/re. From Exp 7, a resistor in the emitter makes the output "follow" and pulls the gain down.

Two terms first:

  • Feedback: feeding part of the output back to the input.
  • Negative feedback opposes the input. It lowers the gain but steadies everything else.
  • Positive feedback adds to the input. It leads to oscillation (Exp 11).
  • Current-series feedback: the feedback voltage is produced by the output current (IE ≈ IC through RE) and subtracted in series with the input (VBE = vin − IERE).
Manual Exp 8, aim

To observe the effect of negative feedback (emitter bypass capacitor) on the frequency response of an amplifier. Viva form: "Removing one capacitor cuts the gain from 122 to 1.7. Why would anyone want that?"

Guess first

Name one thing that gets better when the gain drops.

The idea

  • C1 in: R4 is shorted for AC, so there is no feedback. Av ≈ (RC ∥ RL)/re, which is large.
  • C1 out: the signal current through R4 (2.2 kΩ) makes a voltage that rises with the input, so the BE voltage the transistor actually sees shrinks. That is negative feedback. Av ≈ (RC ∥ RL)/(re + RE) = 4.27k/2.2k ≈ 1.9 at the base.

What negative feedback buys, at the cost of gain:

Property Effect
gain falls, but becomes set by resistors (RC/RE), not by β or temperature, so it is stable
bandwidth increases (gain is traded for bandwidth)
distortion and noise decrease
input resistance increases (series feedback)

Do it

Do it (LTspice)
  1. Draw Fig 8.1. Q1 = 2N2222. V2: AC amplitude 1. Supply V1 = 15 V. Label the output vo (across R5, 47 kΩ).
  2. Directive: .ac dec 10 1 1Meg (manual: 1 Hz – 1 MHz). Run. Plot V(vo): the solid line is the dB gain.
  3. Read the peak dB with a cursor. Move the cursor to the frequencies where the gain is 3 dB lower: those are fL and fH.
  4. Delete C1 (or set it to 1p). Run again and repeat.
  5. Fill the table at 100 Hz, 500, 1k, 5k, 10k, 50k, 100k, 500k, 1M for both cases.

Hand check (Q-point: VB = 3.56 V, VE = 2.90 V, so IE = 1.32 mA and re = 19.7 Ω):

  • Rin with feedback = 68k ∥ 22k ∥ β × 2.2k ≈ 16 kΩ. The source divider is 16/(16 + 2.2) = 0.88.
  • Avf ≈ 0.88 × 4.27k/2.22k = 1.70 (LTspice: 1.68) ✓.
Write in your record

Result: bandwidth ≈ 135 kHz without feedback (gain 41.7 dB). With negative feedback (CE removed), the gain falls to 4.5 dB and the bandwidth rises to ≈ 4.1 MHz: the higher cut-off is far above 1 MHz, as the manual expects.

Theory for the record: removing the emitter bypass capacitor leaves RE unbypassed. The AC emitter current develops a voltage across RE that subtracts from the input in series: current-series negative feedback. The voltage gain falls from ≈ RC/re to ≈ RC/(re + RE), but becomes stable (independent of β and temperature). The bandwidth increases, distortion and noise fall, and the input resistance rises. Negative feedback trades gain for bandwidth and stability.

Check 1

(a) Gain × bandwidth without feedback, and with feedback. Is the product the same? (b) Why is the 2N2222's own β almost irrelevant to the gain once C1 is out? (c) Which part of the response barely changed between the two cases at 100 kHz–1 MHz, and why?

Answers

Opening question. Negative feedback makes the gain stable and predictable (set by resistors, not β), widens the bandwidth (135 kHz → 4.1 MHz), and reduces distortion and noise. You add gain back with more stages; stability is hard to add later.

Check 1. (a) 122 × 135 kHz ≈ 16.5 MHz; 1.68 × 4.1 MHz ≈ 6.9 MHz. Not the same: "gain × bandwidth = constant" holds for a single dominant pole, not exactly for this circuit, but the direction (gain down, bandwidth up) holds. (b) Avf ≈ (RC ∥ RL)/(re + RE), and RE ≫ re, so β drops out. (c) The without-C1 curve is flat across it, while the with-C1 curve is already falling. With feedback, fH moved out to MHz.

What you already have: from Exp 1, a reverse-biased pn junction carries no current and has a depletion region with no free carriers. From Exp 5, families of output curves and slopes taken as parameters.

Three terms first:

  • FET (field-effect transistor): current flows through a channel, and a voltage on the gate controls the channel's width. It is voltage-controlled. A BJT, by contrast, is controlled by base current.
  • JFET terminals: drain (D, current in), source (S, current out), gate (G, controls).
  • Unipolar: only one carrier type carries the current (electrons, in an n-channel JFET). A BJT uses both electrons and holes, which is why it is called bipolar.
Manual Exp 9, aim · professor's slide

To plot the drain and transfer characteristics of a JFET in CS configuration and determine the device parameters. Viva / 8-mark form: "Define gm, rd, gd and μ, find each from the curves, and show μ = gm rd."

Guess first

Which of the four do you think is read from the transfer curve, and which from the drain curves?

How a JFET works

  1. VGS = 0: the channel is fully open, and current flows from D to S.
  2. VGS more negative: the reverse-biased gate junctions' depletion regions widen into the channel, so the channel narrows and ID falls.
  3. VGS = VP (pinch-off voltage, ≈ −3 V here): the depletion regions meet and ID ≈ 0. The JFET is off.
  4. Raising VDS at fixed VGS: at first ID rises like a resistor (ohmic region). Then the channel pinches off near the drain and ID levels out (saturation, or pinch-off region). The amplifier operates here.
  5. The gate junction is reverse-biased, so gate current ≈ 0: the input resistance is enormous (≫ MΩ). This is the JFET's big advantage.

Shockley's equation (the transfer curve, in saturation):

ID = IDSS (1 − VGSVP)2
  • IDSS = drain current with the gate Shorted to the Source (VGS = 0), in saturation.

The four parameters (the professor's slide)

Parameter Definition Held constant Read from
transconductance gm ΔID/ΔVGS VDS two drain curves at one VDS (or the transfer curve's slope)
drain resistance rd ΔVDS/ΔID VGS one drain curve, in saturation
drain conductance gd ΔID/ΔVDS = 1/rd VGS same
amplification factor μ −ΔVDS/ΔVGS ID μ = gm × rd

Why μ = gmrd: μ asks how much VDS change balances a VGS change at constant ID. Write it as a chain of ratios: (ΔVDS/ΔID) × (ΔID/ΔVGS) = rd × gm. The minus sign records that a more negative VGS must be balanced by a larger VDS.

Write in your record

Result: IDSS = 11.85 mA, VP = −3 V, gm = 6.51 mS (VDS = 4 V), rd = 38.5 kΩ (VGS = 0); also gd = 26 µS and μ = gmrd ≈ 250.

Theory for the record: an n-channel JFET is an n-type channel between two p-type gate regions. With the gate–source junction reverse-biased, the depletion regions narrow the channel as VGS becomes more negative, so the gate voltage controls the drain current. Gate current is negligible, giving very high input resistance. As VDS increases, ID first rises linearly (ohmic region), then the channel pinches off and ID saturates. At VGS = VP the channel is cut off. In saturation, ID = IDSS(1 − VGS/VP)² (Shockley). Parameters: gm = ΔID/ΔVGS at constant VDS; rd = ΔVDS/ΔID at constant VGS; μ = gmrd.

Check 1

(a) Use Shockley to predict ID at VGS = −2 V, and compare with the table. (b) Why is a JFET's gate never forward-biased in normal use? (c) BJT vs JFET: give two differences an examiner would accept.

Answers

Opening question. gm from the transfer curve (or two drain curves at one VDS); rd and gd from one drain curve in saturation; μ from gm × rd.

Check 1. (a) 11.85 × (1 − 2/3)² = 11.85/9 = 1.32 mA; the table gives 1.31–1.32 ✓. (b) Forward bias would make the gate junction conduct. The gate would draw current, the high input resistance would be lost, and the device could be damaged. (c) Any two: a JFET is voltage-controlled, a BJT current-controlled; a JFET is unipolar, a BJT bipolar; a JFET has very high input resistance, a BJT moderate (hie ≈ kΩ); a JFET is less noisy and more thermally stable; a BJT has higher gain (gm).

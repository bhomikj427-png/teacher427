What you already have: from Exp 6, re = 26 mV/IE, and voltage-divider bias. From Exp 5, VE = VB − 0.7 V in the active region.

Two terms first:

  • Common collector (CC): the collector sits at the supply, which is AC ground. Input at the base, output at the emitter.
  • Buffer: a stage with gain ≈ 1, high input resistance and low output resistance. It copies a voltage without loading the source.
Manual Exp 7, aim

Verify the gain of an emitter follower circuit. Viva form: "The gain is less than 1. Why would anyone build an amplifier that doesn't amplify voltage?"

Guess first

Hint: think about current and about resistance seen from each side.

How it works

The emitter is always one diode-drop below the base: VE = VB − 0.7. So when the base moves up by 0.5 V, the emitter moves up by (almost) 0.5 V. The emitter follows the base. That is the whole idea.

Q-point (LTspice .op): VB = 1.36 V, VE = 0.75 V, IE = 0.75/2k = 0.376 mA.

Property Value here Why
voltage gain ≈ 0.97, no inversion the emitter follows the base; the output is in phase
input resistance ≈ 3 kΩ (bias resistors), ≈ 600 kΩ into the base alone the base sees RE multiplied by β: β(re + RE) = 290 × 2.07 kΩ
output resistance ≈ 70 Ω (≈ re) looking back into the emitter
current gain large (≈ β) the base current is tiny; the emitter current is β + 1 times bigger

So the answer to the viva question: it is a current amplifier and impedance converter. A weak, high-resistance source can drive a heavy, low-resistance load through it without its voltage sagging. That is why the extra design problem uses it as the output stage.

Trap: the manual's theory paragraph

It says the input impedance is "of the order of hundred ohms" and the output "of the order of ten ohms". The output part is right in spirit (tens of ohms). The input impedance is high: kilohms to hundreds of kilohms. High input, low output is the whole point.

Do it

Hands-on:

  1. Build Fig 7.1. Use the 15 V shown in the figure. The parts list says 10 V; see the trap below.
  2. Check the Q-point with no signal: VB ≈ 1.36 V, VE ≈ 0.75 V.
  3. Generator: sine, 0.5 V peak, 1 kHz, into C2. DSO CH1 on the input, CH2 on Vout. The two traces should overlap, in phase.
  4. Step the frequency through 200 Hz, 500, 1k, 10k, 100k, 1 MHz and record Vo. Gain = Vo/Vi.

LTspice (manual's procedure):

  1. V2 with SINE(0 0.5 1k) and AC amplitude 0.5. Q1 = BC547B.
  2. Run .tran 10m and compare V(out) with the input.
  3. Then run .ac dec 10 200 1Meg. Comment out the line you are not using (right-click the directive and choose Comment), or the ; prefix in the manual's figure does the same thing.

Trap: a 10 V supply clips the output

With 10 V, VE drops to about 0.35 V. A 0.5 V-peak input then pulls the emitter below 0 V and the transistor cuts off: LTspice shows the negative half flattened at −0.32 V. With 10 V, either use 0.2 V peak (clean) or the figure's 15 V.

Write in your record

Result: maximum gain of the emitter follower ≈ 0.97 (−0.3 dB), output in phase with input; flat from 200 Hz to 1 MHz, so the bandwidth exceeds the measured range (simulation: fL ≈ 5 Hz).

Theory for the record: in the common-collector (emitter follower) circuit, the input is applied to the base and the output taken from the emitter, while the collector is at AC ground. Because VE = VB − VBE, the emitter follows the base: voltage gain is slightly below 1 (RE/(RE + re)) with no phase inversion. The input resistance is high (≈ βRE) and the output resistance low (≈ re), so the stage is used as a buffer for impedance matching from a high-impedance source to a low-impedance load. It has a high current gain and power gain, and a wide bandwidth.

Check 1

(a) Why is Av always a little less than 1, never exactly 1? (b) Change RE to 1 kΩ (same bias). Estimate the new IE, re and Av. (c) Which stage, CE or CC, would you put directly in front of an 8 Ω speaker, and why?

Answers

Opening question. It amplifies current (≈ β) and therefore power. It also converts a high source resistance into a low output resistance (a buffer), so a weak source can drive a heavy load.

Check 1. (a) The output is taken across RE, which is in series with re: Av = RE/(RE + re). re is never zero. (b) IE = 0.75/1k = 0.75 mA, re = 26/0.75 = 35 Ω, Av = 1000/1035 = 0.966: still about the same. (c) CC (emitter follower). Its output resistance is tiny, so the 8 Ω load does not collapse the voltage. A CE stage's output resistance (≈ RC) would drop most of the signal internally.

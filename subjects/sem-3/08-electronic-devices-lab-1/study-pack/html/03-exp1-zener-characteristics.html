What you already have: Ohm's law, and the meters from Exp 0.

Three terms first:

  • Diode: a p-type and an n-type piece of silicon joined together (a pn junction). The p side is the anode and the n side is the cathode (the bar on the symbol). Current flows easily from anode to cathode.
  • Forward / reverse bias: anode more positive than cathode = forward. The reverse = reverse.
  • Breakdown: under enough reverse voltage, a junction suddenly conducts heavily.
Manual Exp 1, aim

To plot the characteristics of a zener diode and find its dynamic resistance under reverse-biased condition. Viva form: "Why does a zener's reverse current rise sharply at one voltage, and what is its dynamic resistance?"

Guess first

Sketch I against V for an ordinary diode, both directions, before reading on. Where does it conduct?

The idea: a diode that is meant to break down

  1. Forward: almost no current until about 0.6–0.7 V (silicon), then current rises steeply. This is the same as any diode.
  2. Reverse, below VZ: only a tiny leakage current (µA). The diode is close to an open circuit.
  3. Reverse, at VZ: the junction breaks down. The current can rise from 1 mA to 10 mA while the voltage barely moves from VZ.

Region 3 is what makes a zener useful: a voltage that stays put while the current changes, which is a voltage reference. Exp 2 uses exactly that.

Why breakdown happens (viva staple; from ECE2101 unit 2):

Zener breakdown Avalanche breakdown
doping heavy, so the junction is thin light, so the junction is wide
mechanism the field pulls electrons straight across (tunnelling) fast carriers knock out new ones (chain reaction)
VZ below about 5 V above about 6 V
temperature coefficient negative positive

A 6.2 V zener is near the crossover and uses both mechanisms. That is why ~5–6 V zeners drift least with temperature. "Zener diode" is the trade name for any breakdown diode, whichever mechanism it uses.

Dynamic resistance rz: in breakdown, how much the voltage moves per unit change of current:

rz = ΔVZΔIZ

A small rz (tens of ohms or less) means a better voltage reference. It is called dynamic because it is the slope of the curve, not V/I at one point. VZ/IZ would be a static resistance, and a meaningless one here.

Limits:

  • IZ(min): the least current that keeps the zener in breakdown.
  • IZ(max) = PZ/VZ: the largest current before it overheats. A 0.5 W, 6.2 V zener allows IZ(max) = 0.5/6.2 = 81 mA.

The series 1 kΩ resistor in the circuit limits the current.

The circuit

Trap: the manual's parts list

It lists a 1N4007. That is an ordinary rectifier with a breakdown near 1000 V, so it will never break down on a 0–30 V supply. Ask for a zener (e.g. 6.2 V). A 1N4007 placed reverse-biased just shows µA leakage at every VS.

Do it (hands-on, breadboard)
  1. Supply off, voltage knob at 0, current limit low.
  2. Wire the 1 kΩ from supply + to the ammeter. Ammeter (DMM on mA DC, red lead in the mA port) to the zener's cathode (the banded end). Zener's anode to supply −.
  3. Put the voltmeter (DMM on DC V, or the DSO) across the zener.
  4. Switch on. Raise VS in 1 V steps from 0 to 15 V, reading VZ and IZ at each step.
  5. Plot IZ (y) against VZ (x). Mark the knee: that is VZ.
  6. Take two points well into breakdown, read ΔVZ and ΔIZ, and calculate rz.

What you should see

Simulated with a 6.2 V zener (BZX84C6V2L, the part Exp 2 uses). A different zener shifts the knee to its own VZ, but the shape is identical.

VZ (V) = VS 5.957 6.185 6.245 6.279 6.303 6.322 6.337 6.350 6.362 6.372
IZ (mA) ≈ 0 (µA) 0.04 0.82 1.76 2.72 3.70 4.68 5.66 6.65 7.64 8.63

Below breakdown, the zener takes almost no current, so no voltage drops across the 1 kΩ and VZ = VS. In breakdown, VZ is pinned and the rest of VS drops across the resistor: IZ = (VS − VZ)/1 kΩ. Check: VS = 15 gives (15 − 6.372)/1000 = 8.63 mA ✓.

The zoom figure uses 2 → 8 mA and gives 0.111 V / 6 mA = 18 Ω. rz shrinks as the current grows, so always state the current range used.

Write in your record

Result: breakdown voltage VZ ≈ 6.2 V (at IZ ≈ 1 mA); dynamic resistance rz ≈ 14 Ω (between 3.7 and 8.6 mA). Use your own readings in the lab.

Theory for the record: a zener is a heavily doped pn diode designed to operate in reverse breakdown. Forward, it behaves like a normal diode (about 0.7 V). Reverse, only leakage flows until VZ, where the current rises sharply while the voltage stays nearly constant. Breakdown is by tunnelling (below ~5 V) or avalanche (above ~6 V). Operation must stay between IZ(min) and IZ(max) = PZ/VZ. The slope ΔVZ/ΔIZ in breakdown is the dynamic resistance; a small value means good regulation, which Exp 2 uses.

Trap: no series resistor

Without the 1 kΩ (or with it shorted), the current in breakdown is limited only by the supply, and the zener burns out. Never connect a zener directly across a supply.

Check 1

(a) At VS = 12 V, a student reads VZ = 6.34 V. Predict IZ without the ammeter. (b) Why is rz not simply VZ/IZ? (c) A 3.3 V zener and a 12 V zener: which mechanism does each use, and which way does each one's VZ move when heated?

Answers

Opening question. Reverse current rises sharply at VZ because the junction breaks down (tunnelling or avalanche), producing many carriers. Dynamic resistance = ΔVZ/ΔIZ, the slope in breakdown. It is small (≈ 14–18 Ω here).

Check 1. (a) IZ = (12 − 6.34)/1 kΩ = 5.66 mA. (b) rz describes how V changes when I changes (the slope), which is what matters for regulation. VZ/IZ at one point is a large, current-dependent number with no use here. (c) 3.3 V: Zener (tunnelling) mechanism, VZ falls with heat (negative coefficient). 12 V: avalanche, VZ rises with heat (positive coefficient).

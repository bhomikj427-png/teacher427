Exp 6 · CE amplifier: frequency response and bandwidth

Manual pp. 20–21 · hands-on · CO4, CO5 · the same stage is reused in the extra experiment
5 BJT curves→6 CE amplifier→7 Emitter follower→8 Feedback

What you already have: from Exp 5, in the active region IC = βIB, and the BE junction needs ≈ 0.7 V. From C&S, a capacitor's reactance XC = 1/(2πfC): huge at low f, tiny at high f.

Three terms first:

  • Biasing: DC voltages that park the transistor in the active region at a chosen Q-point (quiescent point = the DC operating point with no signal).
  • Small-signal gain Av: vo/vin for a small sine riding on the Q-point. In decibels: 20 log₁₀ Av.
  • Bandwidth: the frequency range over which the gain stays within 3 dB (a factor of 0.707) of its maximum.
Manual Exp 6, aim

To plot the frequency response of a CE amplifier and determine its bandwidth. Viva form: "Why does the gain fall at both low and high frequencies, and what sets each cut-off?"

Guess first

Name one part responsible for each end before reading on.

The circuit, part by part

25 kΩ + 8.2 kΩ (R1, R2) voltage divider: fixes VB ≈ 15 × 8.2/33.2 ≈ 3.6–3.7 V

Q-point (LTspice .op): VB = 3.60 V, VE = 2.92 V, IC = 5.2 mA, VC = 12.09 V, so VCE = 9.2 V. The transistor is safely active.

Gain by hand:

  • re = 26 mV / IE = 26/5.2 = 5 Ω. This is the BE junction's small-signal resistance.
  • Base to collector: Av = −RC/re = −560/5 = −112.
  • The 2.2 kΩ source resistor and the amplifier's input resistance (Rin = 25k ∥ 8.2k ∥ βre ≈ 1.2 kΩ) form a divider. Only 1.2/(1.2 + 2.2) = 0.35 of vs reaches the base.
  • Overall: vo/vs ≈ 0.35 × 112 ≈ 39. LTspice gives 36.
  • The minus sign means the output is inverted (180° phase shift).

Why the gain falls at both ends

  • Low f: XC = 1/(2πfC) grows. The coupling capacitors start to block the signal, and above all CE stops bypassing RE, which brings in negative feedback (see Exp 8). The lower cut-off is set mostly by CE: fL ≈ 1/(2π CE Re'), where Re' ≈ re + (source ∥ bias)/β ≈ 10.6 Ω. That gives 1/(2π × 47 µF × 10.6 Ω) ≈ 320 Hz.
  • High f: tiny capacitances inside the transistor (between its junctions) and in the wiring start to short the signal. The base–collector capacitance is multiplied by the gain (the Miller effect), so it dominates.
  • Middle (midband): all external capacitors act as shorts and the internal ones as opens, so the gain is flat.
Do it (hands-on)
  1. Build Fig 6.1 on the breadboard with supply +15 V. 25 kΩ is not a standard value: use 22 kΩ + 3.3 kΩ in series. Check the electrolytic capacitors' polarity: + towards the higher DC voltage (base side for Cin, collector side for CC, emitter side for CE).
  2. Check the Q-point first with the DMM, no signal applied: VB ≈ 3.6 V, VE ≈ 2.9 V, VC ≈ 12 V. If VC ≈ 15 V the transistor is off; if VC ≈ VE it is saturated. Fix this before going on.
  3. Generator: sine, 1 kHz. Set the amplitude small: about 20 mV peak (use the generator's −20 dB/attenuator button, or a 1 kΩ:100 Ω divider). Look at Vo on the DSO. It must be a clean sine, not flattened.
  4. Keep Vs constant. Step the frequency through 100 Hz, 500, 800, 1k, 5k, 10k, 50k, 100k, 500k, 800k, 1 MHz and read Vo(pp) at each.
  5. Calculate Av = Vo/Vs and 20 log Av. Plot dB (y) against frequency on semi-log paper (log x-axis).
  6. Draw a line 3 dB below the maximum. Where it cuts the curve gives fL and fH. BW = fH − fL.
Trap: the manual's 500 mV input

At 500 mV the output would need to swing about ±18 V, but the collector can only move between ≈ 3 V and 15 V. The output is clipped flat (below), so the "gain" you read is meaningless. Even the 100 mV in Fig 6.1 distorts (+2.5 / −3.4 V). Use about 20 mV.

On the bench, fL should land close to 300 Hz, because it is set by the capacitors you placed. fH depends on breadboard wiring, probe and generator capacitance, so expect anything from a few hundred kHz upward. The simulation is the ideal-wiring value.

Calculation

BW = fH − fL = 687 kHz − 0.306 kHz ≈ 687 kHz

When fH ≫ fL, the bandwidth is essentially fH.

Write in your record

Result: maximum gain ≈ 36 (31.1 dB); fL ≈ 306 Hz, fH ≈ 687 kHz; bandwidth ≈ 687 kHz (simulation; use your measured values).

Theory for the record: in a CE amplifier the input is applied at the base and the output taken at the collector, with the emitter common (grounded for AC by the bypass capacitor). Voltage-divider bias with an emitter resistor sets a stable Q-point in the active region. A small base signal produces a β-times-larger collector current change, which develops a large, phase-inverted voltage across RC. Gain ≈ RC/re. The frequency response is flat in midband, falls at low frequencies because of the coupling and bypass capacitors, and falls at high frequencies because of junction and stray capacitances. Bandwidth = fH − fL, measured at the −3 dB (0.707 Amax) points, also called the half-power points.

Check 1

(a) Why is the −3 dB point called the "half-power" point? (b) You remove CE. Does the gain go up or down, and roughly to what? (c) VC reads 15 V with no signal. What is wrong?

Answers

Opening question. Low end: the coupling and bypass capacitors' reactance becomes large; CE matters most (fL ≈ 1/(2πCERe')). High end: the transistor's internal junction capacitances (Miller-multiplied Cbc) plus stray capacitance.

Check 1. (a) Power ∝ V². At 0.707 of the voltage, the power is 0.707² = 0.5 of maximum, and 20 log 0.707 = −3 dB. (b) Down. RE returns to the AC path, so Av ≈ −RC/(re + RE) = −560/565 ≈ −1. The overall gain is about 1 or less (this is Exp 8's effect). (c) No collector current: the transistor is cut off. Look for an open base connection, a missing emitter return, a wrong pin order (check the BC547's pinout: C-B-E with the flat face towards you) or a dead transistor.

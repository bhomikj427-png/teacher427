Build

1 · Carriers already move, with no current

Predict

At room temperature a free electron in silicon moves at about 10 million cm/s. A silicon bar sits on the desk with nothing connected. Is a current flowing through it? Why or why not?

Terms

Thermal velocity vth: the typical speed a carrier has just from heat. Scattering: a carrier hitting something that changes its direction: a vibrating atom or a charged dopant ion. Mean free time τ: the average time between two scatterings.

  • Heat gives each carrier kinetic energy: ½m vth2 = (3/2)kT. With the free-electron mass, vth = √(3kT/m) ≈ 1.2 × 107 cm/s at 300 K.
  • It does not fly far: it scatters every 0.01–1 ps (deck slide 47). At 107 cm/s for 0.2 ps it covers only about 20 nm before turning.
  • Every direction after a scattering is equally likely, so after many scatterings the average displacement is zero (deck slide 46).

Answer to the Predict. No current. As many electrons cross any plane going left as going right, so the charge carried each way cancels.

Check 1

(a) Name the two things a carrier scatters off. (b) Why does fast motion not mean current? (c) The scattering time doubles. How far does the electron fly between collisions at 107 cm/s, if τ was 0.2 ps?

2 · A field adds a drift: vd = μE

A battery puts a field across the bar. The random motion stays; a small steady shift is added on top.

practice

1 V is applied across a 100 μm long piece of lightly doped Si. The field is 1 V / 0.01 cm = 100 V/cm. With μn = 1350 cm2/V·s, how fast do the electrons drift? Compare with vth ≈ 1.17 × 107 cm/s.

Guess first

Faster or slower than the thermal speed, and by roughly how much?

Terms

Electric field E: volts per cm; the push per unit charge. Drift velocity vd: the average velocity of the carriers along the field, after averaging out the random part. Mobility μ: drift velocity per unit field, in cm2/V·s; how easily a carrier is pushed.

  • Between scatterings the field accelerates the carrier; each scattering throws away the direction it gained. The result is a steady average velocity proportional to the field (deck slide 47):
vd = μE
  • Holes drift along E (charge +q). Electrons drift against E (charge −q).
  • What sets μ (deck slide 47): μ = qτ/m*. Longer time between scatterings (τ) → more speed gained → higher μ. Heavier effective mass m* → less speed gained → lower μ. Holes in Si are heavier: μp ≈ 480 against μn ≈ 1350.
  • Units: (cm/s)/(V/cm) = cm2/V·s.

Answer. vd = 1350 × 100 = 1.35 × 105 cm/s. That is about 87 times slower than vth: the drift is a slow creep on top of fast random motion.

Check 2

(a) Hole drift velocity in the same field, μp = 480? (b) Given m* = 0.26 m0 for Si electrons, find τ from μn = 1350 cm2/V·s (convert μ to m2/V·s first). (c) In which direction do electrons drift relative to E, and why?

3 · At high field the drift velocity saturates

Predict

vd = μE gave 1.35 × 105 cm/s at 100 V/cm. Raise the field 1000 times, to 105 V/cm. Does vd become 1.35 × 108 cm/s?

Terms

Saturation velocity vsat: the speed vd cannot exceed, about 107 cm/s in Si (deck slides 47–48). It is close to vth.

Reading the figure.

  • Low field (up to a few kV/cm): a straight line of slope 1 on the log–log plot. 10× the field gives 10× the drift (deck slide 47). This is where μ is a constant.
  • High field: the curve bends over and flattens at vsat ≈ 107 cm/s. Carriers now gain so much energy between scatterings that they lose it at once by shaking the lattice.
  • Where it bends: μE reaches vsat at E = vsat/μ = 107/1350 = 7.4 kV/cm for electrons. A simple model of the curve: vd = μE/(1 + μE/vsat).
  • GaAs electrons (deck slide 47) rise faster, overshoot to a peak, then fall back to saturation. Not needed for Si sums.

Answer to the Predict. No. 1350 × 105 = 1.35 × 108 cm/s would be 13× vsat. The model gives 1.35 × 108/(1 + 13.5) = 9.3 × 106 cm/s, just under vsat.

Check 3

(a) At what field does μpE reach 107 cm/s for holes, μp = 480? (b) A transistor channel is 100 nm long with 1 V across it. Find E. Is the electron drift linear or saturated? (c) In the saturated region, is "μ" still the slope vd/E?

4 · What sets the mobility: doping and temperature

Step 2 said μ = qτ/m*. The mass is fixed by the material, so mobility changes only through τ: how often the carrier scatters.

Predict

Add 5 × 1017 donor atoms per cm3 to silicon. Each one becomes a fixed + ion. Does the electron mobility go up, down, or stay?

Terms

Lattice scattering: collisions with atoms vibrating from heat. More heat → more vibration → more collisions. Impurity scattering: a carrier's path bent by a charged dopant ion. More dopants → more collisions.

  • Both scatterings act at once. Their rates add, so 1/μ = 1/μlattice + 1/μimpurity (Matthiessen's rule). The worse one dominates.
  • Doping: light doping (below about 1015) changes almost nothing; above about 1016 the ions take over and μ falls steeply.
Doping (cm−3) 1014 1016 1017 5 × 1017 1018
μn (cm2/V·s) 1420 1230 780 420 310
μp (cm2/V·s) 460 420 310 — 150
  • Temperature (deck slide 48): lattice scattering grows with heat, μlattice ∝ T−3/2, so μ falls as T rises in lightly doped Si. Impurity scattering weakens with heat (faster carriers are bent less), so heavily doped Si changes less.

Answer to the Predict. Down: 5 × 1017 ions cut μn from about 1430 to about 420.

Mobility values in questions

Questions give μ. Books quote 1350 or 1500 for "μn" in lightly doped Si; both are inside the spread. Use the value the question gives.

Check 4

(a) From the table, μn at ND = 1017? (b) Lightly doped Si is heated from 300 K to 400 K. By what factor does μ change (lattice scattering only)? (c) Why is μp below μn at every doping?

5 · Drift current density: counting carriers through a slice

The carriers drift at vd. Current is charge per second, so count how many cross a surface each second.

Deck slides 49–50

Show that the drift current density in a semiconductor is J = q(pμp + nμn)E.

Guess first

The holes inside a slice of width Δx all cross its right face within some time. What time?

Terms

Flux F: the number of carriers crossing one cm2 per second (deck slide 49). Current density J: the current through one cm2, in A/cm2; J = (charge of each carrier) × flux.

Answer (each line follows from the one before):

  1. Holes in the slice: pAΔx. Moving at vdp, all of them cross the right face within Δx/vdp seconds.
  2. Hole flux = (number) / (time) / (area) = pAΔx / (Δx/vdp) / A = p vdp.
  3. Electrons move the other way: electron flux = −n vdn (vdn is the electron speed).
  4. Current = charge × flux: J = (+q)(p vdp) + (−q)(−n vdn) = q(p vdp + n vdn).
  5. Low field: vdp = μpE, vdn = μnE. So
Jdrift = q(pμp + nμn)E = σE
  • Line 4 is the key: electrons move against E, but they carry negative charge, so their current points along E. The two currents add.
  • Units (deck slide 50): C × cm2/V·s × 1/cm3 × V/cm = A/cm2. ✓
  • J = σE is Ohm's law written per cm2: σ is the conductivity.
Check 5

(a) Electrons and holes move in opposite directions. Why do their currents not cancel? (b) n = 1016 cm−3, μn = 1350, E = 10 V/cm, holes negligible. Find J. (c) What are the units of σ?

6 · Conductivity and resistivity of pure silicon

Deck slide 51

(a) Calculate the conductivity and resistivity of intrinsic silicon at T = 300 K. (b) How would σ change if the temperature increased by 10 °C? Given: μn = 1500, μp = 450 cm2/V·s for undoped silicon.

Guess first

Is pure silicon closer to a copper wire (ρ ≈ 2 × 10−6 Ω·cm) or to glass (ρ ≈ 1012 Ω·cm)?

Terms

Conductivity σ = q(nμn + pμp): how much current density one V/cm drives; in (Ω·cm)−1. Set by how many carriers (n, p) times how mobile (μ). Resistivity ρ = 1/σ: in Ω·cm; the material's resistance, independent of the shape.

Answer. (a) Intrinsic: n = p = ni ≈ 1010 cm−3 (deck's value). σ = q ni(μn + μp) = 1.6 × 10−19 × 1010 × (1500 + 450) = 3.12 × 10−6 (Ω·cm)−1. ρ = 1/σ = 3.2 × 105 Ω·cm: about a third of a megohm for a 1 cm cube.

(b) ni roughly doubles for every 10 °C near room temperature (01a step 5; the table: 8.1 × 109 → 1.7 × 1010 from 25 to 35 °C). μ changes only a few per cent over 10 °C. So σ doubles.

  • So pure silicon sits between metal and glass, and its σ is set almost entirely by ni(T). The fall of μ with heat (step 4) is far smaller than the rise of ni.
Check 6

(a) Redo (a) with ni = 1.5 × 1010 cm−3. (b) From the table, by what factor does σ grow from 25 °C to 45 °C? (c) μ falls with temperature, yet σ of pure Si rises. Why?

7 · Doping sets σ: one term is enough

Deck slide 52

Compare (a) silicon with ND = 5 × 1017 cm−3, T = 300 K: σ = 32 (Ω·cm)−1; (b) intrinsic silicon, T = 300 K: σ = 3.12 × 10−6 (Ω·cm)−1.

Guess first

How many silicon atoms per donor is 5 × 1017 donors in 5 × 1022 atoms? And how many times does σ grow?

Answer. n ≈ ND = 5 × 1017; p = ni2/n = 1020/(5 × 1017) = 200 cm−3. The hole term pμp is 10−16 of the electron term: drop it: σ ≈ qNDμn.

  • Slide 52's σ = 32 means μn = 32/(1.6 × 10−19 × 5 × 1017) = 400 cm2/V·s, not 1500: the heavy doping lowered μ (step 4's table gives 420). With 1500 you would get 120.

  • One donor per 5 × 1022/5 × 1017 = 105 atoms raises σ by 32/3.12 × 10−6 = 107 times.

  • For doped material: n-type σ ≈ qNDμn; p-type σ ≈ qNAμp. Use μ at that doping.

Check 7

(a) p-type Si, NA = 1016, μp = 420. Find σ and ρ. (b) Why can the minority term be dropped? (c) n-type, ND = 1016, μn = 1230. Find ρ.

8 · Resistance of a bar: R = ρL/A

ρ belongs to the material. A real resistor also has a shape.

practice

An n-type Si bar has ρ = 0.5 Ω·cm, length 1 mm, and a 100 μm × 100 μm cross-section. Find R and the current at 5 V.

Guess first

Longer bar: more or less resistance? Fatter bar?

  • R = ρL/A. Longer → more resistance (more scattering on the way). Bigger A → less (more paths side by side).
  • Convert everything to cm: ρ is in Ω·cm.

Answer. L = 0.1 cm, A = 0.01 × 0.01 = 10−4 cm2. R = 0.5 × 0.1 / 10−4 = 500 Ω; I = 5/500 = 10 mA.

Same answer from step 5 (independent route): E = 5 V/0.1 cm = 50 V/cm; σ = 1/ρ = 2 (Ω·cm)−1; J = σE = 100 A/cm2; I = J × A = 100 × 10−4 = 10 mA. ✓

Check 8

(a) Double the length: new R? (b) Halve both W and t: new R? (c) J in the original bar at 5 V?

9 · Sheet resistance: counting squares

In an IC a resistor is a thin doped layer at the chip surface. The depth t is fixed by the process; the designer only draws the top view: L and W.

practice

A doped layer is 1 μm thick with ρ = 0.01 Ω·cm. Find its sheet resistance. A resistor in this layer is 200 μm long and 10 μm wide. Find R.

Guess first

A 10 μm × 10 μm square and a 100 μm × 100 μm square of the same layer: which has more resistance end to end?

Terms

Sheet resistance Rs = ρ/t: the resistance of one square of the layer, of any size; units Ω, said "ohms per square" (Ω/□). Number of squares L/W: how many such squares fit end to end along the resistor.

  • Rewrite step 8 with A = W × t:
R = ρLWt = ρt × LW = Rs × (number of squares)
  • Only the shape ratio L/W matters, so the designer just counts squares.

Answer. Rs = 0.01 / (1 × 10−4 cm) = 100 Ω/□. L/W = 200/10 = 20 squares. R = 20 × 100 = 2 kΩ.

Check 9

(a) The ρ = 0.5 Ω·cm material of step 8, as a 1 μm layer: Rs? (b) Rs = 200 Ω/□, W = 5 μm. How long must the resistor be for 10 kΩ? (c) Answer the Guess-first question.

Exam form

Drift (deck slides 46–48). Random thermal motion gives no net displacement. A field adds a drift velocity vd = μE: holes along E, electrons against. μ = qτ/m*, units cm2/V·s. At high field vd saturates at vsat ≈ 107 cm/s (Si). μ falls with doping (impurity scattering) and with temperature (lattice scattering, ∝ T−3/2).

Drift current (deck slides 49–50).

  1. Holes in a slice: pAΔx; crossing time Δx/vdp; hole flux Fp = pvdp; electron flux Fn = −nvdn.
  2. J = (+q)Fp + (−q)Fn = q(pvdp + nvdn).
  3. vd = μE → J = q(pμp + nμn)E = σE.

Formulas. σ = q(nμn + pμp); ρ = 1/σ; intrinsic σ = qni(μn + μp); n-type σ ≈ qNDμn; p-type σ ≈ qNAμp. R = ρL/A. Rs = ρ/t (Ω/□); R = Rs × L/W.

Temperature. Intrinsic σ roughly doubles per 10 °C (ni doubles; μ barely changes).

Traps

  • Dropping one carrier in intrinsic σ: both niμn and niμp count.
  • Thinking electron and hole drift currents cancel: they add.
  • Using μ = 1500 for heavily doped Si: μ falls with doping (slide 52 needs 400).
  • Mixing units: ρ in Ω·cm needs L and A in cm (1 μm = 10−4 cm).
  • Writing Rs in Ω·cm: it is in Ω (per square).
  • Using vd = μE at fields above a few kV/cm: it saturates.

  2. Hole drift velocity at 500 V/cm, μp = 480?
  3. Rs = 50 Ω/□; a resistor 300 μm long, 6 μm wide. Find R.
  4. A bar with ρ = 1.5 Ω·cm, 2 mm long, cross-section 0.5 mm × 0.5 mm. Find R.
  5. Derive J = q(pμp + nμn)E from the slice picture.
  6. A 2 μm layer with ρ = 0.02 Ω·cm: Rs?
  7. Explain in two sentences why μ falls with doping and why it falls with temperature.
  8. Why does the conductivity of pure Si rise steeply with temperature?

Answers

Check 1. (a) Vibrating lattice atoms and charged dopant ions. (b) The directions are random, so the average displacement is zero: equal numbers cross each way. (c) τ = 0.4 ps: 107 × 0.4 × 10−12 = 4 × 10−6 cm = 40 nm.

Check 2. (a) 480 × 100 = 4.8 × 104 cm/s. (b) μ = 0.135 m2/V·s; τ = μm*/q = 0.135 × 0.26 × 9.11 × 10−31 / 1.6 × 10−19 = 2.0 × 10−13 s = 0.2 ps. (c) Against E: the force on charge −q is −qE.

Check 3. (a) 107/480 = 2.1 × 104 V/cm. (b) E = 1 V / 10−5 cm = 105 V/cm, far above 7.4 kV/cm: saturated. (c) No: vd no longer grows with E, so μ is no longer a constant.

Check 4. (a) 780 cm2/V·s. (b) (400/300)−3/2 = 0.65: μ falls to 65 %. (c) Holes have a heavier effective mass, and μ = qτ/m*.

Check 5. (a) Electrons carry negative charge moving the opposite way: negative × opposite = same direction of current. (b) J = 1.6 × 10−19 × 1016 × 1350 × 10 = 21.6 A/cm2. (c) (Ω·cm)−1 (also written S/cm).

Check 6. (a) σ = 1.6 × 10−19 × 1.5 × 1010 × 1950 = 4.68 × 10−6 (Ω·cm)−1; ρ = 2.14 × 105 Ω·cm. (b) ni: 8.1 × 109 → 3.5 × 1010: 4.3 ×. (c) ni grows exponentially with T (doubling per 10 °C); μ falls only slowly (T−3/2). The carrier count wins.

Check 7. (a) σ = 1.6 × 10−19 × 1016 × 420 = 0.672 (Ω·cm)−1; ρ = 1.49 Ω·cm. (b) The minority concentration is ni2/N, many orders of magnitude below the majority, so its term is negligible. (c) σ = 1.6 × 10−19 × 1016 × 1230 = 1.97; ρ = 0.508 Ω·cm.

Check 8. (a) 1000 Ω. (b) A falls to ¼: 2000 Ω. (c) J = σE = 2 × 50 = 100 A/cm2.

Check 9. (a) 0.5 / 10−4 = 5000 Ω/□. (b) 10 000/200 = 50 squares; L = 50 × 5 = 250 μm. (c) Equal: both are one square, R = Rs.

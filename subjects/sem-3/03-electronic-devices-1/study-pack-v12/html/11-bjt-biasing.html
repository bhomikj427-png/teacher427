Build

1 · DC load line, Q-point and fixed bias

Deck L26 slide 15

For the fixed-bias circuit (VCC = 12 V, RB = 240 kΩ, RC = 2.2 kΩ, β = 50), find IB, IC and VCE at the operating point, and the saturation current.

Guess first

Which loop gives IB directly?

Terms

Operating (Q) point: the DC values (VCE, IC) with no signal applied. DC analysis: every capacitor is an open circuit.

  1. Base–emitter loop: VCC = IBRB + VBE, so IB = (VCC − 0.7)/RB.
  2. Active region: IC = βIB.
  3. Collector–emitter loop: VCE = VCC − ICRC.

Step 3 is a straight line on the output curves, the load line. Ends: IC = 0 gives VCE = VCC (cutoff); VCE = 0 gives IC(sat) = VCC/RC. For the largest undistorted swing, put Q near the middle. A larger IB slides Q up toward saturation; a larger RC flattens the line; a lower VCC shifts it inward, parallel.

Answer. IB = 11.3/240 k = 47.08 μA. IC = 50 × 47.08 μA = 2.35 mA. VCE = 12 − 2.35 × 2.2 = 6.82 V. IC(sat) = 12/2.2 k = 5.45 mA. Q sits near the middle of the line.

Check 1

Same circuit: (a) the transistor is replaced by one with β = 100. Find the new IC and VCE. What has happened? (b) Give the end points of the load line if RC becomes 4 kΩ.

2 · Why the Q-point drifts

Predict

Check 1(a) showed a new transistor can wreck the Q-point. Name three things that change IC when the transistor only warms up.

  • ICO (= ICBO, the CB leakage) doubles for every 10 °C rise.
  • VBE falls about 2.5 mV per °C.
  • β rises with temperature, and varies widely between transistors of one type.

All three raise IC. More IC means more heat, which raises IC again: thermal runaway, which can destroy the transistor.

The stability factor measures the leakage effect, with β and VBE held constant. Smaller is better; the ideal is 1. Two more factors are defined the same way: S′ = ∂IC/∂VBE, S″ = ∂IC/∂β.

S = ∂IC/∂ICO

Derivation (general S). Start from IC = βIB + (1 + β)ICO. Differentiate with respect to IC: 1 = β(dIB/dIC) + (1 + β)/S. Rearrange:

S = (1 + β)/(1 − β·dIB/dIC)

Answer. ICO, VBE and β; all three move IC up as temperature rises.

Check 2

(a) Why is S = 1 + β for fixed bias? (b) What value of dIB/dIC would give the ideal S = 1? What sign must it have?

3 · Emitter bias and its stability factor

Deck L27 slide 27

For the emitter-stabilised circuit (VCC = 20 V, RB = 430 kΩ, RC = 2 kΩ, RE = 1 kΩ, β = 50), find IB, IC and VCE. Then find S and compare with fixed bias.

Guess first

What could a resistor in the emitter do about a rise in IC?

How RE stabilises. If IC rises, the drop across RE rises, which raises the emitter voltage and leaves less to drive the base. IB falls and pulls IC back: negative feedback (13).

Base loop: VCC = IBRB + 0.7 + (β + 1)IBRE, so

IB = (VCC − VBE)/(RB + (β + 1)RE)

With RE present: VCE = VCC − ICRC − IERE ≈ VCC − IC(RC + RE), and IC(sat) = VCC/(RC + RE).

Circuit dIB/dIC S
Fixed bias 0 1 + β (worst)
Emitter bias −RE/(RB + RE) (1 + β)(1 + RB/RE)/(1 + β + RB/RE)
Voltage divider −RE/(RTh + RE) same, with RB → RTh (best: RTh is small)
Collector-to-base feedback −RC/(RB + RC) (1 + β)/(1 + βRC/(RB + RC))

Answer. IB = 19.3/(430 k + 51 k) = 40.1 μA. IC = 50 × 40.1 = 2.01 mA; IE = 2.05 mA. VCE = 20 − 2.01 × 2 − 2.05 × 1 = 13.94 V (13.97 V with IE ≈ IC). RB/RE = 430, so S = 51 × 431/(51 + 430) = 45.7, against 51 for fixed bias: RE helps only a little, because RB is far larger than RE.

4 · Voltage-divider bias

practice · circuit (c)

VCC = 22 V, R1 = 39 kΩ, R2 = 3.9 kΩ, RC = 10 kΩ, RE = 1.5 kΩ, β = 100. Find IC, VCE and S, exactly and approximately.

Guess first

If the divider current is much bigger than IB, does β matter at all?

Exact. Replace R1, R2 and VCC by their Thévenin equivalent seen from the base: ETh = VCCR2/(R1 + R2), RTh = R1 ∥ R2. Then IB = (ETh − 0.7)/(RTh + (β + 1)RE).

Approximate (valid when βRE ≥ 10R2): ignore IB. VB = ETh, VE = VB − 0.7, IC ≈ IE = VE/RE. β does not appear: the circuit is β-independent.

Answer. ETh = 22 × 3.9/42.9 = 2.0 V, RTh = 3.55 kΩ. Exact: IB = 1.3/(3.55 k + 151.5 k) = 8.38 μA, IC = 0.84 mA, VCE = 22 − 0.838 × 10 − 0.846 × 1.5 = 12.35 V, S = 101 × 3.36/103.36 = 3.29. Approximate: 150 k ≥ 39 k ✓; VE = 1.3 V, IC ≈ 1.3/1.5 k = 0.87 mA.

Check 4

(a) Why is S so much smaller here than for emitter bias? (b) With β = 200 instead, which answer, exact or approximate, changes?

5 · Bias compensation

Predict

Feedback through RE costs gain (13). Is there a way to cancel drift without feedback?

Compensation uses a temperature-sensitive part to cancel the drift directly.

  • Diode compensation: a forward-biased diode of the same material in the base circuit. Its drop falls with temperature exactly as VBE does, so the two cancel.
  • Thermistor (resistance falls as temperature rises) in the lower divider arm: as it heats, the base voltage drops and holds IC down.
  • Sensistor (resistance rises with temperature) in the upper arm or in the emitter does the same.

Answer. Yes: a part whose value drifts the opposite way to the transistor cancels the change without feedback.

Check 5

Where would you put a thermistor in a divider-bias circuit, and why there?

Exam form

Fixed bias. IB = (VCC − 0.7)/RB; IC = βIB; VCE = VCC − ICRC; load line ends (VCC, 0), (0, VCC/RC).

Stability. Causes: ICO ×2 per 10 °C, VBE −2.5 mV/°C, β rises; thermal runaway. S = ∂IC/∂ICO; derive S = (1 + β)/(1 − β dIB/dIC) from IC = βIB + (1 + β)ICO. Table of S for the four circuits.

Emitter bias. IB = (VCC − VBE)/(RB + (β + 1)RE); explain the feedback in one line.

Divider bias. Thévenin: ETh, RTh; exact IB; approximate when βRE ≥ 10R2: VE = VB − 0.7, IC = VE/RE.

Compensation. Diode, thermistor, sensistor: where and why.

Traps

  • Using VCE = VCC − ICRC when RE is present: subtract the RE drop too.
  • Forgetting (β + 1) in front of RE in the base loop.
  • Using the approximate divider method without checking βRE ≥ 10R2.
  • Calling a smaller S "worse": smaller is better, 1 is ideal.
  • Writing IC(sat) = VCC/RC when RE is present: it is VCC/(RC + RE).

Answers

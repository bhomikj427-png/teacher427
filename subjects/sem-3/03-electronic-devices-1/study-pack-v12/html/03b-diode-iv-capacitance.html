Build

1 · What the I–V curve looks like

Predict

A diode's voltage is swept from −5 V to +0.75 V and its current is plotted. Sketch the curve you expect from 03a step 12 (forward: small barrier; reverse: big barrier).

Terms

I–V characteristic: the graph of current against voltage for a device. Knee (cut-in) voltage Vγ: the forward voltage where the current becomes noticeable: about 0.7 V for Si, 0.3 V for Ge, 1.2 V for GaAs.

  • Reverse (VD < 0): the current is so small that on a mA scale it looks like zero. The log plot shows it: a constant 1 pA (deck slide 75: "the diode blocks conduction").
  • Forward: almost nothing until about 0.5 V, then a near-vertical rise. Above about 0.8 V a small Si diode overheats (deck slide 75).
  • The log plot shows the forward side is a straight line: the current is an exponential of the voltage. Steps 2–3 explain why.

Answer to the Predict. Flat near zero on the reverse side, flat near zero up to about 0.5 V forward, then a steep wall. That one-way shape is rectification.

Check 1

(a) What is the knee voltage of a Si diode? Of a Ge diode? (b) On which plot can you see the reverse current, and why? (c) What shape is the forward curve on a log scale, and what does that shape mean?

2 · Why the current grows exponentially

Combine two things: 03a's barrier height, and 01b's Boltzmann count (the number of electrons above an energy falls by e for each kT higher).

Predict

Forward bias lowers the barrier by 0.06 V. That is a little over 2 kT. By roughly what factor does the number of electrons able to climb it change: 1.1, 2 or 10?

Terms

Injection: majority carriers climbing the barrier and arriving on the other side as minority carriers. Barrier height: the energy step q(Vbi − Va) of 03a step 12.

  • Electrons on the n side have a spread of energies. The share above the barrier is e−(barrier)/kT (Boltzmann, 01b step 6).
  • The barrier is q(Vbi − Va), so the share is e−(Vbi − Va)/VT = e−Vbi/VT × eVa/VT. Every 25.9 mV of forward bias multiplies the crossing by e = 2.72.
  • Two currents cross the junction:
  • Diffusion (majority carriers climbing the barrier): ∝ eVa/VT. Changes hugely with Va.
  • Drift (minority carriers sliding down it, step 5): a fixed small current, call it Is. Does not depend on Va.
  • At Va = 0 they cancel (03a step 6), so the diffusion current at Va = 0 is also Is. With bias it is IseVa/VT. Net:
ID = IseVD/VT − Is

Answer to the Predict. e0.06/0.0259 = e2.32 ≈ 10. Lowering a barrier by a small voltage changes the crossing by a big factor.

Check 2

(a) Forward bias of 0.1 V: by what factor does the diffusion current grow over its zero-bias value? (b) Why does the drift current not change with Va? (c) At Va = 0, what are the two currents and their sum?

3 · The diode equation, term by term

practice · deck slides 79–83

A diode has Is = 1 pA and η = 1 at 300 K. Find its current at VD = 0.6 V. What happens if VD rises by 60 mV?

Guess first

Microamps, milliamps or amps at 0.6 V?

Terms

Reverse saturation current Is: the drift current of step 2; a property of the diode (step 5). nA or less for Si, μA for Ge (deck slide 79: "Is is the reverse saturation current"). Ideality factor η: a number from 1 to 2 that stretches the exponential; 1 when all current is injection (step 2), up to 2 when much of it comes from recombination inside the depletion region. Deck slide 79: "assume η = 1 unless otherwise noted".

Step 2's result, with the ideality factor (deck slides 79, 82):

ID = Is(eVD/ηVT − 1)   VT = kTq = T11 600

Is reverse saturation current A 10−12 to 10−9 (Si)
VD anode − cathode voltage V 0.6–0.7 forward
η ideality factor none 1 (2 if told)

Answer. VD/VT = 0.6/0.0259 = 23.17; e23.17 = 1.15 × 1010. ID = 10−12 × 1.15 × 1010 = 11.5 mA (the −1 is negligible). Another 60 mV multiplies it by e0.06/0.0259 = 10.1: 0.66 V gives 116 mA.

With η = 2 the same diode carries only 0.107 μA at 0.6 V: η matters, so state the value you use.

The deck's two rules for the ideality factor

Slide 79 says take η = 1 unless told. Slide 82 says "η = 1 for Ge and 2 for Si". Questions give η; when one does not, use η = 1 and write that down.

4 · The forward and reverse limits

Predict

At VD = −0.1 V, is the "−1" in the bracket important or negligible? At +0.6 V?

Terms

Decade: a factor of 10 in current. Saturation (reverse): the current no longer changes with voltage.

  • Forward, VD ≫ VT (a few × 26 mV): eVD/VT is huge, the −1 is lost (deck slide 83):
ID ≈ IseVD/ηVT
  • Each ×10 in current needs ηVTln10 = 59.6 mV (η = 1) or 119 mV (η = 2). From 1 mA to 100 mA the voltage changes only 0.12 V; that is why one fixed drop (0.7 V) is a fair model (step 9).
  • Reverse, VD ≲ −0.1 V: eVD/VT → 0, so (deck slide 83)
ID ≈ −Is
  • In reverse the current is flat: more reverse voltage does not add current until breakdown (05).

Answer to the Predict. At −0.1 V: e−3.86 = 0.021, so I = −0.979 Is: the −1 is the whole current. At +0.6 V: e23.2 = 1.15 × 1010: the −1 is negligible.

Check 4

(a) With Is = 1 pA, what VD gives 1 mA? 10 mA? (b) A diode at −10 V carries −2 nA. What current at −20 V (no breakdown)? (c) How many mV per decade if η = 2?

5 · Where Is comes from

Predict

Is is built from minority carriers (step 2). Ge has ni = 2.4 × 1013 cm−3, Si 1010. Which diode leaks more in reverse?

Terms

Thermal generation: heat breaking bonds to make electron–hole pairs (02b step 7). It is the only source of minority carriers in the dark.

  • The minority counts are ni2/NA (p side) and ni2/ND (n side) (03a step 4). Each one that diffuses to the depletion edge is swept across.
  • So Is ∝ ni2: set by the material and the temperature, not by VD. The full formula (stage-2 KB) is Is = qAni2[Dp/(LpND) + Dn/(LnNA)]; the exam needs only the proportionality.
  • Si vs Ge: (2.4 × 1013/1010)2 = 5.8 × 106: a Ge diode's ideal Is is millions of times larger. Real Ge diodes leak μA, Si nA or less (deck slide 79).

Answer to the Predict. Ge, because its ni is far larger and Is ∝ ni2.

Check 5

(a) Why does Is not grow with reverse voltage? (b) Which doping term dominates Is in a p⁺n diode, and why? (c) Why does Is grow fast with temperature?

6 · Temperature

practice · deck slides 80–81

A Si diode has Is = 10 nA at 25 °C. Find Is at 55 °C. Its forward voltage at 25 °C is 0.70 V at 10 mA; estimate it at 75 °C at the same current.

Guess first

Heat makes more carriers. Does the forward voltage at fixed current go up or down?

  • Is doubles every 10 °C (deck slide 81):
Is2 = Is1 × 2(T2 − T1)/10
  • Forward voltage at fixed current falls about 2 mV per °C (Si). Heat lowers the cut-in voltage (deck slides 80–81).
  • Ge diodes are more temperature-sensitive than Si or GaAs (deck slide 80).
  • Both rules come from ni(T) rising steeply (01a step 5) and Is ∝ ni2 (step 5).

Answer. 55 − 25 = 30 °C = three doublings: Is = 10 × 23 = 80 nA. Forward: 0.70 − 0.002 × 50 = 0.60 V.

(a) Is = 1 pA at 25 °C: Is at 125 °C? (b) A diode reads 0.65 V at 25 °C. Estimate at −15 °C, same current. (c) Why does a hot diode conduct at a lower voltage?

7 · Static (DC) resistance

A diode is not a resistor: V/I changes along its curve. Two "resistances" are defined, one for DC and one for small changes (deck slide 84).

practice · deck slide 84

A diode carries 2 mA at 0.7 V. Find its static resistance. Find it also at VD = 0.6 V (Is = 1 pA, so ID = 11.5 mA) and at −5 V (ID = −1 pA).

Guess first

Is RD larger near the knee or high up the curve? In reverse?

Terms

Operating point (Q-point): the DC voltage and current at which the diode sits in a circuit. Static resistance RD = VD/ID: the slope of the line from the origin to the Q-point, inverted.

RD = VDID
  • Near the knee and below: little current for the voltage → large RD (deck slide 84).
  • Higher up: much current → small RD.
  • Reverse: almost no current → enormous RD.

Answer. 0.7/0.002 = 350 Ω. At 0.6 V: 0.6/0.0115 = 52 Ω. At −5 V: 5/10−12 = 5 × 1012 Ω.

Check 7

(a) RD at 0.65 V and 5 mA? (b) Why does RD change along the curve? (c) Which is larger, RD at 1 mA or at 10 mA, for the same diode?

8 · Dynamic (AC) resistance rd

In a circuit a small AC signal often rides on the DC (deck slide 85). The Q-point wobbles up and down the curve, so what matters is the slope there.

practice · deck slides 85–86

Find the dynamic resistance at ID = 2 mA (η = 1, VT = 26 mV). Compare with RD = 350 Ω from step 7.

Guess first

Larger or smaller than RD?

Terms

Dynamic (AC) resistance rd = ΔVD/ΔID for a small change: the inverse slope of the tangent at the Q-point (deck slide 86).

Use the same figure as step 7: the red tangent.

Derivation (deck slide 86; each line follows from the one before):

  1. Forward: ID = IseVD/ηVT (step 4).
  2. Differentiate: dID/dVD = IseVD/ηVT × 1/(ηVT) = ID/ηVT.
  3. Invert:
rd = ηVTID ≈ 26 mVID   (η = 1)
  • Only ID matters, not Is or the exact curve. Double the current, halve rd.
  • For large swings use the average AC resistance rav = ΔV/ΔI between the two end points (deck slide 85).

Answer. rd = 26 mV / 2 mA = 13 Ω, much smaller than RD = 350 Ω: the curve is steep (small rd) though its line from the origin is shallow (large RD).

Check 8

(a) rd at 0.5 mA and at 5 mA? (b) rd at 2 mA if η = 2? (c) Check: at 0.6 V the diode of step 7 carries 11.5 mA. Find rd with VT = 25.9 mV and compare with RD = 52 Ω.

9 · Equivalent circuits: three models

To solve a circuit by hand, the curve is replaced by straight lines (deck slides 87–89).

practice · deck slides 87–89

A 5 V source drives a 1 kΩ resistor in series with a Si diode (forward). Find the current with (a) the ideal model, (b) the simplified 0.7 V model, (c) the piecewise-linear model with rav = 10 Ω.

Guess first

How much do the three answers differ: by half, or by a few per cent?

Terms

Equivalent circuit: simple parts (switch, battery, resistor) that behave like the device. rav (deck: RF): the resistance of the sloped straight line above Vγ.

Model Forward Reverse Use
① Ideal (slide 89) short (0 V) open quick first look, large voltages
② Simplified (slide 88) battery Vγ = 0.7 V open default for exam circuits
③ Piecewise-linear (slide 87) 0.7 V in series with rav open when told rav

Answer. (a) I = 5/1000 = 5.0 mA. (b) (5 − 0.7)/1000 = 4.30 mA. (c) (5 − 0.7)/(1000 + 10) = 4.26 mA; VD = 0.7 + 4.26 mA × 10 Ω = 0.743 V.

The exact diode equation (Is = 1 pA) gives 4.43 mA at 0.575 V. With 5 V available the models agree within 15 %; at low source voltages the 0.7 V matters much more.

10 · Transition (depletion) capacitance CT

A capacitor stores + charge on one plate and − on the other. 03a's depletion region already has that shape. Now change the voltage a little (deck slides 90–95).

practice · deck slides 91–95

The running junction of 03a (NA = 1017, ND = 1016, W = 0.333 μm at 0 V) has area A = 100 μm × 100 μm. Find CT at 0 V and at 5 V reverse.

Guess first

More reverse voltage widens W. Does CT go up or down?

Terms

Capacitance C = dQ/dV: extra charge stored per extra volt (deck slide 93). Transition (depletion, space-charge, junction) capacitance CT: the capacitance from the ions of the depletion region; it dominates in reverse bias (deck slide 95). Varactor: a diode used as a voltage-controlled capacitor.

  • Raise VR by ΔVR: W widens, more ions are bared on each edge, so the charge grows by ΔQ (deck slides 91–92). Majority carriers leave in about 0.6 ps (deck slide 92), so this happens instantly for any practical signal.
  • The extra charge sits at the edges, a distance W apart, like a parallel-plate capacitor (deck slide 94):
CT = εAW
Symbol What it is Unit
ε permittivity of Si, 1.04 × 10−12 F/cm
A junction area cm2
W depletion width at this bias (03a step 12) cm
  • Since W ∝ √(Vbi + VR): CT ∝ 1/√(Vbi + VR). More reverse voltage → smaller CT (deck slide 95). That is the varactor.

Answer. A = (10−2 cm)2 = 10−4 cm2. At 0 V: CT = 1.04 × 10−12 × 10−4 / 3.33 × 10−5 = 3.12 pF. At 5 V reverse W = 0.909 μm (03a step 12): CT = 1.14 pF, 0.37 times as much.

11 · Diffusion (storage) capacitance CD

In forward bias a second store of charge appears, outside the depletion region (deck slides 96–97).

practice · deck slide 96

A Si diode conducts 1 mA forward. The minority-carrier lifetime is τ = 1 μs, η = 1. Find CD and compare with CT = 3.12 pF of step 10.

Guess first

pF, nF or μF?

Terms

Stored charge Q: the extra minority carriers injected across the junction (step 2) and not yet recombined. Lifetime τ: the average time an extra minority carrier lives before recombining (02b step 10). Diffusion (storage) capacitance CD: dQ/dV of that stored charge; it dominates in forward bias.

Building CD (each line follows from the one before):

  1. Each injected carrier lives τ on average. A current ID injects ID/q carriers per second, so the store holds Q = τID (charge in, per second, × time each stays).
  2. CD = dQ/dVD = τ × dID/dVD = τ × ID/(ηVT) (step 8, line 2).
CD = τIDηVT
Symbol What it is Unit
τ mean lifetime of the minority carriers s
ID forward current A
η, VT as in step 3 —, V
  • CD ∝ ID: it is zero in reverse and grows with forward current.

Answer. Q = 1 μs × 1 mA = 1 nC. CD = 10−6 × 10−3 / 0.0259 = 38.6 nF, about 12 000 times CT. In forward bias CD dominates; in reverse CT does.

(a) τ = 20 ns, ID = 10 mA, η = 1: CD? (b) The current doubles: what happens to CD? (c) Why is CD negligible in reverse bias?

12 · Reverse recovery: a diode cannot switch off at once

The stored charge of step 11 has to go somewhere when the diode is switched from forward to reverse (deck slides 97–100).

practice · deck slides 97–100

A diode carries IF = 10 mA (+10 V through 1 kΩ). At t = 0 the source flips to −5 V. With τ = 100 ns, how long does it conduct backwards? Use the charge-control result ts = τln(1 + IF/IR).

Guess first

At the instant the source flips, what is the diode current: zero, or something else?

Terms

Storage time ts: the time the stored minority carriers keep the diode conducting backwards at a steady IR. Transition time tt: the time for the reverse current to then decay to the leakage level. Reverse recovery time trr = ts + tt: the total (deck slide 100).

  • While stored charge remains, the junction stays forward-biased (about 0.7 V). The diode looks like a closed switch, so the current is set by the circuit: IR = 5 V/1 kΩ = 5 mA, backwards (deck slide 99).
  • When the store is empty, the junction can take the reverse voltage; the current decays to −Is over tt.
  • trr ranges from a few ns to 1 μs in commercial diodes (deck slide 100). It sets the highest frequency a diode can rectify: a period must be much longer than trr.
  • Faster recovery: shorter τ, or a bigger IR to pull the charge out faster.

Answer. Not zero: it jumps to −5 mA and stays there. ts = 100 ns × ln(1 + 10/5) = 100 × 1.10 = 110 ns. With tt = 30 ns (an assumed value: it depends on CT and the circuit; the figure uses it), trr ≈ 140 ns.

Check 12

(a) Same diode, IR raised to 10 mA: ts? And 20 mA? (b) Why does the diode conduct backwards for a while? (c) Why does a short trr matter at high frequency?

Exam form

Diode equation (deck slides 79, 82–83). ID = Is(eVD/ηVT − 1); VT = kT/q = T/11 600 ≈ 26 mV; Is reverse saturation current (minority carriers, ∝ ni2); η = 1 to 2 (1 unless told). Forward: ID ≈ IseVD/ηVT; reverse: ID ≈ −Is. Knee: Si 0.7 V, Ge 0.3 V, GaAs 1.2 V.

Why exponential. Barrier q(Vbi − VD); Boltzmann share above it ∝ eVD/VT; diffusion current IseVD/VT minus fixed drift current Is.

Temperature (deck slides 80–81). Is2 = Is1 × 2(T2 − T1)/10; VD falls about 2 mV/°C at fixed current; Ge more sensitive.

Resistances (deck slides 84–86). Static RD = VD/ID. Dynamic: dI/dV = ID/ηVT, so rd = ηVT/ID ≈ 26 mV/ID. Average rav = ΔV/ΔI for large swings.

Equivalent circuits (deck slides 87–89). Piecewise-linear: Vγ + rav; simplified: Vγ only; ideal: switch. Reverse: open.

Capacitances (deck slides 91–97).

Reverse recovery (deck slides 97–100). Stored charge keeps a reverse current IR for ts, then it decays over tt; trr = ts + tt, ns to μs; limits switching frequency.

Traps

  • Using η = 2 without being told: state your choice.
  • VT = 26 mV: rd = 26 mV/2 mA = 13 Ω, not 0.013 Ω.
  • RD (V/I, from the origin) is not rd (the tangent slope).
  • Is doubles every 10 °C, so 30 °C is ×8, not ×3.
  • The forward voltage falls with temperature at fixed current.
  • CT is the reverse-bias capacitance; CD the forward-bias one. Do not swap them.
  • At switch-off the current does not drop to zero at once: it reverses for ts.

1 · Junction formation and the built-in voltage

Deck slides 77–78

For an abrupt silicon pn junction, Vbi = 0.85 V. Let W0 and W1 be the depletion widths for Va = 0 V and Va = 0.6 V. What is W1/W0? Repeat for a reverse bias of 2 V.

Guess first

Does forward bias make the depletion region wider or narrower?

Terms

Abrupt junction: doping switches from p to n in one step. Ion: a dopant atom that lost its carrier: charged, fixed. Bias: a DC voltage across the device.

At contact, holes diffuse p → n and electrons n → p (02) and recombine, leaving fixed ions: − on the p side, + on the n side. This carrier-free strip is the depletion region. Its field (n → p) pushes carriers back; diffusion stops when drift cancels it. The voltage across it then is

Vbi = VT ln(NAND/ni2)

about 0.6–0.9 V in Si. (A voltmeter reads zero across a loose diode: the metal contacts cancel it.) Solving Poisson (02) gives a triangular field and

W = √[(2ε/q)(1/NA + 1/ND)(Vbi − Va)]   NAxp = NDxn

Va is positive for forward bias. The region reaches further into the lightly doped side. Forward bias lowers the barrier to Vbi − Va: the region narrows and majority carriers pour across. Reverse bias widens it; only a tiny current of minority carriers flows.

Answer. W ∝ √(Vbi − Va). Forward 0.6 V: √(0.25/0.85) = 0.54, it shrinks. Reverse 2 V: √(2.85/0.85) = 1.83, it expands.

2 · The diode I–V equation

practice · slides 79–83

A diode has Is = 1 pA and η = 1 at 300 K. Find its current at VD = 0.6 V. What happens if VD rises by 60 mV?

Guess first

Will 60 mV make a small change or a big one?

Lowering the barrier lets exponentially more carriers cross:

ID = Is(eVD/ηVT − 1)   VT = kT/q = T/11 600 ≈ 26 mV
Symbol Meaning
Is reverse saturation current: minority carriers; nA in Si, μA in Ge; ∝ ni2
η ideality factor, 1 to 2 (slides: 1 for Ge, 2 for Si); take 1 unless told
Knee voltage Si 0.7 V, Ge 0.3 V, GaAs 1.2 V

Forward (VD ≫ VT): ID ≈ IseVD/ηVT. Reverse: ID ≈ −Is, flat. Breakdown at large reverse voltage (05). Temperature: Is doubles every 10 °C, Is2 = Is1 × 2(T2 − T1)/10 (slide 81); at fixed current VD falls about 2 mV/°C.

Answer. ID = 10−12 × e0.6/0.0259 = 12 mA. +60 mV multiplies it by e0.06/0.0259 ≈ 10: every 60 mV is a factor of ten, which is why the curve looks like a wall at 0.7 V.

Check 2

(a) Is = 10 nA at 25 °C. Find Is at 55 °C. (b) Why is the reverse current almost independent of reverse voltage? (c) Which leaks more, Si or Ge, and why?

3 · Resistances, models, capacitances, switching

practice · slides 84–100

A diode carries 2 mA at 0.7 V. Find its static and dynamic resistance (η = 1).

Guess first

Which of the two is larger?

Static: RD = VD/ID. Dynamic (slope for a small AC signal), from differentiating the diode equation: rd = ηVT/ID ≈ 26 mV/ID. Average AC resistance rav = ΔVD/ΔID for large swings.

Capacitance When Formula Cause
Transition CT reverse εA/W the depletion region is a dielectric; more reverse V → wider W → smaller CT (varactor)
Diffusion CD forward τID/ηVT stored minority carriers; much larger than CT

Switching. When the voltage reverses, stored minority carriers keep a large reverse current flowing for the storage time ts, then it decays over the transition time tt: trr = ts + tt, ns to μs. It limits the usable frequency.

Answer. RD = 0.7/2 mA = 350 Ω. rd = 26/2 = 13 Ω: the curve is steep there.

Check 3

(a) rd at 0.5 mA and 5 mA? (b) Which capacitance matters in reverse bias, and how does it change as reverse voltage grows? (c) What causes the reverse recovery time?

Junction. Diffusion → ions → depletion region → field → equilibrium; sketch charge, field, potential. Vbi = VTln(NAND/ni2); W formula; forward/reverse effect.

Diode equation. ID = Is(eVD/ηVT − 1); terms; regions; temperature rules.

Resistances, capacitances. RD, rd = 26 mV/ID; models; CT vs CD table; trr = ts + tt.

Traps

  • W depends on √(Vbi − Va), not on Va alone.
  • Using η = 2 without being told; state your choice.
  • Confusing CT (reverse) with CD (forward).
  • rd uses mV over mA, giving ohms: 26/2 = 13 Ω, not 0.013.

Build

1 · Atoms and covalent bonds

Predict

Copper conducts; glass does not. Silicon sits in between. What do you think silicon's outer electrons are doing that a metal's are not?

Terms

Valence electrons: the electrons in an atom's outermost shell; they are the ones that bond. Covalent bond: two neighbouring atoms share one pair of electrons, one from each. Crystal: atoms arranged in one repeating pattern.

  • Silicon (14 electrons) and germanium (32) both have 4 valence electrons (deck slide 5). Each Si atom bonds to 4 neighbours, sharing one electron with each. Every valence electron is then locked in a bond.
  • An electron locked in a bond cannot carry current: it cannot leave its place.
  • In a metal, the outer electrons are not locked in bonds at all: they roam the whole crystal. That is the difference the Predict asks for.
Check 1

(a) How many bonds does each Si atom make, and how many electrons are in one bond? (b) Why does a perfect Si crystal at 0 K not conduct?

2 · Breaking a bond: the free electron

Some bonds must break for silicon to conduct at all.

Predict

What could supply the energy to pull an electron out of a bond?

Terms

Free electron: an electron that has left its bond and can move through the crystal. Intrinsic silicon: silicon so pure that every free carrier comes from broken bonds, none from impurities (deck slide 13).

  • Heat (thermal energy) or light (a photon) can break a bond (deck slide 11). The released electron wanders through the crystal and can carry current.
  • In pure silicon at 300 K the number of free electrons per cm3 is about 1.5 × 1010 (deck slide 11). That sounds large, but Si has 5 × 1022 atoms per cm3: only about 1 bond in 3 × 1012 is broken.
  • So pure silicon conducts, but very weakly. Most of this course is about raising the carrier count on purpose (steps 6–7).
Check 2

(a) Name the two energy sources that free electrons. (b) Pure Si has 5 × 1022 atoms/cm3 and 1.5 × 1010 free electrons/cm3. Roughly how many atoms per free electron?

3 · The hole: the second carrier

A broken bond leaves something behind, and it carries current too.

Predict

A bond is missing one electron. A neighbouring bond electron is next to the gap. What can it do, and what does the gap appear to do?

Terms

Hole: the empty place in a bond after its electron leaves. It behaves like a particle with charge +q. Carrier: anything mobile that carries charge; in a semiconductor, free electrons and holes.

  • A bond electron next to the hole can hop into it with very little energy: it stays a bond electron, just in a neighbouring bond. The hole is now where that electron came from.
  • Repeat: electrons step left one at a time, so the hole travels right. A missing negative charge moving right is the same current as a positive charge moving right. So we track the hole as a +q particle.
  • Every broken bond makes one free electron and one hole. In intrinsic silicon:
n = p = ni

n = free electrons per cm3; p = holes per cm3; ni = the intrinsic carrier concentration (each of them, not their sum).

Check 3

(a) Is a hole an empty seat in a bond or a positive particle? Which description is the physics, and which is the bookkeeping? (b) Why must n equal p in pure silicon?

4 · Energy bands

The bond picture tells what happens. To say how much energy it costs, we need the band picture, which every later file uses.

Predict

A single atom only allows electrons at certain sharp energies. Bring 1022 atoms close together. What do you guess happens to those sharp energies?

Terms

Energy level: an energy an electron is allowed to have. Band: a dense range of allowed energies formed from one level of many atoms. Band gap Eg: a range of energies no electron in the crystal can have.

Reading the figure. Each atom alone allows sharp energies (left edge). When atoms are pushed close, their electrons interact and each sharp level splits into as many levels as there are atoms, packed so tightly they form a continuous band (deck slides 21–24). At the actual spacing in silicon (dashed line), two bands matter:

Band Its electrons Edge
Valence band bond electrons (step 1) top edge EV
Conduction band free electrons (step 2) bottom edge EC

Between them is the band gap, Eg = EC − EV: no allowed energies. Breaking a bond = lifting an electron from the valence band to the conduction band, which costs at least Eg. The hole is the empty place left in the valence band.

Material Eg at 300 K Result
metal bands overlap, no gap electrons always free
Ge · Si · GaAs 0.66 · 1.12 · 1.42 eV heat frees a few
insulator (diamond, glass) > 5 eV almost none freed

5 · How ni depends on temperature and on Eg

Predict

Heat silicon from 25 °C to 35 °C. Does ni rise by about 3 %, about 30 %, or about 100 %?

Reading the figure. Each curve shows how many carriers heat frees (per cm3) at each temperature. Three facts come straight off it:

  • Steep rise with T. For Si near room temperature, ni roughly doubles every 10 °C (deck slide 37: 8.1 × 109 at 25 °C, 1.7 × 1010 at 35 °C).
  • Smaller gap, more carriers. At 300 K: Ge ≈ 2.4 × 1013, Si ≈ 1.5 × 1010, GaAs ≈ 1.8 × 106 cm−3.
  • Why so steep: the controlling factor is e−Eg/2kT (derived in 01b). At 300 K: Ge 2.8 × 10−6, Si 4.1 × 10−10, GaAs 1.2 × 10−12. A small change in Eg/kT changes the count by orders of magnitude.

Answer to the Predict. About 100 %: it doubles. That is why semiconductor resistance falls when heated (negative temperature coefficient), while a metal's rises: a metal already has all its electrons free, so heat only adds collisions (deck slide 19).

ni differs between books

Si: 1.5 × 1010 (deck, Boylestad), 1.45 × 1010, or 1.0 × 1010 (newer measurements). Use the value the question gives; if none is given, state the one you use.

Check 5

(a) Roughly how much does Si's ni grow from 300 K to 330 K? (b) Why does Ge leak more current than Si at the same temperature?

6 · Donors: n-type silicon

ni is tiny and swings with temperature. Devices need carrier counts that are large and fixed. Doping supplies them.

Predict

Replace one Si atom with phosphorus, which has 5 valence electrons. Four go into bonds. What happens to the fifth?

Terms

Doping: adding chosen impurity atoms on purpose (deck slide 18). Donor: an impurity with 5 valence electrons (P, As, Sb) that gives away one electron. ND: donor atoms per cm3.

  • The 5th electron is held only weakly: ≈ 45 meV for P, 54 meV for As (deck slide 39). In the band picture it sits at a donor level ED just below EC.
  • At 300 K, kT = 26 meV, so heat frees practically every donor electron: every donor is ionised. Each gives one free electron and leaves a fixed + ion (the P atom, now one electron short) that cannot move.
  • No hole is made: the bonds are all complete. So electrons outnumber holes.

n-type silicon: electrons are the majority carriers, holes the minority carriers, and n ≈ ND.

How strong this is: 1 part per million of P gives ND = 10−6 × 5 × 1022 = 5 × 1016 cm−3, about 3.3 million times ni (deck slide 17: "1 part per million can transform a poor conductor into a good one").

Check 6

(a) After a donor gives its electron, what is left behind, and can it move? (b) Why is n-type silicon still electrically neutral?

7 · Acceptors: p-type silicon

Predict

Boron has 3 valence electrons. Which bond around it is incomplete, and what will a nearby bond electron do?

Terms

Acceptor: an impurity with 3 valence electrons (B, Al, Ga) that accepts one electron from a neighbouring bond. NA: acceptor atoms per cm3.

  • Boron fills only 3 of its 4 bonds (right panel of the lattice figure above). A neighbouring bond electron needs only ≈ 45 meV to jump in (deck slide 39): the acceptor level EA sits just above EV.
  • The jump completes boron's bonds, so boron becomes a fixed − ion, and leaves a hole in the valence band, free to move.
  • At 300 K every acceptor is ionised: p ≈ NA.

8 · The mass-action law: n · p = ni2

Doping fixes the majority count. The minority count needs one more rule.

practice

Silicon is doped with ND = 1016 cm−3, ni = 1.5 × 1010 cm−3, 300 K. Find n and p.

Guess first

Does adding electrons leave the hole count at ni, raise it, or lower it?

Terms

Thermal equilibrium: steady temperature, no applied voltage or light: nothing drives the material. Recombination: a free electron falls into a hole; both carriers disappear.

  • Bonds keep breaking (generation, set by temperature only) and electrons keep falling into holes (recombination). In equilibrium the two rates are equal.
  • Recombination needs an electron to meet a hole, so its rate grows with n × p. Generation depends only on T. Balance therefore fixes the product:
n · p = ni2   (thermal equilibrium, any doping)
  • So extra electrons make holes rarer: a hole now meets an electron and vanishes sooner. 01b proves the law another way.
  • Charge neutrality: total + charge = total − charge, so n + NA = p + ND. With both dopants, the larger wins: n ≈ ND − NA if ND > NA (called compensated silicon).

Answer. All donors ionised: n ≈ ND = 1016 cm−3. p = ni2/n = (1.5 × 1010)2/1016 = 2.25 × 1020/1016 = 2.25 × 104 cm−3. Holes fell from 1.5 × 1010 to about 2 × 104. (With ni = 1010: p = 104.)

When n ≈ ND fails

If ND is not much larger than ni, solve neutrality and mass action together: n = ND/2 + √((ND/2)2 + ni2). Example ND = 1010, ni = 1.5 × 1010: n = 5 × 109 + √(2.5 × 1019 + 2.25 × 1020) = 2.08 × 1010, p = 2.25 × 1020/2.08 × 1010 = 1.08 × 1010 cm−3.

Check 8

(a) Si has NA = 5 × 1017 cm−3 of boron, ni = 1.5 × 1010. Type, p and n? (b) NA = 1016 and ND = 4 × 1015 cm−3 together. Type, majority and minority concentrations?

Exam form

Intrinsic semiconductor (definition + sketch). Pure crystal; carriers only from bonds broken by heat or light; each broken bond gives one free electron and one hole, so n = p = ni. Draw the flat lattice with one broken bond.

Hole. The vacancy left by a bond electron; neighbouring bond electrons move into it, so it moves opposite to them and acts as a +q carrier.

Energy bands (sketch insulator / semiconductor / conductor). Valence band (bond electrons, top EV), conduction band (free electrons, bottom EC), band gap Eg = EC − EV. Eg: Ge 0.66, Si 1.12, GaAs 1.42 eV; insulator > 5 eV; metal: bands overlap.

Temperature coefficient. Semiconductor: heat frees more carriers (ni ∝ e−Eg/2kT, Si doubles per ≈ 10 °C) → resistance falls (negative). Metal: carrier count fixed, more collisions → resistance rises (positive).

Extrinsic semiconductor. n-type: donors (P, As, Sb; 5 valence e⁻), ED ≈ 45 meV below EC, n ≈ ND, holes minority. p-type: acceptors (B, Al, Ga; 3 valence e⁻), EA ≈ 45 meV above EV, p ≈ NA, electrons minority. Draw both lattices and both band diagrams.

Numerical procedure (carrier concentrations).

  1. Find the net doping: ND − NA (or NA − ND); the larger dopant sets the type.
  2. Majority ≈ net doping (all dopants ionised at 300 K).
  3. Minority = ni2 / majority. State the ni you used.
  4. If net doping is not ≫ ni, use n = N/2 + √((N/2)2 + ni2).

Traps

  • Writing p = ND − n or p = ni for doped material: the minority is always ni2/majority.
  • Saying doped silicon is charged: each free carrier left a fixed ion of the opposite sign. It is neutral.
  • Calling holes "positive electrons" or "missing atoms": a hole is a missing bond electron.
  • Mixing ni = 1010 and 1.5 × 1010 within one answer.
  • Saying n = p = ni counts both carriers together: ni is the count of each.

Answers

Check 1. (a) 4 bonds, 2 electrons per bond (one from each atom). (b) Every valence electron is locked in a bond; none can move.

Check 2. (a) Heat and light. (b) 5 × 1022/1.5 × 1010 ≈ 3 × 1012 atoms per free electron.

Check 3. (a) Physically it is an empty place in a bond; treating it as a +q particle is the bookkeeping that gives the right current. (b) Each broken bond makes exactly one of each.

Check 4. (a) Free electron = an electron in the conduction band; hole = an empty state in the valence band. (b) That energy lies in the gap: there are no allowed states there.

Check 5. (a) Three steps of 10 K, each about ×2: ≈ 8× (formula: 8.3). (b) Ge's gap is smaller (0.66 vs 1.12 eV), so heat frees about 1600× more carriers (2.4 × 1013 vs 1.5 × 1010).

Check 6. (a) A fixed + ion (P with one electron short); it is part of the crystal and cannot move. (b) For every free electron there is one + ion: total charge is zero.

Check 7. (a) p-type (3 valence electrons: acceptor). (b) From bonds broken by heat: n = ni2/p, a very small number.

Check 8. (a) p-type. p ≈ 5 × 1017; n = 2.25 × 1020/5 × 1017 = 450 cm−3. (b) NA > ND: p-type. p ≈ 1016 − 4 × 1015 = 6 × 1015; n = 2.25 × 1020/6 × 1015 = 3.75 × 104 cm−3 (ni = 1.5 × 1010).

Self-test.

  1. P: fixed + ion, one free electron nearby. B: fixed − ion, one hole nearby. All other bonds complete.
  2. Heat lifts electrons across Eg; ni rises ∝ e−Eg/2kT; more carriers → lower resistance. The extra collisions are outweighed.
  3. n = 1015, p = 1020/1015 = 105 cm−3.
  4. n-type. n ≈ 2 × 1017 − 5 × 1016 = 1.5 × 1017; p = 2.25 × 1020/1.5 × 1017 = 1500 cm−3.
  5. Larger gap (1.42 vs 1.12 eV) and ni ∝ e−Eg/2kT: e−0.30/0.0518 ≈ 1/330, and the effective-seat counts (01b) differ too; together ≈ 104.
  6. 45/25.9 = 1.74 kT: small enough that heat frees practically every donor electron at 300 K.
  7. Pure Si has too few carriers (ni ≈ 1010), and ni changes rapidly with temperature. Doping gives a large, temperature-steady count, and sets the carrier type.

1 · Energy bands, electrons and holes

Deck slides 19–24

Using energy bands, explain why heating lowers the resistance of a semiconductor but raises the resistance of a metal.

Guess first

Write one line for each material before reading on.

Terms

Valence electron: an outer electron that forms bonds; Si has 4. Covalent bond: two atoms share a pair of electrons. eV: the energy one electron gains crossing 1 V, 1.6 × 10−19 J.

  • In a crystal the allowed energies form bands. The valence band holds electrons locked in bonds; the conduction band holds free electrons. Between them is the band gap Eg, with no states.
  • At 0 K every bond is full: silicon is an insulator. At room temperature heat breaks a few bonds. Each gives one free electron and one hole, a missing electron that moves as a charge of +q. So pure silicon has n = p = ni.
  • ni ≈ 1010 cm−3 against 5 × 1022 atoms: one bond in 1012. ni2 ∝ T3e−Eg/kT: in Si it roughly doubles every 10 °C. A smaller gap gives a larger ni, so Ge leaks more than Si.

Answer. Semiconductor: heating breaks more bonds, so the carrier count rises exponentially; that outweighs the extra scattering, and resistance falls (negative temperature coefficient). Metal: the bands overlap, so the number of free electrons is fixed; heat only adds scattering, and resistance rises.

Check 1

(a) What are the two carriers, and what is each physically? (b) Why is n = p in pure silicon? (c) Which has the larger ni, Si or Ge, and why?

2 · Doping and the mass-action law

practice

Silicon is doped with ND = 1016 donor atoms per cm3. At 300 K, find the electron and hole concentrations.

Guess first

More holes or fewer holes than in pure silicon?

Terms

Donor: 5 valence electrons (P, As, Sb); the fifth is set free. Acceptor: 3 valence electrons (B, Al, Ga); the missing bond is a hole.

Donors make n-type silicon (electrons majority, holes minority); acceptors make p-type. At room temperature every dopant is ionised: n ≈ ND (n-type), p ≈ NA (p-type). In equilibrium the product is fixed by temperature alone:

n · p = ni2

Extra electrons make holes rarer, because a hole meets an electron and recombines sooner. The crystal stays neutral: each free electron left a fixed + donor ion (n + NA = p + ND). With both dopants, the larger wins: n ≈ ND − NA (compensated).

Answer. n ≈ 1016 cm−3. p = 1020/1016 = 104 cm−3: holes fell from 1010 to 104. (With ni = 1.5 × 1010, p = 2.25 × 104.)

Check 2

(a) Si has NA = 5 × 1017 cm−3 of boron. Type, p and n? (b) A doped bar has billions of extra free electrons. Why is it still neutral?

3 · The Fermi level

Deck slide 38

Show that the Fermi level of an intrinsic semiconductor lies at the middle of the forbidden gap.

Guess first

Which way does EF move when donors are added?

Terms

f(E): the probability that a state at energy E is occupied. NC, NV: effective number of states at the band edges.

f(E) = 1/(1 + e(E − EF)/kT)

EF is where f = ½. Counting filled conduction states and empty valence states:

n = NCe−(EC − EF)/kT   p = NVe−(EF − EV)/kT

EF near EC means many electrons (n-type); near EV, many holes. For n-type, EF − Ei = kT ln(ND/ni). At very high temperature ni catches up with the doping and EF returns to mid-gap.

Answer. Intrinsic: n = p, so NCe−(EC − EF)/kT = NVe−(EF − EV)/kT. With NC = NV the exponents are equal: EC − EF = EF − EV, so EF = (EC + EV)/2. Without that assumption, EF = (EC + EV)/2 + (kT/2)ln(NV/NC), off by only a few meV.

01b — The Fermi level: counting electrons and holes

Unit 1–2 deck, slides 25–38 · syllabus Unit 1 (energy bands in intrinsic and extrinsic silicon) · needs 01a (bands, EC, EV, Eg, n, p, ni, doping) · one deck derivation (slide 38); other openers are Predict prompts

Map

1 States = seats→2 f(E), read point by point→3 f(E) on the band diagram→4 Seats per energy g(E)→5 n = seats × chance→6 The exponential shortcut→7 NC and NV→8 n·p = ni2 proved→9 EF at mid-gap→10 EF moves with doping

01a counted carriers with rules of thumb: n ≈ ND, p = ni2/n. This file explains where those rules come from, using one idea: number of carriers = number of places an electron could sit × the chance each place is taken. The Fermi level EF is the single number that sets that chance. 03a uses it to explain the pn junction.

Build

1 · States: the seats electrons sit in

Predict

A cinema has 500 seats; 80 % are taken. How many people are inside? Now: what two numbers would you need to know how many electrons sit in the conduction band?

Terms

State: one allowed place for one electron at one energy, a "seat". At most one electron per state (Pauli exclusion principle). Occupied / empty: a seat with or without an electron.

  • In the conduction band almost every seat is empty; a filled one is a free electron.
  • In the valence band almost every seat is filled; an empty one is a hole.
  • So, exactly as in the cinema:
electrons in a band = (number of seats) × (chance a seat is filled)

Steps 2–3 give the chance. Steps 4–7 give the seats.

Check 1

(a) In seat language, what is a hole? (b) Why can no electron be found in the gap?

2 · The Fermi–Dirac function f(E), read point by point

Predict

At 0 K electrons fill the lowest seats first, up to some energy, and nothing above. Sketch "chance a seat is filled" against energy at 0 K. Then guess how heat changes the sketch.

Terms

f(E): the probability that a seat at energy E is filled, in thermal equilibrium (deck slide 28). Fermi level EF: the energy where that probability is exactly ½ (deck slide 30). kT: the size of a typical thermal "kick", 0.0259 eV at 300 K.

f(E) = 11 + e(E − EF)/kT

Read it at four seats (300 K):

Seat energy (E − EF)/kT f(E) Meaning
exactly EF 0 1/(1 + 1) = 0.5 half the time filled
1 kT above 1 1/(1 + 2.72) = 0.27 usually empty
3 kT above (0.078 eV) 3 1/(1 + 20.1) = 0.047 rarely filled
3 kT below −3 1/(1 + 0.05) = 0.953 almost always filled
  • At 0 K (dashed): a step. Every seat below EF filled, every seat above empty.
  • At 300 K: heat rounds the step over a width of a few kT. Far below EF: f → 1. Far above: f → 0, falling exponentially (deck slide 30).
  • EF itself is just a reference energy. It does not need a seat at it; in silicon it usually lies inside the gap.
Check 2

(a) f(EF) at 300 K? At 600 K? (b) f for a seat 0.1 eV above EF at 300 K? (c) A seat 0.2 eV below EF: what is f, and what is the chance it is empty?

3 · f(E) placed on the band diagram

The graph in step 2 has energy on the horizontal axis. Turn it so energy points up, and lay it beside the bands: now it shows which seats in each band are filled.

Predict

In pure silicon EF sits in the middle of the gap, 0.56 eV from each band edge. Is the chance a conduction-band seat is filled large or tiny?

Reading the figure.

  • In the gap f(E) has values, but there are no seats there, so nothing is filled.
  • Conduction band: only the tail of f reaches it. At EC, 0.56 eV above EF, f = 1/(1 + e21.6) ≈ 4 × 10−10. Tiny, but there are very many seats, so the electron count is ni.
  • Valence band: f is almost 1. The chance a seat is empty is 1 − f(E): the hole probability. By symmetry it is also ≈ 4 × 10−10 at EV.

Answer to the Predict. Tiny: about 4 in 10 billion. That is why pure silicon has so few carriers.

Check 3

(a) Which function gives the chance a valence-band seat holds a hole? (b) If EF moved up towards EC, which tail grows and which shrinks?

4 · How many seats: the density of states g(E)

The chance per seat is known. The other factor is how many seats there are at each energy.

Predict

Are seats spread evenly up the conduction band, or packed more densely at its bottom edge, or more densely higher up?

Terms

Density of states g(E): number of seats per unit energy per cm3 at energy E (the deck's N(E), slide 26). Effective mass m*: the mass an electron (or hole) appears to have when moving in the crystal; for Si electrons, 1.08 × the free-electron mass.

  • At the very band edge there are no seats; moving into the band, the count grows as a square root (deck slide 26: N(E) = γE1/2, with E measured from the band edge):
gc(E) ∝ √(E − EC)   gv(E) ∝ √(EV − E)
  • The constant in front depends on the effective mass: heavier carriers → more seats.
  • So seats get more numerous going up into the conduction band, while f(E) gets smaller. The electrons end up where both are reasonable: just above EC.
Check 4

(a) What is g(E) inside the gap? (b) In words: what does g(E) = 0 at exactly E = EC mean?

5 · Electrons per energy: multiply seats by chance

Predict

Multiply a curve that rises from zero (seats) by one that falls fast (chance). Sketch the shape of the product.

  • Electrons: at each energy in the conduction band, electrons there = gc(E) × f(E). Adding up over the whole band (an integral):
n = ∫ gc(E) f(E) dE   (over the conduction band)
  • Holes: the same with the chance of being empty:
p = ∫ gv(E) [1 − f(E)] dE   (over the valence band)
  • The humps are narrow: almost all electrons sit within a few kT of EC, almost all holes within a few kT of EV. That is why "the electrons are at EC" is a good picture.
Check 5

(a) Why is the electron hump zero exactly at EC? (b) Why does it die off a few kT above EC?

6 · The exponential shortcut (Boltzmann approximation)

The integral in step 5 is awkward because of the "1 +" in f(E). In silicon it can almost always be dropped.

Predict

For x = 5, compare 1/(1 + ex) with e−x. How close are they?

  • When a seat is more than about 3 kT above EF, e(E − EF)/kT > 20, so the "1 +" barely matters (deck slide 31):
f(E) ≈ e−(E − EF)/kT   (E − EF ≥ 3kT)
  • For holes, the chance of being empty, 1 − f, becomes (deck slide 33):
1 − f(E) ≈ e−(EF − E)/kT   (EF − E ≥ 3kT)
  • In silicon EF normally sits in the gap, more than 3 kT (0.078 eV) from both band edges, so both shortcuts hold. Such silicon is called non-degenerate. (Very heavy doping, above about 1019 cm−3, breaks this; not needed in this course.)

Answer to the Predict. x = 5: exact 0.00669, shortcut 0.00674. They differ by under 1 %.

Check 6

(a) At x = 3, the exact f is 0.0474 and the shortcut gives 0.0498. Percentage error? (b) EF is 0.02 eV below EC. Can you use the shortcut? Why?

7 · NC and NV: the effective number of seats

With the shortcut, the integral of step 5 can be done once and for all. The result has one new number in it, and this is the step that defines it.

Predict

Electrons in the conduction band are spread over a few kT above EC. Could you replace the whole band by a single block of seats sitting exactly at EC and still get the right electron count? What would that block's size have to be?

Terms

NC: effective density of states of the conduction band: the number of seats per cm3 that, if all placed exactly at EC, would hold the same number of electrons as the real band. NV: the same for the valence band, placed at EV, counting holes.

  • Doing the integral with the shortcut (deck slide 32) gives:
n = NC e−(EC − EF)/kT   p = NV e−(EF − EV)/kT
  • Read each formula as seats × chance. NC = how many seats count; e−(EC − EF)/kT = the chance a seat at EC is filled (step 6). Only the gap between EF and the band edge matters.
  • What sets NC: the effective mass and temperature: NC = 2(2πmn*kT/h2)3/2, so NC ∝ T3/2.
Si, 300 K Value Compare
NC 2.8 × 1019 cm−3 (mn* = 1.08 m0) about 1 seat per 1800 atoms
NV 1.04 × 1019 cm−3 (mp* = 0.56 m0) some books: 1.8 × 1019

Worked use. EF is 0.25 eV below EC (300 K). n = 2.8 × 1019 × e−0.25/0.0259 = 2.8 × 1019 × 6.45 × 10−5 = 1.8 × 1015 cm−3.

Check 7

(a) In one sentence each, without symbols: what is NC, and what is the exponential next to it? (b) EF − EV = 0.20 eV. Find p. (c) By what factor does NC grow from 300 K to 600 K?

8 · Multiply n and p: the mass-action law, proved

01a stated n · p = ni2 from a rate argument. The two formulas of step 7 prove it directly.

Predict

Multiply n and p from step 7. Does EF survive in the product?

n · p = NCNV e−(EC − EF)/kT e−(EF − EV)/kT = NCNV e−(EC − EV)/kT = NCNV e−Eg/kT
  • EF cancels. The product depends only on the material (NC, NV, Eg) and T. Doping moves EF, so it changes n and p, but never their product.
  • Pure silicon has n = p = ni, so that product is ni2:
ni = √(NCNV) e−Eg/2kT
  • This is the e−Eg/2kT that made ni so temperature-sensitive in 01a step 5.
Deck slide 36

The slide writes the product as NVNCe−2EF/kT. That is a slip: the two exponents use different energies (EV and EC), and they combine to e−Eg/kT, with no EF. The slide's own conclusion (the product is a constant ni2(T)) needs the corrected form.

Why books disagree on ni

With NC = 2.8 × 1019, NV = 1.04 × 1019, Eg = 1.12 eV: ni = 1.71 × 1019 × e−21.6 ≈ 6.9 × 109, not 1.5 × 1010. The effective masses and Eg are known only to a few per cent, and ni depends on Eg exponentially. Use the ni the question gives.

Check 8

(a) Why can doping not change n · p? (b) Recompute √(NCNV) from the table in step 7.

9 · EF in intrinsic silicon lies at mid-gap

Deck slide 38

Show that the Fermi level in an intrinsic semiconductor lies at the middle of the forbidden gap.

Guess first

Which condition, true only for pure silicon, do you set up first?

Answer (each line follows from the one before):

  1. Intrinsic: n = p.
  2. Substitute step 7: NCe−(EC − EF)/kT = NVe−(EF − EV)/kT.
  3. Approximation: take NC ≈ NV. They cancel: e−(EC − EF)/kT = e−(EF − EV)/kT.
  4. Equal exponentials have equal exponents: EC − EF = EF − EV.
  5. So 2EF = EC + EV, and
EF = Ei = EC + EV2

Ei (the intrinsic level) is the name for EF in pure material.

  • Without step 3, the same algebra gives Ei = (EC + EV)/2 + (kT/2) ln(NV/NC). For Si: 0.01295 × ln(1.04/2.8) = −12.8 meV, about 1 % of the gap below the middle. "Mid-gap" is accurate to that.
Deck slide 38

The slide labels NC = NV "at equilibrium". Equilibrium does not make them equal: NC = NV is an approximation (for Si they differ by about 2.7×). Write "assuming NC ≈ NV" in the exam.

Check 9

(a) Where in the derivation did "intrinsic" enter? (b) If holes were heavier than electrons (NV > NC), would Ei sit slightly above or below mid-gap?

10 · EF moves with doping

practice

Si doped with ND = 1016 cm−3, ni = 1010 cm−3, 300 K. How far above mid-gap is EF? How far below EC?

Guess first

Up or down from mid-gap, and roughly how much of the 0.56 eV half-gap?

  • Measuring from Ei instead of the band edges, step 7 becomes (divide by the intrinsic case):
n = ni e(EF − Ei)/kT   p = ni e(Ei − EF)/kT
  • Each kT that EF rises multiplies n by e (2.72) and divides p by e: n · p stays ni2.
  • n-type, n ≈ ND: EF − Ei = kT ln(ND/ni). p-type, p ≈ NA: Ei − EF = kT ln(NA/ni).

Answer. EF − Ei = 0.0259 × ln(1016/1010) = 0.0259 × 13.8 = 0.358 eV above mid-gap. From the band edge directly: EC − EF = kT ln(NC/n) = 0.0259 × ln(2.8 × 1019/1016) = 0.206 eV. Check: 0.358 + 0.206 = 0.564 eV ≈ half the gap.

Why this matters next. In equilibrium EF is the same everywhere in a connected piece of material (deck slide 36). Join p-type (EF low) to n-type (EF high) and the bands must bend until the two EF line up. That bending is the pn junction, in 03a step 7.

Check 10

(a) p-type, NA = 1017, ni = 1010: how far is EF below mid-gap? (b) An n-type sample has EF 0.3 eV above Ei, ni = 1.5 × 1010. Find n. (c) Heat a doped sample until ni becomes larger than ND. Where does EF go?

Exam form

Fermi–Dirac function. f(E) = 1/(1 + e(E − EF)/kT) is the probability a state at energy E is occupied. f(EF) = ½ at every T. Sketch at 0 K (step) and T > 0 (rounded over a few kT).

Fermi level. The energy at which the occupation probability is ½; in equilibrium it is constant throughout the material.

Carrier concentrations.

  1. n = ∫ gc(E) f(E) dE over the conduction band; p = ∫ gv(E)[1 − f(E)] dE over the valence band; g ∝ √(energy from band edge).
  2. Boltzmann approximation (EF more than 3kT from the band edges): f ≈ e−(E − EF)/kT.
  3. Result: n = NCe−(EC − EF)/kT, p = NVe−(EF − EV)/kT, with NC, NV the effective densities of states (Si: 2.8 × 1019, 1.04 × 1019 cm−3).

Mass action. np = NCNVe−Eg/kT = ni2, independent of EF; ni = √(NCNV)e−Eg/2kT.

EF at mid-gap (deck slide 38). n = p → NCe−(EC − EF)/kT = NVe−(EF − EV)/kT → assume NC ≈ NV → EC − EF = EF − EV → EF = (EC + EV)/2. Exact: + (kT/2)ln(NV/NC).

Doped. n = nie(EF − Ei)/kT; n-type EF − Ei = kT ln(ND/ni); p-type Ei − EF = kT ln(NA/ni). Sketch the three band diagrams.

Traps

  • Saying f(E) is "the number of electrons at E": it is a probability. Electrons = seats × probability.
  • Saying "NC = NV at equilibrium": it is an approximation, not a law.
  • Writing np with EF left in the exponent (the slide 36 slip): it cancels.
  • Using kT in joules with energies in eV: at 300 K use 0.0259 eV.
  • Using the Boltzmann shortcut when EF is within 3kT of a band edge.
  • Putting EF for n-type below mid-gap: more electrons ↔ EF closer to EC.

Self-test

  1. Sketch f(E) at 0 K and 300 K and mark the value at EF.
  2. Probability that a state 0.2 eV below EF is empty, at 300 K?
  3. Explain in two sentences what NC is, using the words "seats" and "EC".
  4. Derive EF = (EC + EV)/2 for intrinsic silicon from memory, stating the approximation.
  5. Show from the n and p formulas that np does not depend on EF.
  6. ND = 1015 cm−3, ni = 1.5 × 1010: EF − Ei?
  7. Si with EC − EF = 0.206 eV: find n and say what ND that corresponds to.
  8. Why does a p-type sample's EF move back toward mid-gap when heated strongly?

Answers

Check 1. (a) An empty seat in the valence band. (b) The gap has no seats (no allowed states).

Check 2. (a) ½ at both: e0 = 1 for any T. (b) 1/(1 + e0.1/0.0259) = 1/(1 + 47.5) = 0.0206. (c) f = 1/(1 + e−7.72) = 0.99956; empty chance 1 − f = 4.4 × 10−4.

Check 3. (a) 1 − f(E). (b) The conduction-band tail grows (more electrons); the valence-band hole tail shrinks (fewer holes).

Check 4. (a) Zero: no seats. (b) Exactly at the edge there are no seats yet; they start and grow as you go into the band.

Check 5. (a) gc(EC) = 0: no seats at the edge. (b) f(E) falls by e for every kT; it outruns the √ growth of seats.

Check 6. (a) (0.0498 − 0.0474)/0.0474 ≈ 5 %. (b) No: 0.02 eV < 3kT = 0.078 eV; the "1 +" matters there.

Check 7. (a) NC: the number of seats that, all placed at EC, hold the band's electrons. The exponential: the chance one seat at EC is filled. (b) p = 1.04 × 1019 × e−0.20/0.0259 = 4.6 × 1015 cm−3. (c) (600/300)3/2 = 2.83×.

Check 8. (a) EF cancels in the product; only NC, NV, Eg, T remain. (b) √(2.8 × 1019 × 1.04 × 1019) = √(2.91 × 1038) = 1.71 × 1019 cm−3.

Check 9. (a) Line 1: n = p. (b) ln(NV/NC) > 0, so Ei sits slightly above mid-gap.

Check 10. (a) 0.0259 × ln(107) = 0.417 eV below. (b) n = 1.5 × 1010 × e0.3/0.0259 = 1.6 × 1015 cm−3. (c) n → ni: EF returns to mid-gap; the sample behaves as intrinsic.

Self-test.

  1. 0 K: a step from 1 to 0 at EF. 300 K: rounded over a few kT. Both pass through ½ at EF.
  2. 1 − f = 1 − 1/(1 + e−7.72) = 4.4 × 10−4.
  3. NC is the number of seats per cm3 that, all placed at EC, would hold as many electrons as the whole conduction band. It lets n be written as seats × the chance a seat at EC is filled.
  4. Step 9, lines 1–5; approximation NC ≈ NV.
  5. Step 8: the exponents add to −(EC − EV)/kT = −Eg/kT.
  6. 0.0259 × ln(1015/1.5 × 1010) = 0.0259 × 11.1 = 0.288 eV.
  7. n = 2.8 × 1019 × e−0.206/0.0259 = 1016 cm−3, so ND ≈ 1016 (step 10).
  8. ni rises steeply with T; once ni ≫ NA, p ≈ n ≈ ni, the sample behaves as intrinsic and EF → Ei.

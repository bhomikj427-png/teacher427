The MOSFET

The JFET controls its channel with a reverse-biased junction. The MOSFET replaces that junction with a metal plate on a thin layer of glass. The idea is the same (a gate voltage sets the channel current), the equations are almost the same, and it is the transistor inside every digital chip.

D1 · Structure and types

Practice · MOSFET deck slides 4–18 With VGS = 0, an enhancement MOSFET carries no drain current but a depletion MOSFET does. Explain why.
Guess first: what would have to exist between source and drain for current to flow?
Oxide: a very thin insulating layer of SiO₂ under the gate. · Substrate (body): the p-type silicon the device is built in, normally tied to the source. · n⁺: heavily doped n-type.
{{ mos_struct() }}

Structure (n-channel enhancement, "nMOS"). Two n⁺ regions, source and drain, sit in a p-type substrate. Over the gap between them lies the oxide, and on top of it the gate electrode. Metal–Oxide–Semiconductor gives the name. The gate, oxide and substrate form a capacitor.

Operation. With VGS = 0 the path from drain to source is n–p–n: two diodes back to back, so no current. A positive gate voltage pushes holes away from the surface and attracts electrons. When VGS reaches the threshold voltage VT (also written VGS(th)), enough electrons gather under the oxide to form an n-type inversion layer: the channel. More gate voltage means more electrons and more current. The channel is enhanced by the gate, hence "enhancement type".

p-channel (pMOS): n-type substrate, p⁺ source and drain, holes carry the current, and every voltage is reversed (VT is negative; it turns on when VGS < VT).

Compared with a JFET: the gate is insulated, so IG is practically zero and the input resistance is even higher (10¹⁰–10¹⁵ Ω). The thin oxide is easily destroyed by static charge, so MOSFETs need careful handling.

Answer. Current needs a continuous channel of one type between source and drain. An enhancement MOSFET has none at VGS = 0, only two back-to-back junctions, so it stays off until VGS exceeds VT and induces a channel. A depletion MOSFET is made with a channel already in place, so ID = IDSS flows at VGS = 0.

The region test is the first step of every MOSFET problem. VGS − VT is called the overdrive voltage. In saturation the channel is pinched off at the drain end, exactly as in the JFET, so ID no longer depends on VDS.

The constant K (A/V²) is found from one data-sheet point: K = ID(on)/(VGS(on) − VT)². In terms of the device, K = ½μnCox(W/L): carrier mobility, oxide capacitance per unit area, and the width-to-length ratio of the channel. Sedra & Smith write kn = μnCox(W/L) = 2K, so their saturation equation reads ID = ½kn(VGS − VT)². Same physics, different letter.

Channel-length modulation. The real curves tilt up slightly in saturation: ID = K(VGS − VT)²(1 + λVDS). This gives the output resistance ro = 1/(λID).

Answer. K = 500 mA/(10 − 1)² = 500/81 = 6.17 mA/V². At VGS = 5 V: ID = 6.17 × (5 − 1)² = 98.8 mA (the sheet prints 98.7). Halving VGS cut the current by five, because the law is a square of (VGS − VT).

D5 · The MOSFET as a switch and CMOS logic gates

Professor's deck "MOSFET biasing and digital circuit" · slide 9 · "Complex Logic Gates in CMOS: Example" Implement f = (a·(b + c))′ as a single CMOS gate. How many transistors does it need?
Guess first: sketch the nMOS part, the network that must connect the output to ground when a·(b + c) = 1.
Logic 1 / 0: VDD / 0 V. · Pull-up network: transistors between VDD and the output. · Pull-down network: transistors between the output and ground.

Switch. Drive the gate fully on or fully off and the MOSFET is a switch: cutoff = open; triode with a large VGS = closed, with a small on-resistance rDS(on) ≈ 1/[2K(VGS − VT)]. It needs no gate current to stay on, which is why it suits digital circuits.

nMOS and pMOS are complementary switches. An nMOS closes when its gate is 1; a pMOS closes when its gate is 0. An nMOS passes a strong 0 but a weak 1 (the output stops at VDD − VT); a pMOS passes a strong 1 but a weak 0. So CMOS uses pMOS to pull up to VDD and nMOS to pull down to ground.

{{ cmos_inv() }}
{{ cmos_vtc() }}

CMOS inverter. One pMOS on top, one nMOS below, gates joined. Input 0: pMOS on, nMOS off, output = VDD. Input 1: nMOS on, pMOS off, output = 0. In either steady state one transistor is off, so no current flows from VDD to ground: static power is nearly zero. Power is used only while switching. The output swings the full 0 to VDD.

{{ cmos_gates() }}

Rules for any CMOS gate. The pull-down network of nMOS implements the function before the final inversion: transistors in series = AND, in parallel = OR. The pull-up network of pMOS is its dual: every series becomes parallel and every parallel becomes series. Each input drives one nMOS and one pMOS, so a gate with n inputs uses 2n transistors. A CMOS gate always inverts: NAND and NOR are natural, AND needs a NAND followed by an inverter.

Answer. Pull-down (nMOS): a in series with (b parallel c). Pull-up (pMOS), the dual: a in parallel with (b in series with c). Three inputs, so 6 transistors: 3 nMOS + 3 pMOS (right-hand circuit above). For every one of the 8 input combinations exactly one network conducts.

D6 · FinFET

Practice · from the two FinFET decks What problems appear when a planar MOSFET is scaled down, and how does a FinFET overcome them?
Guess first: when the channel gets very short, what else besides the gate can influence it?
{{ finfet_fig() }}

The problem: short-channel effects. As the channel length shrinks to tens of nanometres, the drain sits so close to the source that the drain's field reaches into the channel and the gate loses control. The results: the threshold voltage shifts (falls) with channel length and drain voltage; the subthreshold slope degrades, so the device no longer turns off sharply; the off-state leakage current IOFF rises. Standby power goes up and performance stops improving.

The fix: wrap the gate around the channel. In a FinFET (Fin Field-Effect Transistor) the channel is a thin vertical fin of silicon standing on the substrate, and the gate covers it on three sides. Current flows along the two side walls and the top of the fin. With the gate on three sides, it controls the whole thin body, so the drain cannot take over.

Types: shorted-gate (SG), where the gates on both sides are joined and act as one, and independent-gate (IG), where the two side gates are driven separately so one can adjust the threshold. Fins must be spaced correctly: too close and neighbouring fins interfere, too far and area is wasted.

Answer. Scaling a planar MOSFET brings short-channel effects: threshold shift, a worse subthreshold slope and a higher off current, because the drain starts to control the channel. A FinFET raises the channel into a thin fin and wraps the gate around three sides. The gate regains control, so leakage falls, switching is faster and scaling can continue.

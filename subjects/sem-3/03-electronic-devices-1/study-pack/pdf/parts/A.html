A1 · Energy bands, electrons and holes

Practice · built on slides 19–24 of the Unit 1 deck Using energy bands, explain why heating lowers the resistance of a semiconductor but raises the resistance of a metal.
Guess first, on paper.
Valence electron: an outer electron that forms bonds. Si has 4. · Covalent bond: two neighbouring atoms share a pair of valence electrons. · eV: the energy one electron gains crossing 1 volt, 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
{{ bands() }}

Bands. In a crystal the allowed electron energies form bands. The valence band holds the electrons locked in bonds. The conduction band holds electrons that are free to move. Between them is the band gap Eg, where no states exist.

Electron–hole pairs. At 0 K every Si bond is full and silicon is an insulator. At room temperature, heat breaks a few bonds. Each broken bond gives one free electron (in the conduction band) and leaves one hole, a missing electron that behaves as a mobile charge of +q. Pure silicon therefore always has equal numbers: n = p = ni, the intrinsic concentration.

Size of ni. About 10¹⁰ per cm³ in Si at 300 K, against 5 × 10²² atoms per cm³, so only one bond in 10¹² is broken. ni rises steeply with temperature: ni² ∝ T³·e−Eg/kT. In Si it roughly doubles every 10 °C (slide 37). A smaller gap means a larger ni, so Ge (0.67 eV) is leakier than Si (1.12 eV).

Answer. Semiconductor: heating breaks more bonds, so the number of carriers rises exponentially. That outweighs the extra scattering, so resistance falls (negative temperature coefficient). Metal: the bands overlap, so the number of free electrons is fixed. Heat only makes the atoms vibrate more, which scatters the electrons more, so resistance rises (positive temperature coefficient).

A2 · Doping and the mass-action law

Practice · numbers follow the slides' ni = 10¹⁰ cm⁻³ Silicon is doped with ND = 10¹⁶ donor atoms per cm³. At 300 K, find the electron and hole concentrations.
Guess first: will there be more holes or fewer holes than in pure silicon?
Doping: adding a tiny, controlled amount of another element. · Donor: an atom with 5 valence electrons (P, As, Sb); the fifth is given away as a free electron. · Acceptor: an atom with 3 valence electrons (B, Al, Ga); the missing bond is a hole.
{{ doping_bands() }}

Extrinsic silicon. Doped silicon is called extrinsic. Donors make it n-type: electrons are the majority carriers and holes the minority. Acceptors make it p-type, with the roles swapped. At room temperature every dopant atom is ionised, so

n-type: n ≈ ND   ·   p-type: p ≈ NA

Mass-action law. In equilibrium the product of the two concentrations is fixed by temperature alone, doped or not:

n · p = ni²

Adding electrons makes holes rarer, because a hole now meets an electron and recombines sooner. Charge neutrality still holds: the crystal is neutral because each free electron left behind a fixed positive donor ion (n + NA = p + ND). If both dopants are present, the larger one wins: n ≈ ND − NA (a compensated semiconductor).

Answer. n ≈ ND = 10¹⁶ cm⁻³. p = ni²/n = (10¹⁰)²/10¹⁶ = 10⁴ cm⁻³. Holes dropped from 10¹⁰ to 10⁴. With ni = 1.5 × 10¹⁰ the same steps give p = 2.25 × 10⁴ cm⁻³.

A3 · The Fermi level

Practice · derivation on slide 38 of the Unit 1 deck Show that the Fermi level of an intrinsic semiconductor lies at the middle of the forbidden gap.
Guess first: which way does EF move when donors are added?
State: a slot an electron may occupy at a given energy. · f(E): the probability that a state at energy E is occupied. · NC, NV: effective number of states at the conduction-band and valence-band edges.
{{ fermi_plot() }}

Fermi–Dirac function. Electrons fill states from the bottom up. The probability that a state at energy E is filled is

f(E) = 1 / (1 + e(E − EF)/kT)

EF, the Fermi level, is the energy where f = ½. Well above EF states are almost empty; well below, almost full. Counting the filled conduction-band states and the empty valence-band states gives

n = NC e−(EC − EF)/kT     p = NV e−(EF − EV)/kT

So EF is a gauge. Close to EC means many electrons (n-type). Close to EV means many holes (p-type). For n-type material, EF − Ei = kT ln(ND/ni), where Ei is the intrinsic (mid-gap) level. At very high temperature ni catches up with the doping and EF drifts back to mid-gap.

Answer. Intrinsic means n = p: NC e−(EC − EF)/kT = NV e−(EF − EV)/kT. Take NC = NV. Then the exponents are equal: EC − EF = EF − EV, so EF = (EC + EV)/2. Without that assumption an extra term appears, EF = (EC + EV)/2 + (kT/2) ln(NV/NC), which is only a few meV.
Check A3. (a) What is f(EF) at 300 K and at 600 K? (b) For ND = 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ and ni = 10¹⁰, how far is EF above mid-gap? (c) Find f(E) for a state 0.1 eV above EF at 300 K.

A4 · Drift, mobility, conductivity, sheet resistance

Professor's Unit 1 deck · slide 51 · Example (a) Calculate the conductivity and resistivity of intrinsic silicon at T = 300 K. (b) How would σ change if the temperature increases by 10 °C? Given: μn = 1500 cm²/V-s, μp = 450 cm²/V-s at 300 K.
Guess first: bigger or smaller than 1 Ω·cm?
Electric field E: volts per cm; the force per unit charge. · Current density J: current per unit cross-section area, A/cm². · Scattering: a carrier colliding with a vibrating atom or an ion and losing its direction.
{{ drift_diff() }}

Drift. A field accelerates carriers, and scattering keeps stopping them, so they settle at an average drift velocity vd = μE. Mobility μ (cm²/V·s) measures how easily a carrier moves. Electrons are faster than holes (Si: μn ≈ 1350–1500, μp ≈ 450–480). Mobility falls when temperature or doping rises, because both add scattering. At very high fields vd stops rising (velocity saturation, about 10⁷ cm/s in Si).

Both carriers add to the current, since holes move with the field and electrons against it but carry opposite charge:

Jdrift = q(nμn + pμp)E = σE     σ = q(nμn + pμp)     ρ = 1/σ

Answer. (a) n = p = ni = 10¹⁰. σ = q ni(μn + μp) = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 10¹⁰ × 1950 = 3.12 × 10⁻⁶ (Ω·cm)⁻¹, so ρ = 1/σ = 3.2 × 10⁵ Ω·cm. (b) ni doubles for a 10 °C rise while the mobilities barely change, so σ doubles. Slide 52 then dopes the same silicon with ND = 5 × 10¹⁷ cm⁻³: σ = 32 (Ω·cm)⁻¹, ten million times larger. One donor per 10⁵ atoms does that.
{{ sheet_res() }}

Sheet resistance. IC resistors are thin doped layers of fixed thickness t. Group ρ/t into one number, the sheet resistance Rs = ρ/t, in ohms per square (Ω/□). Then R = Rs × L/W. A square of any size has resistance Rs; a strip 10 squares long has 10 Rs.

Check A4. n-type Si has ND = 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ and μn = 1350 cm²/V·s. Find (a) σ and ρ, (b) the drift velocity in a field of 100 V/cm, (c) the sheet resistance of a layer 1 μm thick, (d) the resistance of a strip with L/W = 10.

A5 · Diffusion, the Einstein relation, total current

Practice In a silicon bar the electron concentration falls by 10¹⁸ cm⁻³ over every cm of length. Take μn = 1350 cm²/V·s at 300 K. Find the diffusion coefficient and the electron diffusion current density.
Guess first: no voltage is applied. Can a current still flow?

Diffusion. Carriers move randomly because of heat. If one side is more crowded, more carriers wander out of it than into it, so there is a net flow from high concentration to low. No field is needed. The flow is proportional to the slope of the concentration (the gradient, dn/dx), and the constant is the diffusion coefficient D (cm²/s):

Jn,diff = +qDn dn/dx     Jp,diff = −qDp dp/dx

The signs differ because electrons carry −q: electrons moving in +x make a current in −x.

Einstein relation. Drift and diffusion are both limited by the same scattering, so D and μ are tied together:

Dn/μn = Dp/μp = kT/q = VT ≈ 26 mV at 300 K

Total current. Each carrier can drift and diffuse at once:

Jn = qnμnE + qDn dn/dx     Jp = qpμpE − qDp dp/dx     J = Jn + Jp

In equilibrium Jn = 0 and Jp = 0 separately: wherever a gradient exists, a built-in field sets up a drift that cancels the diffusion exactly. That balance is what happens inside a pn junction (Part B).

Answer. Dn = μnVT = 1350 × 0.0259 = 34.9 cm²/s. |J| = qDn|dn/dx| = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 34.9 × 10¹⁸ = 5.59 A/cm². Electrons flow toward the emptier end; the current points the other way.

A6 · Generation, recombination, continuity and Poisson equations

Practice · syllabus line "Generation and recombination of carriers; Poisson and continuity equation" Write the continuity equation for electrons and state what each term means.
Guess first: list every way the number of electrons in a small box can change.
{{ gr_fig() }}

Generation (G) creates an electron–hole pair: heat or light lifts an electron across the gap. Recombination (R) destroys a pair: an electron drops into a hole. In equilibrium G = R, which is what holds n·p at ni². Direct (band-to-band) recombination releases light and dominates in GaAs, which is why LEDs use it. In Si and Ge the electron usually drops in two steps through a trap, an impurity or defect level near mid-gap (indirect, or Shockley–Read–Hall, recombination), and the energy becomes heat.

Lifetime τ. Extra (excess) minority carriers Δp do not vanish at once. They recombine at a rate Δp/τ, so they decay as Δp(t) = Δp(0)e−t/τ. τ is the minority-carrier lifetime. It sets how fast a diode can switch off (B3).

Continuity equation. It is bookkeeping for a thin slice of material: carriers inside change only because more flow in than out, or because of net recombination.

∂n/∂t = (1/q) ∂Jn/∂x − (R − G)      ∂p/∂t = −(1/q) ∂Jp/∂x − (R − G)

Poisson equation. It links charge to field: wherever there is net charge, the field changes with position.

dE/dx = ρ/ε = (q/ε)(p − n + ND − NA)     and     E = −dV/dx

ρ here is charge density (C/cm³) and ε the permittivity of silicon (11.7 ε0). In neutral material ρ = 0 and the field is constant. In a depletion region ρ ≠ 0, so the field ramps. B1 uses exactly this.

Answer. ∂n/∂t = (1/q) ∂Jn/∂x − (R − G). Left side: the rate of change of electron concentration at a point. First term: the net flow of electrons into the slice (current in minus current out). Second term: the net loss by recombination over generation. For holes the flow term changes sign because the charge is +q.

Unit 7 › Principle

G1 · The oscillator principle and the Barkhausen criterion

Practice · syllabus line "Review of the basic concept, Barkhausen criterion" State the Barkhausen criterion for sustained oscillations.
Guess first: in Af = A/(1 − βA), what value of βA makes the gain infinite?
Oscillator: a circuit that produces a repetitive output signal with no input signal; it converts DC power into AC. · Positive feedback: the returned signal is in phase with the input and adds to it.
{{ osc_block() }}

From amplifier to oscillator. With positive feedback the signs in F1 flip: Af = A/(1 − βA). When βA = 1 the denominator is zero and the gain is infinite, which means a finite output with zero input. Follow a signal once round the loop: it is amplified by A, scaled by β, and arrives back where it started. If it arrives with the same size and the same phase, it sustains itself for ever.

Answer: the Barkhausen criterion. A circuit gives sustained sinusoidal oscillations at the frequency where both of these hold:
  1. the magnitude of the loop gain is unity: |βA| = 1;
  2. the total phase shift round the loop is 0° or 360°, so the feedback is positive.
If |βA| < 1 the oscillations die away. If |βA| > 1 they grow until the amplifier clips.

Starting up. No input is needed: the random noise present in every circuit contains all frequencies. The feedback network is frequency-selective, so only one frequency meets the phase condition; that component is amplified round and round. In practice |βA| is made slightly greater than 1 so the oscillation builds up, and the amplifier's own non-linearity then brings it back to exactly 1.

Unit 7 › RC oscillators

G2 · The RC phase-shift oscillator

Professor's oscillator deck · design example Design a phase-shift oscillator for a frequency of 800 Hz. The capacitors are to be 10 nF.
Guess first: one RC section can shift the phase by less than 90°. How many sections are needed for 180°?
{{ phase_shift_osc() }}

How it works. The amplifier is inverting, so it supplies 180°. The feedback network is three identical RC sections in a chain. Each section shifts the phase by less than 90°, so at least three are needed to reach 180°. The three together give exactly 180° at only one frequency, and the total round the loop is then 360°:

f0 = 1 / (2πRC√6)      β = 1/29 at f0      so the amplifier gain must be |A| ≥ 29

At f0 the network passes only 1/29 of its input, so the amplifier must make up a factor of 29 for |βA| = 1. With an op-amp inverting amplifier of gain Rf/R, this means Rf = 29R. The third R of the network is the op-amp's own input resistor, since the inverting input is a virtual ground.

Transistor versions. With a FET amplifier the same f0 holds and gmRL must be at least 29. With a BJT the low input resistance loads the network: f0 = 1/(2πRC√(6 + 4RC/R)) and hfe ≥ 23 + 29(R/RC) + 4(RC/R).

Good for fixed audio frequencies. To change the frequency all three R or all three C must be changed together, so it is not used where tuning is needed.

Answer. R = 1/(2πf0C√6) = 1/(2π × 800 × 10 × 10⁻⁹ × 2.449) = 8.12 kΩ. Use the standard value 8.2 kΩ. Then Rf = 29R = 29 × 8.2 kΩ = 238 kΩ (the deck's value). With 8.2 kΩ the actual frequency is 792 Hz.

G3 · The Wien-bridge oscillator

Practice · oscillator deck, Wien-bridge slides A Wien-bridge oscillator uses R = 10 kΩ and C = 10 nF. Find the frequency of oscillation. If R1 = 10 kΩ, what should R2 be?
Guess first: this amplifier is non-inverting. How much phase shift may the feedback network add?
{{ wien_osc() }}

How it works. The feedback network is a lead-lag circuit: a series RC arm from the output to the + input, and a parallel RC arm from the + input to ground. At low frequency the series capacitor blocks the signal; at high frequency the parallel capacitor shorts it. In between, at one frequency, the output of the network is largest and exactly in phase with its input:

f0 = 1 / (2πRC)      β = 1/3 at f0, phase shift 0°

The network gives 0°, so the amplifier must be non-inverting, with gain exactly 3 to make |βA| = 1. The non-inverting gain is 1 + R2/R1, so

1 + R2/R1 = 3   ⟹   R2 = 2R1

To start, R2/R1 is made slightly more than 2 (the deck says so). The two RC arms plus R1 and R2 form the four arms of a bridge, hence the name. It gives a clean sine wave, and the frequency is tuned easily by varying the two capacitors (or the two resistors) together. It is the standard audio-frequency oscillator.

Answer. f0 = 1/(2π × 10 × 10³ × 10 × 10⁻⁹) = 1.59 kHz. R2 = 2R1 = 20 kΩ (slightly more, to start).

G4 · LC oscillators: Hartley and Colpitts

Practice · syllabus line "RC oscillators & LC oscillators" A Colpitts oscillator has C1 = C2 = 200 pF and L = 1 mH. Find the frequency of oscillation.
Guess first: the two capacitors are in series round the tank. Is their combined value more or less than 200 pF?
Tank circuit: an inductor and a capacitor connected in a loop. Energy swings back and forth between them at the resonant frequency f0 = 1/(2π√(LC)).
{{ lc_tanks() }}

Principle. A tank on its own would ring at f0 and die away because of its resistance. The amplifier feeds in just enough energy each cycle to replace the loss. Part of the tank voltage is tapped off and returned to the amplifier input. With the tap grounded, the two ends of the tank are 180° apart, which with an inverting amplifier makes the loop total 360°. The two classic circuits differ only in where the tap is.

To remember which is which: Colpitts splits the Capacitor. In both, the amplifier gain must at least make up for the tap ratio so that |βA| = 1.

Answer. Ceq = (200 × 200)/(200 + 200) = 100 pF. f0 = 1/(2π√(1 × 10⁻³ × 100 × 10⁻¹²)) = 503 kHz.

Unit 7 › Crystal oscillators

G5 · The crystal oscillator

Practice · deck "crystal" (3 slides) A quartz crystal has Ls = 1 H, Cs = 0.01 pF, Rs = 1 kΩ and Cp = 10 pF. Find its series resonant frequency, how far above it the parallel resonance lies, and its Q.
Guess first: an ordinary LC tank has a Q of about 100. Will the crystal's be similar?
Piezoelectric effect: squeezing a quartz crystal produces a voltage across it. Inverse piezoelectric effect: applying a voltage makes it change shape. · Q (quality factor): how sharply a resonant circuit selects one frequency; Q = ωsL/R.
{{ crystal_fig() }}

How it works. A thin slice of quartz is mounted between two metal plates. An alternating voltage makes it vibrate (inverse piezoelectric effect), and it vibrates strongly only at its natural mechanical frequency, which is fixed by its cut and thickness. The vibration in turn produces a voltage. Electrically the crystal therefore behaves as a resonant circuit: a series branch Rs–Ls–Cs (a low resistance, a very large inductance and a very small capacitance, representing the vibrating quartz), in parallel with Cp, the capacitance of the plates and holder.

series resonance: fs = 1/(2π√(LsCs))      parallel resonance: fp = fs√(1 + Cs/Cp)

At fs the branch impedance is lowest (just Rs). At fp, slightly higher, the impedance is very large. Because Cs ≪ Cp the two are extremely close, and between them the crystal looks inductive. In an oscillator the crystal replaces the tank (or the inductor of a Colpitts circuit).

Why it is used. The Q of a crystal is 10⁴ to 10⁶, against about 100 for an LC tank, so the frequency hardly moves with temperature, supply voltage or ageing. Crystal oscillators set the clock of watches, computers and transmitters. The frequency is fixed by the crystal and cannot be tuned over a range.

Answer. fs = 1/(2π√(1 × 0.01 × 10⁻¹²)) = 1.592 MHz. fp = fs√(1 + 0.01/10) = fs × 1.0005, which is 795 Hz above fs. Q = 2πfsLs/Rs = (2π × 1.592 × 10⁶ × 1)/1000 = 10 000.

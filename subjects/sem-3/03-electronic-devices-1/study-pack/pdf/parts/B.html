B1 · Junction formation, depletion region, built-in voltage

Professor's Unit 1–2 deck · slides 77–78 · Examples For an abrupt silicon pn junction, the built-in voltage is Vbi = 0.85 V. Let W0 and W1 denote the depletion widths for Va = 0 V and Va = 0.6 V, respectively. What is W1/W0? Repeat for a reverse bias of 2 V.
Guess first: does forward bias make the depletion region wider or narrower?
Abrupt junction: the doping switches from p to n in one step. · Ion: a dopant atom that has lost its carrier; it is charged but fixed in the crystal. · Bias: a DC voltage applied across the device.
{{ junction() }}

Formation. At the moment of contact, holes diffuse from p to n and electrons from n to p (A5), and they recombine. They leave behind fixed ions: negative acceptor ions on the p side, positive donor ions on the n side. This strip with no free carriers is the depletion region (or space-charge region). The ions create a field pointing from n to p that pushes carriers back. Diffusion stops when this drift exactly cancels it. The voltage across the region at that balance is the built-in voltage:

Vbi = VT ln(NAND / ni²)

Vbi is about 0.6–0.9 V in Si. A voltmeter reads zero across an unconnected diode, because the metal contacts add equal and opposite steps.

Width. Solving Poisson's equation (A6) in the depletion region gives a triangular field and

W = √[ (2ε/q)(1/NA + 1/ND)(Vbi − Va) ]     NAxp = NDxn

Va is the applied voltage, positive for forward bias. The region reaches further into the lightly doped side, since both sides must hold equal charge.

Bias. Forward bias (p positive) lowers the barrier to Vbi − Va: the region narrows and majority carriers pour across. Reverse bias (p negative) raises the barrier: the region widens and only a tiny current of thermally generated minority carriers flows.

Answer. W ∝ √(Vbi − Va). Forward 0.6 V: W1/W0 = √(0.25/0.85) = 0.54, the region shrinks. Reverse 2 V (Va = −2): W2/W0 = √(2.85/0.85) = 1.83, it expands.

B2 · The diode I–V characteristic

Practice · equation from slides 79–83 A diode has Is = 1 pA and η = 1 at 300 K. Find its current at VD = 0.6 V. What happens to the current if VD rises by 60 mV?
Guess first.
{{ diode_iv() }}

Shockley equation. Lowering the barrier lets exponentially more carriers cross, so

ID = Is(eVD/ηVT − 1)     VT = kT/q = T/11600 ≈ 26 mV at 300 K

SymbolMeaning
IsReverse saturation current. The small current of minority carriers in reverse bias: nA for Si, μA for Ge. Proportional to ni².
ηIdeality factor, between 1 and 2. The slides use η = 1 for Ge and 2 for Si. Take η = 1 unless told otherwise.
Knee (cut-in) voltageWhere the current becomes noticeable: about 0.7 V for Si, 0.3 V for Ge, 1.2 V for GaAs.

Three regions. Forward (VD ≫ VT): the −1 is negligible and ID ≈ IseVD/ηVT. Reverse: the exponential dies and ID ≈ −Is, flat. Breakdown: at a large reverse voltage the current rises sharply (B8).

Temperature. Is about doubles for every 10 °C rise: Is2 = Is1 · 2(T₂ − T₁)/10 (slide 81). At a fixed current the forward voltage falls about 2 mV per °C, so the whole forward curve shifts left when the diode heats up.

Answer. ID = 10⁻¹² × (e0.6/0.0259 − 1) = 10⁻¹² × 1.2 × 10¹⁰ = 12 mA. Adding 60 mV multiplies the current by e0.06/0.0259 ≈ 10. Every 60 mV is a factor of ten, which is why the curve looks like a wall at 0.7 V.

B3 · Diode resistances, equivalent circuits, capacitances, switching

Practice · definitions from slides 84–100 A diode carries 2 mA at 0.7 V. Find its static (DC) resistance and its dynamic (AC) resistance at that point. Take η = 1.
Guess first: which of the two is larger?
Q-point: the DC operating point (VD, ID) where the diode sits with no signal. · Small signal: an AC wiggle small enough that the curve looks straight around the Q-point.

Static resistance is just the ratio at the point: RD = VD/ID. It is large in reverse and at the knee, small higher up.

Dynamic resistance is the slope seen by a small AC signal riding on the DC point. Differentiating the Shockley equation:

rd = dVD/dID = ηVT/ID ≈ 26 mV / ID

Average AC resistance rav = ΔVD/ΔID between two points is used for large swings.

{{ diode_models() }}

Equivalent circuits. Replace the curve with straight lines. The simplified model (a closed switch in series with 0.7 V when on, an open switch when off) is the one used in every circuit question unless the question says "ideal".

CapacitanceWhenFormulaCause
Transition (depletion) CTreverse biasCT = εA/WThe depletion region is an insulator between two conducting plates. More reverse voltage → wider W → smaller CT. A varactor uses this.
Diffusion (storage) CDforward biasCD = τID/ηVTInjected minority carriers are stored near the junction; the stored charge grows with current. Much larger than CT.

Switching. A conducting diode cannot turn off at once. When the voltage reverses, the stored minority carriers keep a large reverse current flowing for the storage time ts, then it decays over the transition time tt. Their sum is the reverse recovery time trr = ts + tt, from nanoseconds to a few microseconds. It limits the highest usable frequency.

Answer. RD = 0.7 V / 2 mA = 350 Ω. rd = 26 mV / 2 mA = 13 Ω. The curve is steep at that point, so a small change in voltage gives a large change in current.

B4 · Load line and DC diode circuits

Professor's deck L08 · slide 4 · Example For the given diode configuration and diode characteristics, determine: VDQ, IDQ and VR. (E = 10 V, R = 0.5 kΩ, silicon diode.)
Guess first: roughly how much of the 10 V lands on the resistor?

{{ load_line() }}

Load line. The diode and the resistor carry the same current, and KVL gives E = VD + IDR. Rearranged, ID = (E − VD)/R is a straight line on the diode's own axes. Find its two ends: VD = 0 gives ID = E/R; ID = 0 gives VD = E. The circuit must sit on the line and on the diode curve at once, so the answer is where they cross: the Q-point.

Answer. Ends: E/R = 10/0.5 k = 20 mA and E = 10 V. The crossing reads VDQ ≈ 0.78 V, IDQ ≈ 18.5 mA. VR = IDQR = 18.5 mA × 0.5 kΩ = 9.25 V (check: E − VDQ = 9.22 V, the same within reading error).
Slip in the slide. Slide 5 writes VR = (18.5 mA)(1 K) = 18.5 V. R is 0.5 kΩ, and 18.5 V cannot come out of a 10 V source. Use 9.25 V.

Without the graph: the three-step method. Used for every other circuit on these slides.

  1. Decide the state. Mentally remove the diode. If the voltage that appears across the gap would push current in the direction of the arrow and is more than 0.7 V (Si), the diode is ON. Otherwise it is OFF.
  2. Substitute. ON → a 0.7 V drop (0.3 V for Ge, 0 for ideal). OFF → an open circuit, I = 0.
  3. Solve with KVL and Ohm's law.
{{ ckt_two_source() }}

Worked (deck L08, slide 14): determine I, V1, V2 and Vo. The sources total 15 V around the loop and push current along the arrow, so the diode is ON. I = (10 + 5 − 0.7)/(4.7 k + 2.2 k) = 2.07 mA. V1 = IR1 = 9.74 V. V2 = IR2 = 4.56 V. Vo = V2 + E2 = 4.56 − 5 = −0.44 V.

Rules for combinations. Diodes in series: the drops add (Si + red LED = 0.7 + 1.8 V). A reversed diode anywhere in a series path opens the path. Diodes in parallel: the voltage across the pair is one drop, 0.7 V, and identical diodes share the current equally.

B5 · Rectifiers

Professor's deck L09 · slide 9 · Example Sketch dc output vo and determine the dc level of the output. Repeat if the ideal diode is replaced by silicon diode. (Input: sine wave of 20 V peak. The diode points toward the source, with R = 2 kΩ across the output.)
Guess first: which half-cycle reaches the output, and with what sign?
Rectifier: a circuit that turns AC into one-direction (pulsating DC) voltage. · Vm: the peak of the input sine. Vrms = Vm/√2. · Vdc: the average of the output over one period, which is what a DC voltmeter reads.
{{ ckt_hw() }}
{{ rect_waves() }}

Half-wave. One diode. It conducts on one half-cycle and blocks the other. Averaging one half-sine over a full period: Vdc = Vm/π = 0.318 Vm. With a real Si diode the peak is lower: Vdc ≈ 0.318(Vm − 0.7).

PIV (peak inverse voltage) is the largest reverse voltage the diode must survive while blocking. Its rating must exceed this. Half-wave: PIV = Vm.

{{ bridge_fig() }}{{ ckt_ct() }}

Full-wave bridge (left). Four diodes. On the positive half D1 and D3 conduct; on the negative half D2 and D4. Either way, current goes through R in the same direction, so both halves are used: Vdc = 2Vm/π = 0.636 Vm. Two diodes are in the path, so with Si diodes Vdc ≈ 0.636(Vm − 1.4).

Centre-tapped full-wave (right). Two diodes and a transformer whose secondary has a middle tap. Each half of the secondary feeds R through its own diode on alternate half-cycles. Vm here is the peak from the tap to one end. The off diode sees both halves, so PIV = 2Vm.

Ripple factor measures how much AC is left in the output: r = (rms of the AC part)/Vdc = √[(Vrms/Vdc)² − 1]. Smaller is better. Efficiency is DC power delivered over AC power drawn: η = (Vdc/Vrms)² for ideal diodes. A capacitor across R (a filter) holds the voltage up between peaks and cuts the ripple.

Answer. The diode points toward the source, so it conducts only when vi is negative: the output is the negative half-cycles. Ideal: Vdc = −0.318 × 20 = −6.36 V. Silicon: Vdc = −0.318 × (20 − 0.7) = −6.14 V. PIV = 20 V.

B6 · Clippers

Professor's deck L10 · slide 3 · Example Determine the output waveform for the network. (Series clipper: a 5 V battery, then an ideal diode, then R. Input: sine wave of 20 V peak.)
Guess first: sketch vo for one cycle.
Clipper (limiter): a circuit that removes part of a waveform and passes the rest unchanged. · Transition voltage: the input value at which the diode changes state.
{{ clip_series() }}

Series clipper: the diode is in series with the output, so the output exists only while the diode conducts. A battery in series shifts the level at which it starts.

  1. Find the transition voltage. Set the ideal diode at the edge of conduction (id = 0, vd = 0) and write KVL. Here vi + 5 = 0, so the diode switches at vi = −5 V.
  2. Diode ON (vi > −5 V): KVL gives vo = vi + 5.
  3. Diode OFF (vi < −5 V): no current, so vo = 0.
Answer. The output is the input lifted by 5 V while vi > −5 V, and zero otherwise. Its peak is 20 + 5 = 25 V.
{{ clip_parallel() }}

Parallel clipper: the diode is across the output. While it is OFF the output simply follows the input. While it is ON it pins the output to the battery level. Worked (deck L10, slide 6): triangular input of 16 V peak, ideal diode with its anode on a 4 V battery. The diode conducts whenever vi < 4 V and holds vo = 4 V. Above 4 V it is off and vo = vi. With a silicon diode (slide 7) the pinned level becomes 4 − 0.7 = 3.3 V.

Two such branches facing opposite ways clip both peaks, which squares up a sine wave.

B7 · Clampers and voltage multipliers

Professor's deck L10 · slide 13 · Example Determine vo for the network. (Square wave input, +10 V and −20 V, f = 1000 Hz. C = 0.1 μF, R = 100 kΩ, ideal diode in series with a 5 V battery.)
Guess first: the input swings 30 V in total. What total swing will the output have?
Clamper: a capacitor, a diode and a resistor that shift a whole waveform up or down without changing its shape. · Time constant τ = RC: a capacitor needs about 5τ to charge or discharge fully.
{{ clamper() }}

How it works. On one half-cycle the diode conducts and charges C quickly to a fixed voltage. On the other half-cycle the diode is off, and C can only discharge through the large R. If 5τ is much longer than the half-period, C holds its voltage and acts like a battery in series with the input. That battery is the shift.

  1. Start with the half-cycle that turns the diode ON. Here that is vi = −20 V. With the diode a short, the output is pinned to the battery: vo = 5 V.
  2. Find VC from KVL in that state: −20 + VC − 5 = 0, so VC = 25 V.
  3. Other half-cycle, diode OFF, C keeps 25 V: vo = vi + VC = 10 + 25 = 35 V.
  4. Check the swing. Output swing = input swing: 35 − 5 = 30 V. ✓
Answer. vo is a square wave between 5 V and 35 V. The hold condition is met: τ = RC = 100 kΩ × 0.1 μF = 10 ms, so 5τ = 50 ms, which is 100 times the half-period T/2 = 0.5 ms.
{{ doubler() }}

Voltage multipliers chain clampers and peak detectors to give a DC output of several times the input peak.

Half-wave doubler. Positive half: D1 conducts and charges C1 to Vm. Negative half: the source and C1 add in series, D2 conducts, and C2 charges to 2Vm (top plate negative). Each diode must stand PIV = 2Vm.

Adding more diode–capacitor sections gives a tripler (3Vm) and a quadrupler (4Vm). They suit high-voltage, low-current loads.

B8 · Breakdown, the Zener regulator, the Schottky diode

Practice · Zener regulator, fixed Vi and R In the regulator shown, Vi = 16 V, R = 1 kΩ, VZ = 10 V. Find VL, IL, IZ and PZ for (a) RL = 1.2 kΩ and (b) RL = 3 kΩ.
Guess first: in which case is the Zener actually regulating?
{{ zener_iv() }}
{{ zener_reg() }}

Breakdown is the sharp rise of reverse current at a certain reverse voltage. It does not destroy the diode if the current is limited. Two mechanisms:

Zener breakdownAvalanche breakdown
Dopingheavy on both sideslight
Depletion regionvery thin, so the field is enormouswide
Mechanismthe field pulls electrons straight out of their bonds (tunnelling)carriers gain enough speed to knock other electrons out of bonds; each new carrier does the same (multiplication)
Breakdown voltagebelow about 5 Vabove about 6 V
As temperature risesbreakdown voltage falls (negative coefficient)breakdown voltage rises (positive coefficient)

Zener diode. A diode built to work in breakdown. There its voltage stays almost fixed at VZ over a wide range of current, so it is used as a voltage reference.

  1. Test the state. Remove the Zener and find the voltage across RL: V = ViRL/(R + RL). If V ≥ VZ the Zener is ON. If not, it is OFF and acts as an open circuit.
  2. If ON: VL = VZ. IR = (Vi − VZ)/R. IL = VZ/RL. IZ = IR − IL. PZ = VZIZ, which must stay below the rating PZM.
Answer. (a) V = 16 × 1.2/2.2 = 8.73 V < 10 V, so the Zener is OFF: VL = 8.73 V, IZ = 0, PZ = 0. No regulation. (b) V = 16 × 3/4 = 12 V ≥ 10 V, so it is ON: VL = 10 V, IR = 6/1 k = 6 mA, IL = 10/3 k = 3.33 mA, IZ = 6 − 3.33 = 2.67 mA, PZ = 26.7 mW. The smallest load that keeps it on is RL,min = RVZ/(Vi − VZ) = 1.67 kΩ.

Schottky diode. A junction between a metal and n-type silicon instead of p and n. Only majority carriers (electrons) take part, so there is no stored minority charge and almost no reverse recovery time: it switches very fast. Its forward drop is low, about 0.2–0.4 V. The price is a larger reverse leakage and a lower breakdown voltage. Used in high-frequency rectifiers and fast logic.

DeviceBiasWhat happensKey facts
LEDforwardElectrons and holes recombine at the junction and each recombination releases a photon. Needs a direct-gap material (GaAs, GaAsP, GaN), not Si. The colour is set by the gap: λ(μm) = 1.24/Eg(eV). Forward drop 1.8–3.3 V. Always used with a series resistor.

Tunnel (Esaki) diodesmall forwardDoping is about 1000 times heavier than normal, so the depletion region is only about 10 nm thick. Electrons pass straight through the barrier (tunnelling). Shows negative resistance. Very fast. Used in microwave oscillators and fast switches.
{{ tunnel_iv() }}
Answer. As the forward voltage rises from zero, filled states on the n side line up with empty states on the p side and tunnel current rises to the peak Ip at Vp. Raising the voltage further pulls those states out of line, so fewer electrons can tunnel and the current falls to the valley Iv at Vv. Between peak and valley the current falls while the voltage rises, so dI/dV is negative: negative resistance. Beyond the valley, ordinary diode current takes over and rises again.

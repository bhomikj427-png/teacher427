Part E · Unit 5

The BJT and its amplifiers

The FETs of Parts C and D use one junction or an oxide to control a channel with a voltage. The bipolar junction transistor (BJT) is two pn junctions back to back, and a small current into the middle layer controls a large current through the device. The slides spend the most lectures on this unit: biasing, stability and h-parameters carry the numericals.

E1 · Construction and operation

Practice · decks L20–L21 Why is the base of a transistor made very thin and lightly doped, while the emitter is heavily doped?
Guess first: what should happen to electrons that the emitter sends into the base?
Emitter (E): the layer that supplies (emits) the carriers. · Base (B): the thin middle layer that controls them. · Collector (C): the layer that collects them.
{{ bjt_struct() }}

Construction. Three layers, n–p–n or p–n–p, so two junctions: emitter–base (EB) and collector–base (CB). It looks like two diodes back to back, but the base is so thin that the two junctions interact. "Bipolar" because both electrons and holes take part.

Operation (npn, active region). The EB junction is forward biased, so the emitter injects electrons into the base. The base is thin and has few holes, so only about 1% of those electrons recombine there; that needs a small base current IB. The other 99% diffuse across the base and are swept into the collector by the field of the reverse-biased CB junction. That is IC.

{{ bjt_symbols() }}

IE = IC + IB

The base current sets how much charge the base can pass, so a small IB controls a large IC: the BJT is a current-controlled device. For pnp, swap n and p, reverse all voltages and currents; holes are the main carriers.

RegionEB junctionCB junctionBehaviour · use
ActiveforwardreverseIC = βIB, VBE ≈ 0.7 V (Si). Amplifier.
SaturationforwardforwardIC is limited by the external circuit, less than βIB. VCE(sat) ≈ 0.2 V. Closed switch.
CutoffreversereverseIC ≈ 0. Open switch.
Answer. The goal is for almost every carrier from the emitter to reach the collector. A thin, lightly doped base has few holes and gives the electrons little time to meet one, so very few recombine: IB is small and the current gain IC/IB is large. The emitter is heavily doped so that the forward EB current is almost entirely electrons injected into the base, not holes injected back into the emitter. The collector is the largest region because it dissipates the most heat.

E2 · The three connections and the gains α, β, γ

Practice · decks L22–L25 A transistor has β = 100 and ICBO = 20 nA. Find α and ICEO.
Guess first: is α slightly below 1 or slightly above?

A transistor has three terminals but an amplifier needs two input and two output wires, so one terminal is shared ("common") between input and output. That gives three connections.

Relations. Substitute IE = IC + IB into the definitions:

β = α/(1 − α)     α = β/(1 + β)     γ = 1 + β = 1/(1 − α)

Leakage currents. The reverse-biased CB junction leaks a small current of minority carriers. ICBO (also ICO) is the collector-to-base current with the emitter open. In CE the transistor amplifies its own leakage: ICEO = (1 + β)ICBO is the collector current with the base open. Complete expressions:

IC = αIE + ICBO     IC = βIB + (1 + β)ICBO

Characteristics. Input curves (IB against VBE in CE, IE against VBE in CB) look like a forward diode. Output curves are IC against VCE (or VCB), one per input current, nearly flat in the active region (figure in E3).

Answer. α = β/(1 + β) = 100/101 = 0.990. ICEO = (1 + β)ICBO = 101 × 20 nA = 2.02 μA.

E3 · DC load line, Q-point and fixed bias

Professor's deck L26–27 · slide 15 · fixed-bias example (circuit (a) in E4: VCC = 12 V, RB = 240 kΩ, RC = 2.2 kΩ, β = 50) For the fixed-bias circuit, find IB, IC and VCE at the operating point, and the saturation current.
Guess first: which loop gives IB directly?
Operating (Q) point: the DC values (VCE, IC) with no signal applied. · DC analysis: every capacitor is an open circuit.
{{ bjt_out_curves() }}

Fixed bias has one resistor RB from VCC to the base. Two loops:

  1. Base–emitter loop: VCC = IBRB + VBE, so IB = (VCC − 0.7)/RB.
  2. Active region: IC = βIB.
  3. Collector–emitter loop: VCE = VCC − ICRC.

Load line. Step 3 is a straight line on the output curves. Its ends: IC = 0 gives VCE = VCC (cutoff); VCE = 0 gives IC(sat) = VCC/RC (saturation). The Q-point is where the line crosses the curve for the actual IB. For the largest undistorted output swing, place Q near the middle of the line.

Moving the Q-point (slides 20–22): a larger IB slides Q up the line toward saturation. A larger RC lowers the top end, so the line gets flatter. A lower VCC shifts the whole line inward, parallel to itself.

Answer. IB = (12 − 0.7)/240 k = 47.08 μA. IC = 50 × 47.08 μA = 2.35 mA. VCE = 12 − 2.35 mA × 2.2 kΩ = 6.82 V. IC(sat) = 12/2.2 k = 5.45 mA. Q sits near the middle of the line: a good place for an amplifier.

E4 · Bias stabilisation, stability factor, the three bias circuits

Professor's deck L26–27 · slide 27 · emitter-bias example (circuit (b): VCC = 20 V, RB = 430 kΩ, RC = 2 kΩ, RE = 1 kΩ, β = 50) For the emitter-stabilised circuit, find IB, IC and VCE. Then find its stability factor S and compare with the fixed-bias circuit (a).
Guess first: Check E3 showed fixed bias is ruined by a change in β. What could RE do about it?
{{ bjt_bias_three() }}

Why stabilise. Three things shift the Q-point as the transistor warms up or is replaced: ICO (= ICBO) doubles for every 10 °C rise; VBE falls about 2.5 mV per °C; β rises with temperature and varies widely between transistors of the same type. All three raise IC. More IC means more heat, which raises IC again: thermal runaway.

Stability factor. It measures how much IC changes when the leakage ICO changes, with β and VBE held constant. Smaller is better; the ideal is 1.

S = ∂IC/∂ICO     general result: S = (1 + β) / (1 − β·dIB/dIC)

The general result comes from differentiating IC = βIB + (1 + β)ICO with respect to IC. Two more factors are defined the same way: S′ = ∂IC/∂VBE and S″ = ∂IC/∂β.

CircuitBase currentdIB/dICStability factor S
(a) Fixed biasIB = (VCC − VBE)/RB01 + β (worst)
(b) Emitter biasIB = (VCC − VBE)/(RB + (β + 1)RE)−RE/(RB + RE)(1 + β)(1 + RB/RE) / (1 + β + RB/RE)
(c) Voltage dividerIB = (ETh − VBE)/(RTh + (β + 1)RE)−RE/(RTh + RE)same formula with RB → RTh (best: RTh is small)
Collector-to-base feedbackIB = (VCC − VBE)/(RB + (β + 1)RC)−RC/(RB + RC)(1 + β) / (1 + βRC/(RB + RC))

How RE stabilises. If IC rises, the drop across RE rises, which raises the emitter voltage and leaves less voltage to drive the base. IB falls and pulls IC back. This is negative feedback (Part F). In every circuit with RE: VCE = VCC − ICRC − IERE ≈ VCC − IC(RC + RE), and IC(sat) = VCC/(RC + RE).

Answer. IB = (20 − 0.7)/(430 k + 51 × 1 k) = 19.3/481 k = 40.1 μA. IC = 50 × 40.1 μA = 2.01 mA. IE = 51 × 40.1 μA = 2.05 mA. VCE = 20 − 2.01 × 2 − 2.05 × 1 = 13.94 V (13.97 V if IE ≈ IC is used). RB/RE = 430, so S = 51 × 431/(51 + 430) = 45.7. The fixed-bias circuit has S = 1 + β = 51. RE helps only a little here, because RB is so much larger than RE.

Voltage-divider bias, worked (circuit (c), β = 100). Exact: replace R1, R2 and VCC by their Thévenin equivalent as seen from the base: ETh = VCCR2/(R1 + R2) = 22 × 3.9/42.9 = 2.0 V, RTh = R1 ∥ R2 = 3.55 kΩ. IB = (2.0 − 0.7)/(3.55 k + 101 × 1.5 k) = 8.38 μA, IC = 0.84 mA, VCE = 22 − 0.838 × 10 − 0.846 × 1.5 = 12.35 V. S = 101 × (1 + 2.36)/(101 + 2.36) = 3.29. Approximate (valid when βRE ≥ 10R2; here 150 k ≥ 39 k ✓): ignore IB, so VB = ETh = 2.0 V, VE = VB − 0.7 = 1.3 V, IC ≈ IE = 1.3/1.5 k = 0.87 mA. β does not appear at all in the approximate answer: that is why this circuit is called β-independent.

Bias compensation uses a temperature-sensitive part to cancel the drift instead of feedback. Diode compensation: a forward-biased diode of the same material in the base circuit; its drop falls with temperature exactly as VBE does, so the two cancel. Thermistor (resistance falls as temperature rises) placed in the lower divider arm: as it heats, the base voltage drops and holds IC down. Sensistor (resistance rises with temperature) placed in the upper arm or in the emitter does the same.

E6 · Multistage amplifiers and the Darlington pair

Practice · deck "Multi-Stage Amplifier" Two amplifier stages with voltage gains 40 and 60 are cascaded. Find the overall gain as a number and in dB.
Guess first: do the gains add or multiply?
{{ two_stage() }}

Cascading. When one stage cannot give enough gain, the output of one stage feeds the input of the next. The overall gain is the product of the stage gains, where each stage's gain is found with the next stage connected as its load (the input resistance of stage 2 loads stage 1). In decibels, Av(dB) = 20 log10|Av|, and the stage values add.

CouplingHowFor · against
RC couplinga capacitor between stagescheap, small, good in the audio range; the capacitor blocks DC so each stage is biased separately. Gain falls at low frequency.
Transformer couplinga transformer between stagesmatches impedances, so maximum power transfer; bulky, costly, poor frequency response.
Direct couplinga wireamplifies down to DC; the bias of one stage disturbs the next, and drift is amplified.
{{ darlington() }}

Darlington pair. The emitter current of Q1 is the base current of Q2, and the collectors are joined. The pair behaves as one "super-β" transistor.

IE1 = (1 + β1)IB, and IE2 = (1 + β2)IE1 = (1 + β1)(1 + β2)IB, so

βD = β1β2 + β1 + β2 ≈ β1β2

Two base–emitter drops in series: VBE ≈ 1.4 V. Very high input resistance. Used as an emitter follower to drive heavy loads. DC bias with RB and RE: IB = (VCC − VBE)/(RB + βDRE).

Answer. Av = 40 × 60 = 2400. In dB: 20 log 40 + 20 log 60 = 32.0 + 35.6 = 67.6 dB (= 20 log 2400).

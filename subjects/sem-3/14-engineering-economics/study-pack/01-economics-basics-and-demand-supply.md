## 1. Scarcity and what economics is

**Q ▸** (L1) Wants are unlimited. Resources are limited. What problem does that create, and what do people
have to do about it?

**Scarcity**: wants exceed the resources available to satisfy them. Faced with scarcity, people must
**choose**. So:

- **Economics** is the study of the choices people make to cope with scarcity, or equivalently the
  allocation of scarce resources among alternative uses to satisfy human wants.
- The **basic economic problems** are **resource allocation** (what to produce, and whether labour-intensive or
  capital-intensive) and the **distribution of income**.

| | Microeconomics | Macroeconomics |
|---|---|---|
| Studies | individual units: consumers, firms, workers, investors, and their markets | **aggregates**: national output and its growth, interest rates, unemployment, inflation |
| Example question | how a firm prices one product | why inflation rose this year |

**Economic goals:** high employment · price stability · equitable income distribution · growth. They are
**not always complementary**. Cutting unemployment sharply tends to raise inflation.

## 2. Production possibilities curve (PPC)

**Q ▸** A country can build roads or produce something else with fixed resources. What does the curve of
all the maximum combinations look like, and what makes it move?

The **PPC** plots the maximum output combinations of two goods with the given resources and technology. It is bowed
outward. The economy is on the curve when it is efficient and inside it when resources are idle.

| Event | PPC |
|---|---|
| More resources, or better technology | shifts **outward** (growth) |
| Fewer resources, or degraded technology / low investment | shifts **inward** (low growth) |

## 3. Economic systems

An **economic system** is the set of arrangements and institutions that meets a society's two objectives:
**increasing the availability of resources** and **ensuring their economic use**.

| | Capitalist | Socialist | Mixed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Decides what/how to produce | free market (demand and supply) | a regulatory authority (e.g. a planning commission). Private property and inheritance are abolished | both |
| Merits | self-regulating, faster growth, operative efficiency, flexible | social security, fewer fluctuations, coordinated development, fewer social disputes | aims to get the benefits of both |
| Demerits | income/wealth inequality, unequal opportunity, distorted production, merit goods unprofitable so not produced, wasteful competition, loss of welfare values | weak economic incentives, slow growth, low labour productivity, low per-capita income (the slide also lists cyclical fluctuations) | its features have to be chosen by judging the market mechanism's effects |

## 4. Engineering economics and the two efficiencies

**Q ▸** (L2) A diesel engine's efficiency is below 100%. A business's efficiency must be *above* 100%.
How can both be true?

They are two different efficiencies:

```
 Technical efficiency  = system output / system input      (physical, e.g. energy)
                         ALWAYS < 100%  (friction, incomplete combustion)

 Economic efficiency   = system worth / system cost
                         worth = annual revenue; cost = total annual expenses
                         MUST be > 100% for the business to survive and grow
```

**Engineering economics** covers the methods for taking economic decisions that **minimize cost and/or
maximize benefit** to a business. It runs from recognising a need, through developing and evaluating
alternatives, to deciding and executing efficiently.

**Economics vs engineering economics (L1 case studies: 100 MW solar farm, high-speed rail).**
- **Economics view:** the **whole society**, meaning jobs, emissions, income distribution, regional growth,
  externalities, and effects on prices.
- **Engineering economics view:** the **project's own financial feasibility**, meaning investment, operating cost,
  revenue, **NPV, IRR, payback**.
- Exam line: *"Economics gives the social picture; engineering economics decides whether the project pays.
  Good decisions use both."*

## 5. Demand

**Q ▸** (L2) "Desire? Ability? Willingness?" You would like a sports car but can't pay for it. Is that
demand?

No. That is only **desire**. The chain is:

- **Desire**: a wish to possess something.
- **Want**: desire backed by purchasing power and by willingness to buy.
- **Demand**: the quantity of a commodity a consumer wishes to buy **at a given price**, backed by
  purchasing power.

Three rules about a quantity demanded:
1. It is the **desired** purchase, not the actual one.
2. It is a **flow**, measured per period of time. "10 oranges" means nothing. "10 per week" does.
3. It has meaning only **at a given price**. "10 per week at ₹100/dozen" is a complete statement.

**Static vs dynamic analysis**

| Static | Dynamic |
|---|---|
| variables **not dated**, "timeless" | variables **dated**, and time matters |
| one point of equilibrium | an economy in motion, with unexpected changes (new taxes, subsidies) |
| Dₜ and Sₜ both depend on today's price Pₜ | **Dₜ = f(Pₜ), Sₜ = g(Pₜ₋₁), Dₜ = Sₜ**: today's supply depends on **yesterday's price** (a time lag) |

**Determinants of an individual's demand** (the demand function **Dₓ = f(Pₓ, Pᵧ, P_z, M, T)**):

| Symbol | Factor | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Pₓ | own price | ↑ price → ↓ quantity (law of demand) |
| M | income | **normal good**: ↑M → ↑Q. **Inferior good**: ↑M → ↓Q |
| Pᵧ, P_z | prices of related goods | **substitutes** (used instead) and **complements** (used together) |
| T | taste | a taste for the good → more demand, and tastes can be seasonal |

**Market demand** adds two more: **size of the population**, and **income distribution** (a richer top share
means more demand for luxuries, a richer poor share means more demand for wage goods).
Only one factor is changed at a time. The rest are held constant (*ceteris paribus*).

## 6. Law of demand and why the curve slopes down

**Q ▸** (L3) Coke goes from ₹20 to ₹25 while Pepsi stays at ₹20. Pepsi's price didn't move. Why do
people buy more Pepsi?

Because Pepsi became **relatively** cheaper. That is the **substitution effect**.

**Law of demand**: other things equal, quantity demanded varies **inversely** with price, so the demand curve
**slopes downward to the right**.

It slopes down because of the **price effect = substitution effect + income effect (PE = SE + IE)**:
- **Substitution effect (SE):** a price change alters **relative** prices, and buyers switch toward the relatively
  cheaper good.
- **Income effect (IE):** a price fall raises **real income** (purchasing power), so the buyer can afford more.
- The two operate **simultaneously**.

**Change in quantity demanded vs change in demand. This is a near-certain 2-marker:**

| | Change in **quantity demanded** | Change in **demand** |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | the good's **own price** | anything **else** (income, tastes, related prices, population) |
| On the graph | **movement along** the same curve | the **whole curve shifts** |
| Names | **expansion** (price ↓) / **contraction** (price ↑) | **increase** (shift right) / **decrease** (shift left) |

## 7. Supply

**Supply** is the quantity producers are **willing to sell** at each price, per unit of time.
**Law of supply**: price and quantity supplied move in the **same direction**, because a higher price means more
profit (profit = TR − TC, with TR = p·q and TC = q·AC). Example (L3): pens at ₹2 → 25 thousand/month, up to
₹6 → 70 thousand.

**Supply function**: Qₛ = f(P₁, P₂…Pₙ, F₁…Fₘ, T, G)

| Determinant | Effect on supply of this good |
|---|---|
| own price P₁ | ↑ → ↑ (the most immediate one) |
| prices of other goods P₂…Pₙ | ↑ → ↓ (other goods become more attractive to produce) |
| prices of factors F (the cost of production) | ↑ → ↓ |
| technology T | better → lower cost → ↑ |
| producer's goal G | shapes how much is produced |

## Self-test

1. Give one example each of a normal good and an inferior good, and state the income effect on each.
2. A fall in the price of petrol raises car sales. Is that a movement along the car-demand curve or a shift? Which way?
3. Write the formulas for technical and economic efficiency, and say which must exceed 100%.
4. Write the dynamic demand–supply model and say what makes it dynamic.
5. Give two merits and two demerits of a capitalist economy.
6. What shifts a PPC inward?

---

## Answers

1. Normal good: branded clothing (↑income → ↑Q). Inferior good: coarse grain / cheap bus travel (↑income → ↓Q).
2. A **shift to the right** (an increase in demand). Petrol is a **complement** of cars, and its price is not the cars' own price.
3. Technical = output/input, always < 100%. Economic = worth/cost, which **must be > 100%**.
4. Dₜ = f(Pₜ), Sₜ = g(Pₜ₋₁), Dₜ = Sₜ. The variables are dated and supply reacts to **last period's price** (a time lag).
5. Merits: self-regulating, faster growth (also operative efficiency, flexibility). Demerits: inequality of income/wealth, merit goods not produced (also wasteful competition, distorted production).
6. A decrease in available resources, degraded technology, or low investment.

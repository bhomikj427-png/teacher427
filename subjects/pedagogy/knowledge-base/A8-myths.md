# A8 — Myths About Learning (and How to Test Any Claim)

> **Information type:** A3 row 6 (metacognitive — beliefs about how learning works). Each myth is
> refuted by a mechanism taught earlier (A1–A7), which is why A8 comes last in Part A.
> Confidence tags: `settled` / `contested` / `uncertain` / `suspect`. Source IDs → `sources.md`.
> Myths are not stupid: each survives because it *feels* true. The "why it feels true" column
> matters as much as the correction.

---

## 1. How common these beliefs are `settled` (for this sample)

- **Dek12** surveyed 242 UK and Dutch teachers who were *interested* in brain science. They
  believed about half of 15 myths. % believing (UK / NL):
  - learning styles: 93 / 96
  - left/right-brain dominance explains individual differences: 91 / 86
  - coordination exercises integrate the two hemispheres: 88 / 82
  - "we use only 10% of our brain": 48 / 46
- **"Possessing greater general knowledge about the brain does not appear to protect teachers
  from believing in neuromyths"** (Dek12). Knowing more brain facts is not the same as knowing
  how to test a claim (§3).
- Limit: a self-selected sample, not a national estimate.

## 2. The myths

| # | Myth | Why it feels true | What the evidence says | Status | Mech. (unit) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Learning styles:** people learn better when taught in their preferred style (visual, auditory, kinaesthetic) | People do have preferences; some content suits some formats; matching *feels* comfortable | Testing the claim needs a **crossover interaction**: style-A learners do better with method A *and* style-B learners do better with method B. Virtually no study shows it; some contradict it (P08). Preference ≠ differential learning | **X** `settled` | Match the method to the *content* (A3 row) and prior knowledge, not the person (A4) |
| 2 | **Left-brained vs right-brained people** (logical vs creative) | Some functions do lean to one side (language left) | 1,011 brains: lateralization is local, in everyone; no person-level "left-brained" or "right-brained" network pattern (N13) | **X** `settled` | A2 (memory systems are about *kinds of information*, not person types) |
| 3 | **Learning pyramid:** we retain 10% of what we read … 90% of what we teach | Neat round numbers; matches the true idea that doing beats watching | Versions are over 160 years old, so they did not come from research; no data behind the percentages (LH18). The kernel that *is* true (constructive > passive) comes from ICAP, without the numbers (CW14) | **X** `settled` | A6 §3 |
| 4 | **Multitasking while studying is fine** ("I study better with my phone / TV") | You still *perform* the reading; it feels unaffected | Dividing attention *at encoding* causes large memory losses; at retrieval it mostly slows you (CGNA96) | **X** `settled` | A2 §4 (encoding needs attention) |
| 5 | **Pomodoro is a learning technique** | Short sprints feel focused and productive | 32 studies, N = 5,270: outcomes were mostly *self-rated* focus, fatigue and motivation; academic outcomes usually self-report (Og25). A possible scheduling aid, not evidence of learning | **T** for learning (engine: excluded) | A1 (performance/feelings ≠ learning) |
| 6 | **Growth-mindset training reliably raises grades** | The message is hopeful; effort does matter | Mindset–achievement r = .10; interventions d = 0.08 overall, ≈ 0.03 for middle/upper-SES students, d = 0.34 for low-SES (7 effect sizes) (Sis18). A national RCT found gains for **lower-achieving** students, where peer norms supported the message (Y19) | `contested` — small, conditional | A7 (beliefs → regulation); praise process, not person (engine) |
| 7 | **The Feynman Technique is Feynman's method** | Named after him; matches his reputation for clear explanation | The named 4-step method appears c. 2011 in Scott Young's writing (SY-FT); no Feynman text describing it was found (`uncertain`). Its parts *are* evidenced: self-explanation g = 0.55 (Bi18), teaching as a generative strategy (FM16) — and Young himself warns it is no substitute for practice problems | Attribution `uncertain`; method **S** via its parts | A6 §6, A4 |
| 8 | **Rereading, highlighting, summarizing are good study** | Fluency; visible effort | Rated low utility (D13); covered in A4 and M6 | **T** `settled` | A1 §6 |

**Not covered here (prevalence only, no refutation read):** "10% of the brain", coordination
exercises / Brain Gym → backlog S11 (find a primary refutation or drop).

## 3. A test kit for any learning claim (what all eight failures share)

Ask, in order:
1. **Is the outcome learning?** Delayed, unaided recall or transfer — not focus, enjoyment,
   confidence or how productive it felt (A1; myth 5).
2. **Is there a proper comparison?** Against a real alternative (e.g. retrieval), not against
   reading or nothing (A6 §4).
3. **For "fits the person" claims: is there a crossover interaction?** (P08; myth 1).
4. **Where do the numbers come from?** Trace them to a study. Round numbers with no study are a
   warning sign (myth 3).
5. **How big, for whom?** Overall effect vs subgroups; how many studies; publication bias (myth 6;
   engine `research/07`).
6. **Who is selling it?** Commercial programmes carry the most-believed myths (Dek12 abstract).
7. **Does a known mechanism predict it?** If the claim contradicts A1–A7 (attention, WM,
   retrieval, generation), the bar is higher.

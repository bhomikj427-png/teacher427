# A1 — Studying vs Learning

> **Information type:** all types. This unit is about *how to tell whether any learning happened*,
> so it sits above the A3 taxonomy. Evidence is strongest for factual and conceptual material.
> **Threshold concepts 1** (learning ≠ performance), **4** (retrieval is encoding) and **5**
> (some forgetting is useful).
> Confidence tags: `settled` / `contested` / `uncertain` / `suspect`. Source IDs → `sources.md`.

---

## 1. Three words that get mixed up

| Term | What it is | Can you see it? |
|---|---|---|
| **Studying** | An activity: time spent with the intent to learn | Yes: hours, pages, videos |
| **Performance** | "What we can observe and measure during instruction or training" (BB11) — answers right now, in the study context | Yes, immediately |
| **Learning** | "The more or less permanent change in knowledge or understanding that is the target of instruction" — "something we must try to infer" (BB11) | Only later: on a delayed, unaided test, ideally in a new situation |

- Studying is the input. Learning is the output. Performance is the only thing visible during
  the session, and it is a poor gauge of the output. `settled` (BB11, SB15)
- The learner's own definition matches the research: you can study (input) without learning
  (output). The research adds a third term, performance, and most of the confusion sits there.

## 2. Learning and performance can come apart in both directions `settled`

SB15 (an integrative review across motor and verbal learning) documents both directions:
- **Performance without learning:** fast gains during practice that do not last or transfer.
  Example: massed rereading gives quick fluency that fades.
- **Learning without performance:** learning that shows up on a later test even though practice
  performance looked flat or worse. Example: spaced or mixed practice.
- **Opposite effects:** some manipulations help performance now and hurt learning later, and vice
  versa. "Conditions of learning that make performance improve rapidly often fail to support
  long-term retention and transfer, whereas conditions that create challenges and slow the rate of
  apparent learning often optimize long-term retention and transfer." (BB11, verbatim)

**The key demonstration, Roediger & Karpicke 2006 (RK06)** `settled`: students either restudied
a prose passage or took recall tests on it.
- At **5 minutes**, restudying won.
- At **2 days and 1 week**, the result reversed: the tested group recalled more.
- Reported one-week figures: 61% (tested) vs 40% (restudied). `uncertain` (press-release figures;
  check the paper's own table in S11)
- A student judging by the 5-minute result picks the worse method.

## 3. Mechanism: storage strength vs retrieval strength `settled` as a framework

Bjork & Bjork's "new theory of disuse" (BB92; restated at full text in BB11) splits "how well you
know something" into two quantities:
- **Storage strength:** "how entrenched or interassociated a memory representation is with related
  knowledge and skills" (BB11). It only grows; it is assumed never to be lost (BB92).
- **Retrieval strength:** "the current activation or accessibility of that representation",
  driven by situational cues and how recently you saw it (BB11). It rises fast and falls fast.
  Current performance is "entirely a function of current retrieval strength" (BB11).
- **The interaction (the core claim, BB92):** the *higher* current retrieval strength is, the
  *smaller* the gain in storage strength from studying or retrieving again.
  - Re-exposing material that is still fresh feels productive but adds little.
  - Retrieving after some forgetting (lower retrieval strength) adds more storage strength.
- **Consequences:**
  1. **Forgetting enables learning.** Partial forgetting sets up a bigger storage gain at the
     next retrieval. This is the mechanism behind spacing (threshold concept 5).
  2. **Fluency misleads.** High retrieval strength during study *feels* like learning, but it is
     retrieval strength, not storage strength.
  3. **"Can't recall it" ≠ "never learned it."** Storage strength can be high while retrieval
     strength is low; relearning is then faster (Ebbinghaus's "savings", MD15 → A2).
- Status: a theoretical framework that organises a large body of findings (spacing, testing,
  interleaving, variation). It is not a directly measured brain quantity. `settled` as the
  standard explanatory frame; `contested` in mechanistic detail.

## 4. Desirable difficulties and their limit `settled`

- **Definition:** a difficulty is desirable when it "trigger[s] encoding and retrieval processes
  that support learning, comprehension, and remembering" (BB11).
- **Named examples (BB11):** varying the conditions of practice, spacing, interleaving, and using
  tests as learning events. The strength of evidence for each → A4.
- **The limit (verbatim, BB11):** "If, however, the learner does not have the background knowledge
  or skills to respond to them successfully, they become undesirable difficulties."
  - So difficulty is not the goal; *successful effortful processing* is.
  - This links to the engine's ~80% success calibration and to expertise reversal (A4).
- **Undesirable difficulties:** confusing instructions, noise, tasks the learner cannot attempt at all.

## 5. Retrieval is itself an encoding event `settled` (core) / `contested` (scope)

- **Karpicke & Blunt 2011 (KB11):** after reading science texts, students either practised
  retrieval (free recall) or built concept maps with the text open (elaborative study).
  - Retrieval produced more learning on **verbatim *and* inference questions**.
  - It also won when the final test asked students to *make a concept map*.
  - Students predicted concept mapping would be better.
  - So retrieval did not just "maintain" memory; it built learning that supports inference.
- **Transfer (PR18):** a meta-analysis of 192 effect sizes finds testing benefits transfer to new
  questions and formats, about d ≈ 0.40 overall. The benefit is larger when the final test asks
  for the same kind of response (≈ 0.58) and smaller otherwise (≈ 0.28).
  - `uncertain` on the exact values (secondary report; verify S11).
  - Effect sizes are optimistic upper bounds (publication bias).
- **Why this matters:** it is the hinge of the Justin Sung audit (J4, J6, J7, J16). The claim
  "retrieval only fights forgetting, it doesn't encode" conflicts with KB11.
  - Still `contested`: whether retrieval of *isolated facts* builds relational understanding.
    KB11 used free recall of whole texts, not single flashcards.
  - The real variable may be the prompt's depth (SOLO level, A3). S10 tests that, not the
    label "retrieval".

## 6. Learners cannot feel which method works `settled`

- **Kornell & Bjork 2008 (KoB08):** people studied painters' styles, with paintings either massed
  (one artist at a time) or spaced (artists mixed).
  - Spacing produced better classification of *new* paintings.
  - Yet more than 80% rated massing as equal or better, and only 15% actually did better massed.
  - The judgment persisted **even after their own test results showed the opposite**.
  - Replicated (Kornell et al. 2010 with older adults; 2014 Frontiers replication).
- Same pattern in KB11 (students favoured concept mapping) and in BB11's warning that we are
  "misled by our current performance".
- **Implication:** the feeling of learning is not evidence of learning; a delayed, unaided test
  is. (→ A7)

## 7. Diagnostic: studying-without-learning checklist

Each line is a test you can run, not a feeling to consult.
| Sign | Why it signals performance, not learning | Fix (→ A4) |
|---|---|---|
| Material feels familiar when you reread it | Recognition and fluency = retrieval strength (BB92) | Close the book; recall first |
| You can answer only straight after studying | Immediate performance favours massing (RK06) | Re-test after ≥ 1 day |
| You can answer only one wording of the question | Retrieval tied to one cue; weaker transfer (PR18) | Vary questions; apply to new cases |
| Practice always feels smooth | No desirable difficulty (BB11) | Space, mix, self-test, aiming at ~80% success |
| You choose methods by how they feel | Feelings favour the worse method (KoB08) | Choose by delayed test scores |

## 8. Uses
- **As a learner:** judge learning only by delayed, unaided, varied retrieval. Expect effective
  sessions to feel harder and slower.
- **As a teacher:** do not read success off in-lesson performance. Build delayed checks into the
  next lesson. Warn students in advance that good methods feel worse.
- **For the engine:** already in place (start-of-session delayed recall; CHECK gates). A1 adds one
  point to watch: **retrieval prompts should sometimes be relational**, given the scope question
  in §5 → a note for the S12 engine proposal, not an edit.

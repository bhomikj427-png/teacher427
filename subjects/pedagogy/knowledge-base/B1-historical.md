> Part B unit. Each entry: who → what they actually did (from their own text where possible) →
> the mechanism it relies on (Part A) → **serves** (A3 row) · **grade** · **use as learner** ·
> **use as teacher**. Grades follow A4 §1: **S** strong · **M** moderate · **T** thin ·
> **X** debunked. A historical method is graded by the **modern evidence for its mechanism**, never

## 1. Socrates (c. 470–399 BC) — the elenchus

**What he did (primary: Plato, *Meno* 82b–85d, Jowett tr., Gutenberg #1643, read S7a).** Socrates
asks an untaught slave boy how to double a square. The boy answers confidently and wrongly
(double the side), is questioned until he sees the contradiction, becomes perplexed, and is then
led by further questions to the diagonal construction. Socrates' own commentary marks the three
moves: "I am not teaching the boy anything, but only asking him questions"; after the boy is
stuck, "Is he not better off in knowing his ignorance?"; and the explanation he gives —
"this spontaneous recovery of knowledge in him is recollection."

Two things to separate:
- **The method** = question → learner commits to an answer → contradiction exposed → learner
  rebuilds. Testable, and its parts have been tested.
- **The theory** (knowledge is recollection of what the soul already knew) = not a learning claim
  anyone tests; drop it. Note also the questions in the *Meno* are heavily leading — many are
  yes/no — so "only asking" is generous: the teacher's questions carry most of the structure.

**Mechanism (Part A).** (a) Committing to an answer before instruction = **pretesting /
generation** (A6 §5; SCA24: g = 0.54 on the asked items). (b) Making the wrong belief explicit and
then refuting it = the structure of **refutation text** — state the misconception, refute it,
give the correct account. Schroeder & Kucera 2022 (SK22): 44 independent comparisons, n = 3,869,
g = 0.41 vs standard expository text. (c) A tutor who **prompts instead of explaining**: Chi et
al. 2001 (CSJ01) suppressed tutors' explanations and feedback and had them only prompt —
"Surprisingly, students learned just as effectively", attributed to the students' own
construction (≈ self-explanation, Bi18 g = 0.55). (d) "Better off knowing his ignorance" =
**calibration** — the learner's confidence is corrected before learning (A7).

**Serves:** A3 row 3 (principles/models) and the misconception problem across rows 2–3; row 6
(metacognitive: discovering you don't know).

**Grade:** **M** — the parts are evidenced (pretesting S for asked items; self-explanation S;
refutation text M, a single meta with g = 0.41; prompting-only tutoring = one study, CSJ01). The
whole "Socratic method" as practised in classrooms is not tested as a package. `contested` edge:
a novice with no relevant knowledge has nothing to rebuild from — the boy could do arithmetic and
see squares; questioning cannot create knowledge that has no parts in place (A5 schemas; KSC06).

**Use as learner.** Before reading a topic, write your answer to its central question and commit
to it; then read to find where you were wrong. When you find the error, write the wrong belief
and the correction side by side (your own refutation text) — that pair is a better flashcard than
the correct fact alone.

**Use as teacher.** Ask for a committed prediction, show the case that breaks it, and *then* let
the learner rebuild — but supply the pieces they lack (Socrates supplies the diagonal by his
questions). Prompting ("why?", "what follows?") can replace explaining only when the learner
already has the parts. Never use "Socratic" as a reason to withhold information from a novice.

## 2. Confucius (551–479 BC) — learning with thought; readiness; review

**What he said (primary: *Analects*, Legge tr., Gutenberg #3330, read S7a).**
- 2.15: "Learning without thought is labour lost; thought without learning is perilous."
- 2.11: "If a man keeps cherishing his old knowledge, so as continually to be acquiring new, he may
  be a teacher of others."
- 7.8: "I do not open up the truth to one who is not eager to get knowledge, nor help out any one
  who is not anxious to explain himself. When I have presented one corner of a subject to any
  one, and he cannot from it learn the other three, I do not repeat my lesson." (Both sentences
  confirmed verbatim, S7a.)

These are aphorisms, not a method with steps. The *Analects* is a compiled record of sayings; how
Confucius actually taught day to day is not documented in a way that can be checked.

**Mechanism (Part A).** 2.15 = information must be **processed** (elaborated, related) to be
learned, and processing without information produces confident error — levels of processing and
self-explanation (A6 §1–2; Bi18), and the reverse warning matches A5 (no schema → no sound
reasoning). 2.11 = **review old material while learning new** — the same behaviour as spacing and
successive relearning (A4 §1, S), but nothing in the text specifies intervals or retrieval.
7.8 = teach only after the learner has struggled and tried to say it ("anxious to explain
himself") → the learner's attempt comes first (pretesting / productive failure, A4 §1); and "one
corner → three corners" is a **transfer** expectation (A5): give one case, expect the learner to
generate the analogous ones.

**Serves:** A3 row 3 (principles), row 6 (metacognitive — "thought" about one's learning); 2.11
touches row 1 (keeping facts alive).

**Grade:** **T** as a method (sayings, no procedure, no outcome record). The mechanisms they
point to range from S (spacing; generation) to M (productive failure). Do not credit Confucius
with "discovering" spacing — 2.11 says *review*, not *spaced retrieval*.

**Use as learner.** After each reading block, close the book and write what it means and how it
connects to what you knew (2.15). Each session, open with something old before anything new (2.11
— and make it retrieval, not rereading). From one solved example, generate two variants yourself
before looking at more (7.8).

**Use as teacher.** Give one fully worked case and ask for the "other three corners" — but only
once the learner has the prerequisites; for a novice, three corners from one is too far (A5:
far transfer is rare and needs varied examples).

## 3. Quintilian (c. 35–100) — *Institutio Oratoria*: imitate, then practise, then revise

**What he wrote (primary: *Institutio Oratoria*, Loeb 1920–22, Butler tr., LacusCurtius, read S7a).**
- 1.2.28: "Vessels with narrow mouths will not receive liquids if too much be poured into them at a
  time" — feed a young mind only what it can take in.
- 1.3.1: "The surest indication in a child is his power of memory"; next is imitation.
- 1.3.10: approves of play; 1.3.13: "I disapprove of flogging, although it is the regular custom."
- 10.2.4: "imitation alone is not sufficient" — the student must add his own (10.2.28).
- 10.3.10: "write quickly and you will never write well, write well and you will soon write quickly."
- 10.4.2: "the best method of correction is to put aside what we have written for a certain time."
- 10.7.29: "We must write, therefore, whenever possible."

**Mechanism (Part A).** The sequence *study models → imitate → produce your own → revise* is the
ancestor of **worked examples → completion → independent practice** (A4 §1, S for novices;
KACS03). "Narrow-mouthed vessels" is a pre-scientific statement of **limited working memory**
(A2; CLT) — the image fits, but it is an analogy, not a finding. "Write well → soon write
quickly" = accuracy first, then **fluency/automaticity** (A3 row 4: declarative → compiled
procedure, A82). Setting work aside before correcting = a delay that lets the writer read it as a
reader would; no direct modern test of this specific practice was checked.

**Serves:** A3 row 4 (procedures — composing and delivering speech) and row 8 (delivery, voice);
1.2.28 bears on every row.

**Grade:** **T** as a method (one teacher's manual, no outcome record); the worked-example →
fading sequence it resembles is **S** for novices. Do not read "memory is the surest sign of
ability" as modern advice — it is a 1st-century claim about talent, not a learning method.

**Use as learner.** Copy a model solution's *structure*, then solve a new problem in that
structure without looking, then compare. Get it right slowly before trying to go fast. Re-check
your own written answer after a break, not straight away.

**Use as teacher.** Show a model, have learners imitate it closely, then require variation — and
fade the model. Give less per step to beginners (1.2.28) and correct individually.

## 4. Jesuit *Ratio Studiorum* (1599) — prelection, daily repetition, Saturday review

**What it prescribed (primary: *Ratio Studiorum* of 1599, English text at the Kolbe Foundation
site, "Common Rules for the Teachers of the Lower Classes"; translator not named on the page —
the standard English is Farrell 1970, not confirmed; read S7a).** The rules build a review
cycle at several timescales:
- **Daily:** "The repetition of the previous day's lesson … should be conducted in the same way";
  "Only the more important and useful points should be reviewed." Pupils "recite the
  prelections from memory to the decurions" (student leaders), who recite to the chief decurion
  or teacher.
- **Weekly:** "On Saturday what has been learned during one or several weeks should be publicly
  recited from memory"; "On Saturday all the prelections of the week are to be reviewed."
- **Yearly:** "a month's strenuous review before the examination" covering "the main points of
  the year's work"; and "the matter studied the previous year will always be reviewed at the
  beginning of the next year."
- **Feedback:** "The written work of each pupil ought to be corrected daily by the teacher," ordinarily
  individually; rivals "correct publicly any mistake as soon as they notice it."
- **Motivation:** class split into "two fairly equal camps to stimulate rivalry"; contests,
  prizes, honour.
- Higher faculties (Rules of the Academy): an hour of repetitions on class days; disputations
  weekly, where a student presents a question with "arguments for both sides" and takes
  counter-arguments.

**Mechanism (Part A).** This is the clearest historical match to **retrieval + spacing at
expanding intervals** (A4 §1, S): recall from memory (not rereading) on day +1, at the week, at
the year's end, and again at the next year's start — a day/week/year ladder close to successive
relearning (RDS13). Daily individual correction = **feedback after a retrieval attempt** (engine
feedback protocol). Peer recitation to decurions = many retrieval events per pupil without the
teacher. Disputation (argue both sides, answer objections) = elaboration and explanation (A6).

**Serves:** A3 row 1 (facts, texts, grammar — the bulk of the curriculum) and row 3 (disputation).

**Grade:** **T** for the *Ratio* as a system — no outcome data; the Jesuit schools' reputation is
not evidence (survivorship, selected pupils). **S** for the mechanism it schedules (retrieval +
spacing). **Rivalry, public naming of the slack and prizes: not supported as learning methods** —
competition and public comparison are motivational devices with mixed effects (not reviewed in
Part A; do not import). Note what the schedule lacks: the intervals are set by the calendar, not
by each pupil's forgetting.

**Use as learner.** Steal the ladder: recall yesterday's material at the start of each session,
everything from the week on one fixed day, and the whole course before the exam *and* at the
start of the next term. Recite from memory, then check — never reread instead.

**Use as teacher.** Open every class with recall of the previous lesson; give a weekly cumulative
recall; correct written work quickly and individually. Peer recitation in pairs is a cheap way to
multiply retrieval. Leave out the public shaming.

## 5. Comenius (1592–1670) — *Didactica Magna*: senses first, example + rule, repetition by teaching

**What he wrote (primary: *The Great Didactic*, Keatinge tr., 1907 text, Internet Archive
cu31924031053709, full text searched S7a).**
- Ch. XX §6, the "golden rule for teachers": "Everything should, as far as is possible, be placed
  before the senses. … If an object can make an impression on several senses at once, it should be
  brought into contact with several." §7: "the commencement of knowledge must always come from the
  senses."
- Ch. XXI: arts are taught "rather by example than by precept", but "precepts and rules must be
  given as well, that they may guide the operations and prevent error … Reasons should also be
  given for each rule." And: "We should always commence with what is easy."
- Ch. XVIII §43: "education cannot attain to thoroughness without frequent and suitable
  repetitions of and exercises on the subjects taught." The repetition he designs is **pupils
  teaching the lesson to each other**: repetition works "not only because constant repetition
  impresses a fact indelibly on the mind, but because the process of teaching in itself gives a
  deeper insight into the subject taught" (citing Joachim Fortius, who forgot what he read within a
  month but kept what he taught). The same passage also recommends reading the lesson over morning
  and evening — rereading, which Part A grades low (A1 §6).
- *Orbis Sensualium Pictus* (1658), a picture vocabulary book, is usually called the first
  illustrated children's textbook — standard secondary claim, not checked at a primary source.

**Mechanism (Part A).** (a) Concrete, perceived example before abstract rule = **concrete →
abstract** (A4; concreteness fading — sourced in Pestalozzi, §6). (b) Example + rule + *reason
for the rule* = worked example with explanation (A4 §1, S). (c) Learning by teaching:
Kobayashi 2019 (Ko19) meta, 28 studies: preparing-to-teach g = 0.35; teaching after preparing
g = 0.56; benefits held after a delay and were larger for **interactive** teaching. Comenius's
"teaching gives deeper insight" is the mechanism these studies isolate (explaining → generation
and retrieval). (d) Words with pictures: Noetel et al. 2022 (No22), 29 reviews / 1,189 studies:
11 multimedia design principles had positive meta-analytic effects (contiguity, signalling,
modality, coherence, segmenting …). The bare "add a picture" principle is not named in the
abstract → `uncertain` at that grain; design quality matters more than presence.

**Not learning styles.** "Several senses at once" matches the *content* to the senses that carry
it (show visible things, let pupils hear audible things) — for every learner. Learning-styles
myths match a *person* to a modality; that is what fails (P08; A8 myth 1).

**Serves:** A3 row 2 (concepts via perceived examples), row 1 (vocabulary — *Orbis Pictus*),
row 4 (arts: example + rule).

**Grade:** **T** for the system (a design treatise, no outcome data); its parts map to **S**
(worked examples with reasons), **M** (learning by teaching, one meta, g 0.35–0.56) and **M**
(multimedia design). The morning-and-evening rereading advice is **T** (D13 low).

**Use as learner.** For anything with a physical or visual form, look at the real thing or a
good diagram before the definition. Teach the topic aloud to someone (or an empty chair) with
the book closed — expecting to answer questions makes it work better (Ko19: interactive > not).

**Use as teacher.** Example first, then the rule, then the reason for the rule. Build "pupil
explains to pupil" into every lesson. Pair words and pictures carefully (near each other,
nothing decorative) rather than adding pictures for their own sake.

## 6. Pestalozzi (1746–1827) — *Anschauung*: sense-impression first, simple elements, essential features

**What he wrote (primary: *How Gertrude Teaches Her Children*, 1801; Holland & Turner tr., ed.
Cooke, 1894/1898 Bardeen printing, Internet Archive howgertrudeteach00pest, full text searched S7a).**
- "I have fixed the highest supreme principle of instruction in the recognition of
  sense-impression [*Anschauung*] as the absolute foundation of all knowledge." All instruction
  reduced to "three elementary means": **number, form and language**, taught together.
- His principles of the "physical mechanism" include: "The most complex sense-impressions rest upon
  simple elements. When you are perfectly clear about these, the most complex will become simple"
  and "The more senses you have questioned about the nature or appearance of a thing, the more
  accurate will be your knowledge of it."
- He warns that "single views of an object, under varying conditions" can "confuse … the essential
  view": the learner must separate a thing's **essential nature from its accidental qualities**, and
  "comprehensive sense-impressions" protect against "one-sided views".
- The translators note *Anschauung* has no exact English equivalent ("sense-impression" is a
  compromise; it also means direct intuitive apprehension).

**Mechanism (Part A).** (a) Concrete → abstract is the idea that **concreteness fading** later
made precise: Fyfe & Nathan 2018 (FN18) define it as "a three-step progression that begins with
enacting a physical instantiation of a concept, moves to an iconic depiction, and then fades to
the more abstract representation"; they credit the general notion to Bruner (1966), cite
"increasing empirical and theoretical support" (incl. the Fyfe et al. 2014 systematic review,
FMSG14, read at bib level only), and call it "an underspecified theory of instruction".
(b) Separating essential from accidental features = **concept induction** (A3 row 2): a category
is learned from varied examples that differ in irrelevant features and share the defining ones
(A4 bridge row 2). Pestalozzi saw the danger (one view misleads) but his remedy — more senses —
is not the evidenced one; **varied and contrasted examples** are (A4 §2). (c) "Simple elements
first" = segmenting high element-interactivity material (CLT; A3 §2.4).

**Serves:** A3 row 2 (concepts — number, form), row 1 (language/vocabulary), early row 3.

**Grade:** **T** for the object-lesson method (no outcome data; the fame of his schools at
Burgdorf and Yverdon is reputation, not evidence). **M** (`uncertain` on size) for concreteness
fading — mostly maths/science studies, theory admitted to be underspecified (FN18). "More senses →
more accurate knowledge" is **T** as stated; see the Comenius note on learning styles (§5).

**Use as learner.** Start a new abstract idea from one concrete case you can see or handle, draw
it, then write it in symbols — and say aloud which features of the concrete case *don't* matter.

**Use as teacher.** Concrete → picture → symbol, and **make the fade explicit** (point at what in
the symbol stands for what in the object). Remove the concrete scaffold on purpose; a concept
that lives only in the blocks will not transfer.

## 7. Herbart (1776–1841) — four formal concepts → the Herbartians' five steps

**What he wrote (primary: *The Science of Education*, Felkin & Felkin tr., 1908 printing,
Internet Archive scienceofeducati00herbuoft, read S7a).** Herbart names four stages of
instruction: "Instruction must universally point out, connect, teach, philosophise; the first is
productive of **Clearness**, the second of **Association**, the third leads to **System**, the last
to **Method**." His own worked example (quoted by the translators from the *Kurze Encyklopädie*):
first separate the objects and examine them "as far as possible singly, for Clearness requires
the absence of everything through which one object can obscure the rest"; then combine them in
"manifold incidental connections" (Association); only then the systematic exposition (System);
finally Method, "which shows with regard to every part of System the necessity of its position" —
i.e. using and applying the system. His central aim was "many-sided interest".

**Attribution correction.** The famous **five steps** — preparation, presentation, association,
generalisation, application — are **not Herbart's**. Ziller reworked the four; **Wilhelm Rein**
split Clearness into preparation + presentation and gave the five names (Ellerton & Clements 2005,
EC05, MERGA conference paper; consistent with encyclopaedia entries — secondary, Rein not read).
The rigid, scripted Herbartian lesson plan of the 1890s is the followers' system.

**Mechanism (Part A).** Clearness (one element at a time, nothing obscuring it) = **segmenting /
low extraneous load** (A2, CLT). Association = **elaboration**, linking the new to known items
(A6). Preparation (Rein) = **activating prior knowledge / advance organizer** (A6 §5, LAA80 —
small, old). Generalisation = abstraction from examples (A3 row 2). Application/Method = practice
and use (A4). **What is missing:** no retrieval after the lesson, no spacing, no checking whether
the learner got it — the sequence presents one lesson well; it says nothing about memory over time.

**Serves:** lesson structure across A3 rows 2–4 (a sequence, not a type-specific method).

**Grade:** **T** — a theory-driven lesson sequence, never tested as a package; its single steps
match evidenced moves (segmenting S under CLT; elaboration M; advance organizers M, small). Its
descendants with evidence — Gagné's nine events, Rosenshine's principles — added review, checks
for understanding and practice over time (→ B2, B4b).

**Use as learner.** Before connecting ideas, get each one clear *on its own* (can you state it
without the others?); then link them; then build the whole map; then use it on a problem.

**Use as teacher.** Herbart's order is a sound skeleton for one lesson. Add what it lacks: open
with retrieval of the last lesson, check understanding before moving on, and schedule the topic
to come back.

## Summary (S7a)

| Teacher | Core move | Serves (A3) | Grade (method / mechanism) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Socrates | commit → contradiction → rebuild by prompts | 3, 6 | M / pretesting S, self-explanation S, refutation M |
| Confucius | learn + think; review old; learner tries first; one corner → three | 3, 6, 1 | T / spacing S, generation S, transfer (A5) |
| Quintilian | model → imitate → own production → revise; accuracy before speed | 4, 8 | T / worked-example→fading S |
| Jesuit *Ratio* | daily/weekly/yearly recall from memory; daily correction | 1, 3 | T / retrieval + spacing S; rivalry not supported |
| Comenius | senses first; example + rule + reason; learn by teaching | 2, 1, 4 | T / worked examples S, teaching M (Ko19), multimedia M (No22) |
| Pestalozzi | sense-impression → simple elements → essential features | 2, 1 | T / concreteness fading M (`uncertain` size) |
| Herbart | clearness → association → system → method (Rein: 5 steps) | 2–4 (structure) | T / segmenting, elaboration; lacks retrieval & spacing |

**Pattern across S7a.** Every historical method that lasted contains *some* evidenced mechanism —
and every one also carries untested or contradicted parts (recollection theory, rivalry,
rereading, "more senses"). Longevity selects for methods that feel right to teachers, not for
methods tested on retention (A8 §3 kit, Q1–Q2). The useful habit: extract the mechanism, grade it
by Part A, drop the rest.

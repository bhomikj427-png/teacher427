## Full entries

### M1 — Time spent studying = learning
- **Why it feels true:** hours are visible and countable; effort feels like progress; schools and
  timetables reward time-on-task.
- **Correction:** studying is input; learning is "the more or less permanent change in knowledge
  or understanding", inferred later (BB11). Hours spent on low-yield methods (rereading) can
  produce performance with little learning (SB15, RK06).
- **Exposing question:** "If you sat 5 hours yesterday, what can you produce *now*, without notes,
  that you couldn't before?"
- **Unit:** A1 §1. Status: written.

### M2 — If it feels fluent, I've learned it
- **Why it feels true:** fluency (smooth reading, instant recognition) is a real signal; it just
  measures the wrong thing, *current retrieval strength* (BB92).
- **Correction:** fluency during study is a weak guide to long-term learning; conditions that
  feel harder often produce more lasting learning (BB11, SB15).
- **Exposing question:** "Would it still feel easy tomorrow, with the page closed, if the question
  were worded differently?"
- **Unit:** A1 §3, A7. Status: written.

### M3 — Forgetting means the session was wasted
- **Why it feels true:** blanking on something you studied feels like losing it.
- **Correction:** retrieval strength drops while storage strength persists (BB92); relearning is
  faster (savings, MD15). Partial forgetting makes the next retrieval a *bigger* learning event
  (BB92). This is why spacing works.
- **Exposing question:** "Second time through, did it take less time than the first?"
- **Unit:** A1 §3, A2 §5. Status: written.

### M4 — Working memory holds 7 ± 2 items
- **Why it feels true:** famous number (Miller 1956), repeated in textbooks; phone numbers seem to fit.
- **Correction:** 7 mixed in rehearsal and chunking; the core limit is about 4 chunks (C01). What
  grows with expertise is chunk *size*, not slot count.
- **Exposing question:** "Is a 7-digit phone number 7 items or 2–3 chunks for you?"
- **Unit:** A2 §2. Status: written.

### M11 — Retrieval practice only checks memory; it doesn't build understanding
- **Why it feels true:** tests are associated with *measuring*; flashcards often target isolated
  facts; popular framing ("active recall fights the forgetting curve", J4).
- **Correction:** retrieval is itself a learning event. In KB11 it beat concept mapping on
  inference questions and even on a concept-map final test; benefits transfer to new questions
  (PR18, d ≈ 0.40, `uncertain` exact). Scope caveat: depth of the *prompt* matters (A1 §5).
- **Exposing question:** "After free-recalling a chapter, could you answer an inference question
  you never practised?"
- **Unit:** A1 §5, A6. Status: written.

### M16 — Any difficulty is a "desirable difficulty"
- **Why it feels true:** "harder = better" is the slogan version of the research.
- **Correction:** difficulty helps only when it triggers successful encoding/retrieval; "if,
  however, the learner does not have the background knowledge or skills to respond to them
  successfully, they become undesirable difficulties" (BB11, verbatim). Confusion and noise are
  just difficulties.
- **Exposing question:** "Can the learner succeed at this with effort — or not at all yet?"
- **Unit:** A1 §4, A4. Status: written.

### M18 — If I can't recall it, I never learned it
- **Why it feels true:** recall failure is the only signal you can see.
- **Correction:** recall depends on retrieval strength; storage strength can remain high (BB92).
  Relearning shows savings (MD15). Cues can bring back "lost" items (TT73).
- **Exposing question:** "Can you recognise it, or relearn it much faster than the first time?"
- **Unit:** A1 §3. Status: written.

### M19 — Doing well right after studying proves the method works
- **Why it feels true:** immediate scores are real numbers, and they look like evidence.
- **Correction:** immediate tests favour massing and restudy; in RK06 restudy won at 5 minutes
  and lost at 2 days and 1 week (RK06 full text, S11a: Exp. 2 at 5 min 83/78/71%, at 1 week
  40/56/61% for SSSS/SSST/STTT). Only delayed tests measure learning (SB15).
- **Exposing question:** "What would the score be in a week?"
- **Unit:** A1 §2. Status: verified (2026-11-16, RK06 full text).

### M20 — For deep understanding, elaborating (e.g. concept mapping) beats testing yourself
- **Why it feels true:** elaboration *looks* deep (connections drawn, effort visible); retrieval
  looks like rote.
- **Correction:** KB11: retrieval practice beat elaborative concept mapping on verbatim and
  inference questions; students predicted the opposite. Elaboration still helps (A6), but it is not
  automatically superior. Combining (elaborate, then retrieve) is the open design question.
- **Exposing question:** "Which of the two produced more on a test a week later?"
- **Unit:** A1 §5, A6. Status: written.

### M21 — I can tell which study method works for me by how it feels
- **Why it feels true:** people trust their own experience; easy methods feel productive.
- **Correction:** KoB08: >80% rated massing equal or better while only 15% did better with it, and
  the judgment survived seeing their own results. Same pattern in KB11.
- **Exposing question:** "Have you ever compared the two methods on a delayed test, not by feel?"
- **Unit:** A1 §6, A7. Status: written.

### M22 — Brain-training games expand working memory and make you smarter
- **Why it feels true:** scores on the game improve steadily; marketing claims; "the brain is a muscle".
- **Correction:** WM training improves trained-like tasks briefly but shows no convincing transfer
  to reasoning, reading or arithmetic vs active controls (MLH13 + 2016 update). Expertise expands
  *effective* capacity through chunks (A5), not through slot training.
- **Exposing question:** "Did the training improve anything other than tasks that look like the training?"
- **Unit:** A2 §2. Status: written.

### M23 — You forget ~70% of everything within a day (the forgetting curve)
- **Why it feels true:** the Ebbinghaus curve is widely shown with dramatic percentages.
- **Correction:** the curve describes nonsense syllables learned once, by one person (Ebbinghaus;
  replicated N = 1, MD15). Meaningful, connected, retrieved material decays far more slowly; the
  curve is the worst case, not a law for everything.
- **Exposing question:** "Was the material meaningful, linked to what you know, and retrieved since?"
- **Unit:** A2 §5. Status: written.

### M24 — Memory works like a recording you play back
- **Why it feels true:** vivid memories feel like replays.
- **Correction:** retrieval is cue-dependent (TT73) and modifies the memory itself (A1 §5); memory
  is spread across multiple systems with different rules (Sq04). What you can get out depends on
  how it went in and what cues you have now.
- **Exposing question:** "Why can you recall it with one cue and not another, if it's a recording?"
- **Unit:** A2 §4. Status: written.

### M25 — Sleep is time lost from studying; all-nighters add study time
- **Why it feels true:** more waking hours = more input.
- **Correction:** sleep is when recent memories are reactivated and consolidated (RB13); waking is
  optimised for encoding, sleep for consolidation. Cutting sleep trades consolidation for more
  input that will not be kept. Spacing across nights uses this.
- **Exposing question:** "How much of last night's all-nighter material can you produce today, unaided?"
- **Unit:** A2 §4. Status: written.

### M7 — One best study method works for all material
- **Why it feels true:** "the best study technique" lists rank methods globally (even D13 rates
  techniques overall); one method that worked once gets generalized.
- **Correction:** Gagné: effects of learning variables "can be generalized within these categories
  but not between them" (G84). KLI: retrieval practice fits constant facts, example study fits
  variable skills (CRK22: concept-type × training-type interaction, d = 0.38, single study).
- **Exposing question:** "Is this a fact with one answer, or a skill where each problem is
  different?"
- **Unit:** A3 §2, §5; A4. Status: written.

### M26 — Memorizing facts is the opposite of understanding
- **Why it feels true:** rote cramming without meaning is real and useless; "understanding over
  memorization" is a common slogan.
- **Correction:** conceptual knowledge is defined as "the interrelationships among the basic
  elements" (K02). The relationships need the elements. Facts are the material understanding is
  built from; the failure is facts *left unconnected* (SOLO multistructural, BC82), not facts
  themselves.
- **Exposing question:** "Can you explain why KVL holds if you can't recall what a node and a
  loop are?"
- **Unit:** A3 §2.1. Status: written.

### M27 — If I understand how a procedure works, I can do it
- **Why it feels true:** following a worked solution feels like being able to produce one.
- **Correction:** skills start declarative (slow, step-by-step) and must be compiled into fluent
  condition→action rules through practice (A82; FP67 for motor skills). Understanding is the start
  of the trajectory, not the end.
- **Exposing question:** "Can you do a fresh one, start to finish, without the example open, at
  exam speed?"
- **Unit:** A3 §2.4. Status: written.

### M28 — Bloom's taxonomy is a strict ladder and "remember" is low-value
- **Why it feels true:** the pyramid graphic; "higher-order thinking" rhetoric.
- **Correction:** the revision relaxed the hierarchy: categories overlap, and some Understand
  processes (explaining) are more complex than some Apply processes (executing) (K02, p. 215).
  Remembering is the base the others operate on, not a lesser goal.
- **Exposing question:** "Can you analyze a circuit whose component laws you can't remember?"
- **Unit:** A3 §2.1. Status: written.

### M29 — Knowing a concept = being able to state its definition
- **Why it feels true:** tests often ask for definitions; reciting one feels like knowing.
- **Correction:** for Gagné, concepts are intellectual skills, shown by applying them to new
  instances (G84). In KLI, category knowledge has a *variable* application condition: it must
  fire on cases never seen before (CRK22). A definition is a fact *about* the concept.
- **Exposing question:** "Here are five new circuits; which connections are in series?"
- **Unit:** A3 §2.2, §3 row 2. Status: written.

### M30 — If it were taught right, it would come as naturally as learning to talk
- **Why it feels true:** speech and face recognition are effortless, so effortful learning feels
  like a teaching failure.
- **Correction:** language is biologically primary; school and university content is secondary.
  It is culturally recent, effortful, constrained by working memory, and needs explicit instruction
  (G08; `contested` as a theory but widely used in cognitive-load research).
- **Exposing question:** "Did anyone ever need to be taught to talk? Did anyone ever learn calculus
  without being taught?"
- **Unit:** A3 §4. Status: written.

### M6 — Rereading and highlighting are good study techniques
- **Why it feels true:** they are easy, feel productive, and raise fluency (A1 §6).
- **Correction:** rated *low utility* (D13); restudy loses to retrieval on delayed tests (RK06).
  They raise retrieval strength briefly without much storage gain (BB92).
- **Exposing question:** "After the reread, close the book: what can you produce?"
- **Unit:** A4 §1. Status: written.

### M8 — Experts just have better memories / are smarter
- **Why it feels true:** experts recall and solve things that look impossible to novices.
- **Correction:** chess masters' advantage vanishes on random boards (CS73). Their edge is
  domain-specific chunks, and they represent problems by deep principle rather than surface (CFG81).
- **Exposing question:** "Would the expert still win on a random arrangement with no meaningful pattern?"
- **Unit:** A5 §1–2. Status: written.

### M9 — If you learned it, you can use it anywhere
- **Why it feels true:** understanding feels general.
- **Correction:** far transfer is hard and depends on content and context distance (BC02). Even with
  the analogy in memory, people rarely notice it without a hint (GH80).
- **Exposing question:** "Have you ever used this outside the type of problem you practised it on?"
- **Unit:** A5 §3. Status: written.

### M17 — Novices learn best by discovering things themselves
- **Why it feels true:** discovery feels deep and memorable; "learning by doing" sounds right.
- **Correction:** minimally guided instruction is less effective and efficient for novices (KSC06).
  Productive failure works only when attempts are designed *and followed by instruction*, and it
  reverses for young learners (SK21). Scaffolded inquiry ≠ minimal guidance (2007 rebuttals).
- **Exposing question:** "After the struggle, who teaches the canonical solution, and when?"
- **Unit:** A4 §3. Status: written.

### M31 — Worked examples are spoon-feeding; real learners should solve problems from the start
- **Why it feels true:** solving feels active; studying a solution feels passive.
- **Correction:** for novices, worked examples beat unguided problem solving (worked-example effect;
  KACS03; CRK22 for skills). The reverse holds once the learner is fluent (expertise reversal), so
  the answer depends on level.
- **Exposing question:** "Can the learner already do these steps unaided? If not, why make them guess?"
- **Unit:** A4 §1, §3. Status: written.

### M32 — Interleaving always beats blocking
- **Why it feels true:** it is a famous desirable difficulty.
- **Correction:** BR19: g = 0.42 overall but strongly moderated. It is best when categories are
  similar to each other and complex; maths effects are small; some materials show no or reversed
  benefit. For novices: block first, then mix (BB11 limit on difficulty).
- **Exposing question:** "Can the learner already do each type on its own?"
- **Unit:** A4 §1. Status: written.

### M33 — Productive failure means letting students struggle without help
- **Why it feels true:** the name emphasizes failure.
- **Correction:** the evidence is for *problem-solving followed by instruction* (SK21, g = 0.36).
  The struggle is designed, and the instruction that follows builds on students' attempts. Remove
  the instruction and it becomes minimal guidance (KSC06).
- **Exposing question:** "What happens straight after the struggle?"
- **Unit:** A4 §1, §3. Status: written.

### M34 — 10,000 hours of practice makes anyone an expert
- **Why it feels true:** popular books; every expert practised a lot.
- **Correction:** deliberate practice explained 26% of performance variance in games, 21% music,
  18% sports, 4% education, <1% professions (MHO14; operationalization disputed by Ericsson et al.).
  Practice is necessary, not sufficient, and its *quality* matters more than hours.
- **Exposing question:** "Was the practice targeted at weaknesses, with feedback, at the edge of ability?"
- **Unit:** A5 §4. Status: written.

### M35 — Once you've seen an example, you'll recognise when to use it
- **Why it feels true:** the example felt clear and memorable.
- **Correction:** in GH80, most people did not apply an analogous solution they had just read
  until hinted. Retrieval needs a matching cue (TT73). Comparing two or more analogs builds an
  abstract schema that new problems can trigger (GH83 full text: two analogs + comparison → 45%
  unprompted transfer vs 21% with one analog).
- **Exposing question:** "Would you recognise the same principle in a problem that looks completely different?"
- **Unit:** A5 §3. Status: verified (2026-11-16, GH83 full text; GH80 numbers via GH83 recap).

### M36 — Experts follow the same steps as novices, just faster
- **Why it feels true:** expert performance looks like sped-up novice performance.
- **Correction:** experts represent problems differently, by deep principle (CFG81), with larger
  chunks (CS73), and their procedures are compiled (A82). Copying an expert's speed without their
  representation fails.
- **Exposing question:** "How does the expert *describe* the problem before solving it?"
- **Unit:** A5 §2. Status: written.

### M10 — Wrong guesses before studying hurt learning
- **Why it feels true:** "errorless learning" intuition — a wrong answer might get stored and
  compete with the right one.
- **Correction:** guessing wrongly and then seeing the answer beat simply reading the answer for
  the same time (KHB09, six experiments; RKK09 with a science text). Prequestions improve learning
  of the asked items, g = 0.54 (SCA24, k = 97). Condition: the correct answer must follow.
- **Exposing question:** "After your wrong guess, did you see the right answer — and did you
  remember it better because you'd been wrong?"
- **Unit:** A6 §5. Status: written.

### M12 — Mind maps always beat linear notes
- **Why it feels true:** maps look organized and show the big picture at a glance.
- **Correction:** *constructing* a concept map helps (g = 0.72), *studying* a given one less
  (g = 0.43) (Sch17), mostly against weak comparisons such as reading. Retrieval practice beat
  map construction even on inference questions (KB11). The benefit comes from generating the
  links, not from the map format.
- **Exposing question:** "Could you redraw the map from memory, with the reasons on each link?"
- **Unit:** A6 §4. Status: written.

### M37 — Active learning means being busy: highlighting, copying, rewriting notes
- **Why it feels true:** your hands are moving and the page changes, so it feels like work.
- **Correction:** in ICAP these are *active* (manipulating), which integrates information into
  what you already know but generates nothing new. *Constructive* activities (explaining, comparing,
  asking, mapping) go beyond the material and predict more learning (CW14). Highlighting is rated
  low utility (D13).
- **Exposing question:** "Did you produce anything that wasn't already on the page?"
- **Unit:** A6 §3. Status: written.

### M38 — Pre-reading or "priming" a chapter makes all of it stick better
- **Why it feels true:** after a preview the chapter feels familiar, and familiarity feels like a head start.
- **Correction:** prequestions help the specific items asked (g = 0.54) but have essentially no
  effect on the rest of the material (g = 0.04, k = 91; SCA24). The attention they create is
  targeted. Familiarity from skimming is fluency (A1), not learning.
- **Exposing question:** "Which questions did your pre-study ask — and were those the ones you improved on?"
- **Unit:** A6 §5. Status: written.

### M39 — Deeper processing always helps, whatever the material and the test
- **Why it feels true:** "deep" sounds better than "shallow" in every case.
- **Correction:** processing helps when it matches what the test needs (transfer-appropriate
  processing, `uncertain` at source). Explaining *why* gave no benefit for arbitrary rules (English
  articles, Wylie 2011 in CW14). Learners with no relevant schema cannot infer (CW14). Shallow tests
  show no difference between modes (CW14). Generating nonwords gave d = .05 (Be07).
- **Exposing question:** "Is there a reason behind this fact that you could actually work out?"
- **Unit:** A6 §1, §3. Status: written.

### M40 — Studying a good mind map someone else made is as useful as making your own
- **Why it feels true:** the finished map holds all the same links, organized better than yours would be.
- **Correction:** constructing g = 0.72 vs studying a given map g = 0.43 (Sch17). The gain comes
  from deciding the links yourself (generation, Be07; ICAP constructive, CW14). A given map is
  mainly a passive or active resource.
- **Exposing question:** "Which links in the map did *you* have to work out?"
- **Unit:** A6 §4. Status: written.

### M41 — Rating how well I know it right after studying tells me what I'll remember
- **Why it feels true:** right after study the answer is vivid; it really is known *now*.
- **Correction:** immediate judgments read working memory and current retrieval strength.
  Judging after a delay, by attempting recall, is far more accurate at separating known from
  unknown items (relative accuracy g = 0.93, RT11). Overconfident judgments lead to stopping too
  early (DR12). The delay helps most when the judgment is made from the cue alone, so the learner
  has to reach for the answer; and the judgment still has to be followed by producing the answer
  to count as practice (TR21 full text; M87).
- **Exposing question:** "If you close the book and wait ten minutes, can you still produce it?"
- **Unit:** A7 §3. Status: written (S11b-2-rest: RT11 read at abstract only, DR12 unread — not
  verified; backlog S13).

### M42 — Getting an item right once means I can stop studying it
- **Why it feels true:** a correct answer is proof — of something.
- **Correction:** one correct recall in a session shows current retrieval strength (A1 §3).
  Successive relearning — criterion recall repeated in later sessions — is what improved course
  exams and long-term retention (RDS13): exam scores >10 points over baseline, and 24 days later
  >60% vs <20% recall (RDS13 Exp. 1, as reported in the authors' own full-text guide DGBWR23).
- **Exposing question:** "Did you get it right on a *different day* than you studied it?"
- **Unit:** A7 §4. Status: verified (2026-11-16, via DGBWR23 full text; RDS13 itself at abstract).

### M43 — Metacognition means reflecting a lot; more reflection = better learning
- **Why it feels true:** "thinking about your thinking" sounds like general reflection, and reflecting feels responsible.
- **Correction:** what the evidence supports is specific: accurate *monitoring* (delayed,
  retrieval-based judgments, RT11) feeding *control* decisions (what to restudy, when to stop;
  DR12), inside a plan → do → judge cycle (Z02). General reflection with no test and no changed
  decision does not fix the cues that mislead monitoring (BDK13).
- **Exposing question:** "What did your reflection make you *do differently* in the next session?"
- **Unit:** A7 §1, §5. Status: written.

### M44 — Being able to manage your own studying is a trait you have or don't
- **Why it feels true:** some classmates seem naturally organized and self-aware.
- **Correction:** self-regulation is a set of learnable steps (forethought, performance,
  self-reflection; Z02), and school training programmes improve achievement (DB08; size
  `uncertain` until full text). Calibration improves with a concrete procedure (delayed
  self-tests, RT11).
- **Exposing question:** "Which of the three phases did you actually do this week?"
- **Unit:** A7 §5. Status: written (correction size `uncertain`).

### M5 — People learn best in their "learning style"
- **Why it feels true:** preferences are real, and comfort feels like learning; 93–96% of surveyed
  teachers believed it (Dek12).
- **Correction:** the claim needs a crossover interaction (style-A learners gain more from method A,
  style-B from method B). Reviews found virtually none, and some studies contradict it (P08).
  The format should fit the *content* (A3 row) and prior knowledge, not the person.
- **Exposing question:** "Has anyone shown that *your* style group learned more with the matched
  method and *less* with the other one — on a real test?"
- **Unit:** A8 §2 myth 1. Status: written.

### M13 — The "Feynman Technique" was Feynman's method
- **Why it feels true:** it carries his name and suits his reputation as a great explainer.
- **Correction:** the named four-step method appears around 2011 in Scott Young's writing (SY-FT);
  no Feynman text describing it was found. **S10b-1: settled** — Young's own 2011 transcript gives
  the steps and names them after a Feynman anecdote (SY-FT11); in 2024 he calls it "the most
  popular piece of studying advice I've ever come up with", "only loosely based on Richard Feynman's practices" (SY-FT24). The method's parts
  are well-evidenced anyway: self-explanation (Bi18), teaching as generative learning (FM16). Its
  popularizer warns it does not replace practice problems.
- **Exposing question:** "Where did Feynman write these steps down?"
- **Unit:** A8 §2 myth 7; B5 §2. Status: written (attribution `settled` S10b-1).

### M14 — The learning pyramid ("we remember 10% of what we read … 90% of what we teach")
- **Why it feels true:** tidy numbers; its message (doing beats watching) is partly right.
- **Correction:** versions have circulated for over 160 years and did not come from research; the
  percentages have no data behind them (LH18). The true part — constructive and interactive
  activity beats passive receiving — is supported by ICAP without any percentages (CW14).
- **Exposing question:** "Which study measured those percentages, with what test?"
- **Unit:** A8 §2 myth 3. Status: written.

### M45 — People are left-brained (logical) or right-brained (creative)
- **Why it feels true:** some functions really do lean to one side (language mostly left), and
  personality labels are appealing.
- **Correction:** in 1,011 brain scans, lateralization was local to particular networks and present
  in everyone; there was no whole-brain "left-brained" or "right-brained" type (N13, full text:
  "a local rather than global property of brain networks").
- **Exposing question:** "If language is left-sided in almost everyone, what would a
  'right-brained' person use to talk?"
- **Unit:** A8 §2 myth 2. Status: verified (2026-11-16, N13 full text).

### M46 — I can study fine while texting or watching something
- **Why it feels true:** you keep reading and the page still makes sense, so nothing seems lost.
- **Correction:** dividing attention while encoding causes large memory losses; dividing it during
  retrieval mostly costs speed (CGNA96). Feeling that it makes sense is performance, not storage (A1).
- **Exposing question:** "Tomorrow, without notes, what can you recall from the pages you read with
  the phone on?"
- **Unit:** A8 §2 myth 4. Status: written.

### M47 — The Pomodoro technique makes you learn more
- **Why it feels true:** timed sprints feel focused, and breaks reduce tiredness.
- **Correction:** the evidence (32 studies, N = 5,270) mainly measured self-rated focus, fatigue and
  motivation, not delayed learning (Og25; full text: 3 of the 32 were RCTs, and academic gains were
  "typically measured through subjective assessments and self-reported measures"; one cited
  experiment reported higher test scores — not read). It may help you sit down and keep going; it is not a
  learning method. What you *do* in the 25 minutes decides the learning (A4).
- **Exposing question:** "What did the studies measure — your feeling of focus, or what you could
  recall a week later?"
- **Unit:** A8 §2 myth 5. Status: verified (2026-11-16, Og25 full text).

### M48 — Teaching students a growth mindset reliably raises their grades
- **Why it feels true:** the message is hopeful, and believing effort matters seems obviously useful.
- **Correction:** across 43 studies, interventions gave d = 0.08 overall and about zero for
  middle- and upper-SES students; larger effects for low-SES (d = 0.34, 7 effect sizes) (Sis18).
  A national RCT found gains for lower-achieving students where peer norms supported the message
  (Y19 full text: +0.10 grade points, SMD 0.11; higher achievers 0.01 ns). Small and conditional,
  not a general lever.
- **Exposing question:** "For which students, and how big was the gain?"
- **Unit:** A8 §2 myth 6. Status: verified (2026-11-16, Sis18 + Y19 full text).

### M87 — Asking myself "do I know this?" (without producing the answer) is the same as testing myself
- **Why it feels true:** both happen with the book closed, and in both you reach into memory; the
  judgment even comes with a feeling of having "checked".
- **Correction:** a delayed judgment made from the cue alone is a good *monitor* (RT11), but as a
  learning event it is weak: in RT11's data cue-only delayed judgments gave almost no memory gain
  (g = 0.03), and in TR21's experiments (word pairs, 2-day cued recall) they gave **poorer recall
  than overtly retrieving the answer** (Exp. 2a/2b). The covert "yes, I know it" may never fetch
  the whole answer.
- **Exposing question:** "When you said 'I know it', did you actually say or write the answer?"
- **Unit:** A7 §3. Status: verified (2026-11-16, TR21 full text; word pairs only).

### M88 — Once a test shows me which study method worked better, I'll switch to it
- **Why it feels true:** people assume that seeing their own result is the strongest possible
  feedback.
- **Correction:** in KoB08, spacing beat massing for most learners, yet more than 80% rated
  massing as equal or better *after* the test (only 15% actually did better massed). The feeling
  of fluency during massed study outweighed the result. Monitoring illusions survive feedback
  (BDK13); the fix is a rule decided in advance (delayed self-tests, criterion recall), not a
  hope that the result will change the feeling.
- **Exposing question:** "Your test said spacing won — which method did you *choose* for the next
  chapter, and why?"
- **Unit:** A7 §2. Status: written (KoB08 at abstract).

### M89 — Any study-skills programme works the same way, at any age
- **Why it feels true:** "learning how to learn" sounds like one general skill, so one course
  should suit everyone.
- **Correction:** self-regulation is trainable (DB08), but what made training work differed by
  school level: in primary school, programmes built on social-cognitive theory that also taught
  metacognitive and motivational strategies did better; in secondary school, programmes built on
  metacognitive theory with reflection did better — yet so did ones teaching cognitive rather than
  metacognitive strategies (DB08 p. 246, quoted in Pan17). The picture is mixed; the overall size
  is not readable here (`uncertain`).
- **Exposing question:** "Was this programme tested with learners like you, and which parts of it
  did the work?"
- **Unit:** A7 §5. Status: written (DB08 moderators via Pan17 full text; DB08 unread).

### M90 — Knowing a lot about the brain protects you from neuromyths
- **Why it feels true:** myths look like ignorance, so more knowledge should cure them.
- **Correction:** among 242 UK and Dutch teachers interested in brain science, more general
  brain knowledge *predicted more* belief in neuromyths, not less (Dek12, full text). Facts about
  the brain are not the same as the habit of asking for the evidence (A8 §3 test kit).
- **Exposing question:** "What study would you need to see before believing this brain claim?"
- **Unit:** A8 §1. Status: verified (2026-11-16, Dek12 full text; self-selected sample).

### M91 — Coordination exercises (e.g. Brain Gym) "integrate the hemispheres" and boost learning
- **Why it feels true:** movement breaks can feel refreshing, the programmes are sold with
  brain diagrams, and 82–88% of the teachers in Dek12 believed it.
- **Correction:** a review of Brain Gym's theory and its peer-reviewed studies "failed to support
  the contentions of the promoters" (Hy07). The mechanism also conflicts with brain data: the
  hemispheres are not separate person-level systems waiting to be joined (N13).
- **Exposing question:** "Which controlled study compared these exercises with an ordinary break,
  on a learning test?"
- **Unit:** A8 §2 (Brain Gym note). Status: written (Hy07 at abstract; N13 full text).

### M92 — About 80% success during practice is a proven optimal rate for learning
- **Why it feels true:** it is a precise number, it is repeated by trusted sources (Rosenshine;
  this engine's principle 4), and the idea behind it (hard but mostly successful) is sound.
- **Correction:** the classroom 80% comes from process-product research — effective teachers'
  classes happened to show about 82% vs 73% success — which is **correlational**, not a tested
  optimum (Ros12, full text). The one formal result, the "85% rule", is a model of
  gradient-descent learners on two-choice tasks; it shifts to 82% or 75% under other noise
  assumptions and was not tested with human students (Wi19, full text). Keep the principle; hold
  the number loosely.
- **Exposing question:** "Was the 80% set by an experiment, or observed in classes that were
  doing well?"
- **Unit:** A8 §3 item 4 (B4b §1). Status: verified (2026-11-16, Ros12 + Wi19 full text).

### M93 — A big, pre-registered trial with a "significant" effect means the effect is large
- **Why it feels true:** "national", "pre-registered" and "significant" all sound like strength,
  and they are strengths — of the *design*.
- **Correction:** rigour tells you the effect is probably real, not that it is big. The national
  growth-mindset RCT (Y19, full text) found +0.10 grade points (SMD 0.11) for lower-achieving
  students and 0.01 (ns) for higher achievers. "Significant" means "unlikely to be zero"; the size
  and *for whom* are separate questions.
- **Exposing question:** "How much did the average student's grade move, and for which students?"
- **Unit:** A8 §3 item 5. Status: verified (2026-11-16, Y19 full text).


### M49 — The Socratic method means the teacher never tells, only asks
- **Why it feels true:** in the *Meno* Socrates says "I am not teaching the boy anything, but only
  asking him questions", and questioning feels more respectful and active than lecturing.
- **Correction:** his questions are heavily leading and carry the structure of the proof; the boy
  already had the needed parts (counting, seeing squares). Prompting can replace explaining only
  when the learner has the pieces: in CSJ01, prompt-only tutoring matched explaining tutoring for
  students who could construct; for novices, minimal guidance underperforms explicit instruction
  (KSC06). What is evidenced is the *parts*: commit first (SCA24), refute the wrong belief (SK22,
  g = 0.41), self-explain (Bi18).
- **Exposing question:** "Could the learner have answered your questions if they knew none of the
  pieces? Who supplied the diagonal?"
- **Unit:** B1 §1. Status: verified (CSJ01 + SK22 full text, Meno primary — S12a).

### M50 — A method used for centuries is proven by its longevity
- **Why it feels true:** surviving for hundreds of years looks like a long field test; famous
  schools and pupils seem to vouch for it.
- **Correction:** methods survive because teachers find them workable and they feel right, and the
  famous pupils were selected (survivorship). None of the S7a methods was tested on retention or
  transfer as a package; each mixes evidenced parts (the Jesuit retrieval ladder) with unsupported
  ones (public rivalry, Comenius's morning-and-evening rereading, Socrates' recollection theory).
  Grade the mechanism by Part A (A8 §3 kit: Q1 outcome, Q2 comparison).
- **Exposing question:** "Compared with what, and measured how — a week later?"
- **Unit:** B1 §summary. Status: verified (Pl-Meno, RS1599, Comenius primaries read in full; S12a).

### M51 — Concrete, hands-on, realistic materials are always better than abstract ones
- **Why it feels true:** concrete objects are easy to grasp and engaging; "learning by doing" and
  Pestalozzi's sense-impression sound obviously right.
- **Correction:** concrete materials help as a *start*; the target is the abstract idea. Concreteness
  fading moves concrete → iconic → abstract and makes the link explicit (FN18); a concept that stays
  tied to its concrete form need not transfer. Pestalozzi himself warned that single views mislead
  about the "essential" features — the evidenced remedy is varied, contrasted examples (A4 §2).
  Bound (BG13, abstract): concrete-only instruction transferred worse than concrete → formal fading,
  and fading only *tied* formal-only — concrete is a starting point, not a superior end state.
- **Exposing question:** "Can you solve it when the blocks are gone and the symbols are all you have?"
- **Unit:** B1 §6 (also B2 Bruner, B1 §10 Montessori). Status: verified (FN18 author full text; BG13 abstract — S12a).

### M52 — Herbart invented the five-step lesson plan
- **Why it feels true:** the steps are taught as "Herbart's five formal steps" in teacher-training texts.
- **Correction:** Herbart wrote four (Clearness, Association, System, Method — *Science of Education*,
  Felkin tr.); Ziller reworked them and Rein split Clearness into preparation + presentation (EC05; confirmed at Rein's own *Outlines*,
  RV1893, which also warns against running all five steps in each single lesson).
  The rigid scripted plan is the Herbartians'. More important than the name: neither version
  includes retrieval, spacing or checks for understanding.
- **Exposing question:** "Where in the five steps does the learner recall anything a week later?"
- **Unit:** B1 §7. Status: verified (Herb-SE + Rein RV1893 primary — S12a).


### M53 — Scaffolding (contingent tutoring in the ZPD) is a proven effect
- **Why it feels true:** "ZPD" and "scaffolding" are in every teacher-training course; helping more
  when the learner struggles and less when they succeed sounds obviously right.
- **Correction:** the ZPD is Vygotsky's developmental frame, not a technique; "scaffolding" comes from
  WBR76 (one tutor, young children). The contingent-tutoring experiment (WWM78, 32 children) failed a
  preregistered direct replication with 285 children (Sm25: no advantage, Bayes factors favour null).
  Reviews find few effectiveness studies and measurement problems (VVB10). What is evidenced is
  **fading** support as competence grows (worked examples → independent practice, A4 S).
- **Exposing question:** "Which experiment shows contingent help beats other help, and has it been replicated?"
- **Unit:** B1 §9. Status: verified (Sm25 full text — S12a).

### M54 — Learning by doing means discovery without being told — that is what Dewey said, and it works best
- **Why it feels true:** "learning by doing" is Dewey's slogan; doing feels more engaging than listening.
- **Correction:** Dewey's own essentials of method require that the learner "possess the information
  and make the observations needed" and that the problem be "sufficiently connected with existing
  habits" (Dew-DE). Within inquiry, guidance improves outcomes (LH16, d = 0.50); minimal guidance
  underperforms explicit instruction for novices (KSC06). LH16's own abstract frames inquiry as
  beating expository teaching only "as long as students are supported adequately".
- **Exposing question:** "Where does the learner get the information the problem needs?"
- **Unit:** B1 §11. Status: verified (Dew-DE full text; LH16 + KSC06 at abstract — S12a).

### M55 — Teaching general problem-solving strategies makes you a better problem-solver in any subject
- **Why it feels true:** Pólya's heuristics (work backwards, special cases) look universal, and experts
  visibly use them.
- **Correction:** taught as general skills, heuristics did not transfer to new domains (Wilson 1967;
  Smith 1973 — via Sch92); they are "descriptive rather than prescriptive" and each is a family of
  domain-specific moves. What helped was **control** (monitoring, abandoning dead ends), trained over
  extended time inside a domain (Sch92). Transfer rides on domain knowledge (A5).
- **Exposing question:** "Did the strategy work on a problem type you had never practised it on?"
- **Unit:** B1 §15. Status: verified (Sch92 full text — S12a).

### M56 — Good instruction is errorless — learners should never be allowed to make mistakes
- **Why it feels true:** errors feel like failure and might be "learned"; programmed instruction (Sk58)
  was designed so learners move on "without having made a wrong response."
- **Correction:** attempting an answer *before* learning it — including wrong attempts — improves
  learning of those items when correct feedback follows (KHB09; pretesting SCA24, g = 0.54 on asked
  items). Easy, heavily prompted responses give high performance with little retrieval effort
  (learning ≠ performance, A1). Keep the feedback, drop the errorlessness.
- **Exposing question:** "If they never get anything wrong, how hard were they actually retrieving?"
- **Unit:** B1 §10. Status: verified (Sk58 + SCA24 full text — S12a).

### M15 — One-to-one tutoring reliably gives a 2-sigma gain
- **Why it feels true:** Bloom's 1984 paper is famous, the number is memorable ("above 98% of the
  class"), and anyone who has had a good tutor felt the difference.
- **Correction:** the 2σ came from two dissertations, grades 4–8, **11 lessons over 3 weeks**,
  tests made for the study, and the tutoring condition **also** ran mastery-learning
  feedback-correctives (Bl84, full text). Later reviews: human tutoring d = 0.79 vs no tutoring
  (VL11, about as good as step-based computer tutors at 0.76; the 0.79 rests on only 10 effects —
  VL11-sl table); preK–12 randomized tutoring programmes pooled **0.29 SD** (NOQ24). Tutoring
  works — at roughly a seventh of the famous size at scale.
- **Exposing question:** "How long did the 2-sigma study run, on what test, and what else did the
  tutored group get?"
- **Unit:** B2 §2. Status: verified (Bl84 full text; VL11 table via author slides — S12b).

### M57 — Mastery learning reliably gives about a 1-sigma gain in ordinary schools
- **Why it feels true:** Bloom reported ≈1σ for mastery learning beside the 2σ for tutoring;
  early metas reported means of 0.5–0.9.
- **Correction:** in group-based mastery learning in real schools over ≥ 4 weeks, the median
  effect on **standardized** tests was **+.04** (7 studies) and on experimenter-made tests **+.255**
  (8 studies), with little evidence of maintenance (Sl87, full text). Larger effects came from
  short studies with generous corrective time and aligned tests. Mastery *mechanisms* (test →
  correct → retest) are well supported; the 1σ figure is not.
- **Exposing question:** "Was that effect on a standardized test or a test written for the study?"
- **Unit:** B2 §2. Status: verified (Sl87 full text — S12b).

### M58 — Advance organizers are a strong, proven way to start a lesson
- **Why it feels true:** Ausubel's principle — learning depends on what the learner already knows
  — is true, and "give them the big picture first" sounds like an application of it.
- **Correction:** the meta-analytic effect of advance organizers is **small** (LAA80, 135 studies),
  and does not follow the theory's prediction about low-knowledge learners (Stone); Bloom's own
  table put them at .20 (Bl84). Opening with **questions the learner must answer** (pretesting,
  SCA24 g = 0.54 on asked items) has stronger evidence than opening with an overview.
- **Exposing question:** "Did the learners *do* anything with the overview, or just read it?"
- **Unit:** B2 §4 (evidence at A6 §5). Status: written (S12b: stays — "small" rests on LAA80 /
  Stone abstracts; numeric ES carried to S13).

### M59 — Direct Instruction means lecturing — the teacher talks, students listen
- **Why it feels true:** "direct" and "instruction" suggest transmission; critics use "direct
  instruction" for any teacher-led lesson.
- **Correction:** Engelmann's DI is built on constant learner **responses** (choral and
  individual answers every few seconds), example/non-example sequences, immediate correction and
  mastery checks (St18 distinguishes DI from "little di"). Its effect on achievement is moderate to
  large (St18: 0.54–0.60, developer-affiliated authors). A lecture with no responses has none of
  those mechanisms.
- **Exposing question:** "How many times per minute does each student answer in this lesson?"
- **Unit:** B2 §6. Status: verified (St18 full text — S12b).

### M60 — Letting every learner go at their own pace is always better
- **Why it feels true:** learners differ in speed, and forced pacing leaves some behind and bores
  others; self-paced courses (PSI, Kumon, online platforms) sound respectful.
- **Correction:** self-paced mastery programmes "often reduce the completion rates in college
  classes" (KKB90, abstract) — the mastery and feedback parts help, the absence of deadlines lets
  procrastination win. Kumon's RCT ran a fixed 30 minutes daily inside school time (SMS24): *level*
  was individual, *schedule* was not.
- **Exposing question:** "Self-paced on what — level, or schedule? Who sets the deadline?"
- **Unit:** B2 §1. Status: written (S12b: stays — the completion finding is KKB90's abstract
  sentence; full text not reachable).

### M61 — A famous teacher's results prove that their method works
- **Why it feels true:** the results are real and dramatic (18/18 AP passes; a school off
  probation), and a vivid story feels like evidence.
- **Correction:** a single teacher is a case with no comparison group, selection (we hear about
  the successes), and a method that cannot be separated from the person. Garfield's AP passes
  fell after Escalante and his principal left; Beidler's reading gain under Marva Collins went
  10.2% → 32.5% → 13.5% (Chicago Reporter, 2005) — and a rise from a very low base partly
  rebounds anyway (regression to the mean). Treat the legend as a pointer to mechanisms (daily
  retrieval, practice time), then check those in controlled studies (A4).
- **Exposing question:** "Compared with whom — and did it last after the teacher left?"
- **Unit:** B3 (all). Status: verified (CSM88 + CR05 full text — S12b).

### M62 — Escalante's students succeeded because he believed in them — high expectations are the method
- **Why it feels true:** *Stand and Deliver* centres on his belief in the students; "high
  expectations" is the moral most retellings draw.
- **Correction:** the expectations were enforced by a practice regime — contracts for daily
  quizzes, weekly exams, 90 minutes of nightly study, homework checked at the door, extra hours
  (CSM 1988). Those are retrieval practice, spacing and time on task (A4 S). Expectation
  effects on their own: a 35-year review concludes classroom self-fulfilling prophecies are real
  but "typically small", do not build up much over time, and that teacher expectations predict
  results mostly because they are **accurate** (JH05, abstract); in 18 IQ-expectancy experiments,
  the better teachers already knew the pupils, the smaller the effect (Ra84, abstract). The
  mechanism with evidence is the practice. (Possible larger effects for stigmatized groups —
  JH05 — are a real caveat for Garfield's students, not a substitute for the practice regime.)
- **Exposing question:** "What exactly did his students *do* every day and every night?"
- **Unit:** B3 §2. Status: written (S12b: correction now sourced at JH05 + Ra84 abstracts; full
  texts carried to S13).

### M63 — Time spent on a learning platform (videos, Khan Academy) is learning; such platforms are proven to raise scores a lot
- **Why it feels true:** platforms report minutes and badges; vendor reports show users gaining
  more than non-users; videos feel clear.
- **Correction:** the randomized trial of Khan Academy replacing one weekly maths class in 157
  Brazilian schools found **no average effect** on proficiency (FFL20); the best real-world causal
  estimate is **+0.031 SD at ≈11 min/week, projected +0.085 SD at 30 min/week**, scaling with
  *practice* time, with Khan Academy co-authors (EB26). Vendor "users gained more" reports are
  correlational: motivated students both use more and gain more. Watching is passive; answered
  practice items are the active ingredient.
- **Exposing question:** "Was that a randomized comparison — and is it minutes watched or items
  answered?"
- **Unit:** B3 §4. Status: verified (FFL20 full text; EB26 numbers labelled abstract — S12b).

### M64 — Multiple-choice quizzes can't help learning — only recall tests or essays count
- **Why it feels true:** recognition is easier than recall, and A1/A4 stress that *producing* the
  answer is what builds memory.
- **Correction:** in real classrooms, low-stakes **multiple-choice quizzes with feedback** raised
  unit-exam scores by 13–25% for quizzed content, with benefits persisting to end-of-year exams
  (McD11), and improved later **short-answer** exam performance too (RAMM11). Free recall is often
  stronger, but well-built MC quizzes followed by feedback are a real retrieval event.
- **Exposing question:** "Did the quiz require choosing between plausible answers — and did
  feedback follow?"
- **Unit:** B4a §2. Status: written.

### M65 — If I can recall it once, I've learned it — move on
- **Why it feels true:** one correct recall feels like proof; carrying on seems wasteful.
- **Correction:** one correct recall at the end of a session says retrieval strength is high
  *now* (A1). RD11 (533 students, retention 1–4 months later): reach **3 correct recalls** in the
  first session, then **relearn 3 times at widely spaced intervals**; relearning gave "pronounced
  effects on long-term retention with a relatively minimal cost."
- **Exposing question:** "Could you recall it next month without having seen it in between?"
- **Unit:** B4a §3. Status: written.

### M66 — Interleaving means only mixed practice, from the very first lesson
- **Why it feels true:** "mixing beats blocking" is the headline of the interleaving research.
- **Correction:** in Rohrer's classroom studies the new skill was still introduced in its lesson;
  interleaving rearranged a **portion of the practice problems** across earlier units (RDS15,
  RDHC20). In RDHC20 (full text) that was nine 8-problem worksheets over four months in 54
  seventh-grade classes — 60.7% vs 37.6% on a test a month later (d = 0.83). Novices need a few
  blocked examples to form the method before they can choose between methods (engine rule: block
  first, then interleave; BR19 moderators). Interleaving helps the *choice*; it does not replace
  learning the procedure.
- **Exposing question:** "Can the learner already do each method on its own?"
- **Unit:** B4a §4. Status: verified (2026-11-16, RDHC20 + BR19 full text).

### M67 — Formative assessment reliably raises achievement by 0.4–0.7 SD
- **Why it feels true:** the figure is repeated in training courses and attributed to Black &
  Wiliam; checking understanding obviously helps.
- **Correction:** Kingston & Nash 2011 found only 13 of 300+ studies usable; 42 effect sizes,
  median 0.25, weighted mean **0.20** (ELA 0.32, maths 0.17, science 0.09) (KN11). Bennett 2011:
  the quantitative claims derive "from untraceable, flawed, dated, or unpublished sources" (Ben11).
  The components (feedback on task and process, every-student retrieval) are well supported; the
  big number for the label is not.
- **Exposing question:** "Which meta-analysis gives 0.7, and how many studies in it measured
  achievement with a comparison group?"
- **Unit:** B4b §6. Status: written.

### M68 — Productive failure contradicts cognitive load theory — one of them must be wrong
- **Why it feels true:** CLT says novices learn best from worked examples (KSC06); Kapur says
  struggle first. They look like opposite advice.
- **Correction:** they answer different questions. Worked examples win for **procedures** with
  true novices (A4 row 4); productive failure (SK21, g = 0.36) helps **conceptual** understanding
  when learners have prerequisites and the instruction phase uses their attempts — and reverses for
  young learners and domain-general skills. On **procedural** knowledge SK21 found no difference
  (g = −0.03), and KSC06 itself says guidance's advantage recedes as prior knowledge rises. The
  engine's ROUTE step encodes exactly this split.
- **Exposing question:** "Is the goal a procedure or a concept — and do they have the
  prerequisites?"
- **Unit:** B4b §4 (and §2). Status: verified (2026-11-16, SK21 + KSC06 full text — S12d).

### M69 — Any activity where students are busy (highlighting, copying, clicking) is "active learning"
- **Why it feels true:** "active" sounds like "doing something"; busy classrooms look engaged.
- **Correction:** ICAP (CW14) separates **active** (manipulating given material — highlighting,
  copying, pausing a video) from **constructive** (generating beyond it — explaining, mapping,
  asking) and **interactive** (co-constructing with a partner). Learning is predicted to rise in
  that order; highlighting is rated low utility (D13). The active-learning gains in physics
  (Hak98, Smi09) come from committing to answers and arguing about them — constructive and
  interactive work. Boundary: the ordering is a prediction, not a law — one independent test on a
  text-reading task did not reproduce it (Cap25, A6 §3) — but no evidence makes highlighting or
  copying count as more than *active*.
- **Exposing question:** "What does the student produce that was not already on the page?"
- **Unit:** B4b §5, §7. Status: verified (2026-11-16, CW14 full text; Cap25 boundary).

### M70 — All the popular evidence-based strategies (the "six strategies") are equally proven
- **Why it feels true:** they are presented together, in matching posters, all labelled
  "evidence-based".
- **Correction:** retrieval practice and spacing are rated high utility with large, replicated
  classroom evidence (D13; ANB21); interleaving is moderate with real moderators (BR19 — weak or
  reversed for expository text); elaboration moderate (D13); concrete examples and dual coding rest
  on narrower or mixed evidence (concreteness fading `uncertain` size; No22 for multimedia).
  WMS18 describes "robust support" — true for some more than others.
- **Exposing question:** "Which meta-analysis supports *this* one, and what are its moderators?"
- **Unit:** B4b §12. Status: verified (2026-11-16, ANB21 + BR19 full text; D13 lists S11a).

### M71 — Students need exactly three encounters with a concept to learn it
- **Why it feels true:** Nuthall's "three encounters" is quoted widely as a rule from careful
  classroom research.
- **Correction:** the count comes from very small observational studies (e.g., 14 students in
  NAL95); the book itself reportedly gives three (p. 63) and later four (p. 126) (secondary,
  `uncertain`); encounters must contain the **full** set of information; the number was never
  manipulated experimentally. Treat it as a planning floor, and use spaced retrieval to criterion
  (RD11) as the evidenced version.
- **Exposing question:** "Three encounters of what — and was the number ever tested by changing it?"
- **Unit:** B4b §13. Status: written.

### M72 — Hattie's effect-size rankings tell you which teaching method to choose
- **Why it feels true:** one table, hundreds of meta-analyses, a clear hinge point (d = 0.40) —
  it looks like the most evidence-based list possible.
- **Correction:** standardized effect sizes depend on the test, the sample's spread, the
  comparison and the study length, so combining and ranking them across different studies can
  rank "openness to research design manipulations" rather than impact (Sim17; Ber17). The same
  intervention gives 2σ in a 3-week study with an aligned test and +.04 over a semester on a
  standardized test (Bl84 vs Sl87). Read the primary meta for the method you care about.
- **Exposing question:** "Were the studies behind these two numbers measuring the same outcome,
  over the same time, against the same comparison?"
- **Unit:** B4b §14. Status: written.

### M73 — If a class makes me feel I'm learning less, I am learning less
- **Why it feels true:** a polished lecture feels clear and complete; active work feels confusing
  and slow.
- **Correction:** in a randomized crossover with identical content (149 students, each taught
  both ways), students in the active class **learned more (+0.46 SD on the end-of-class test) but
  felt they learned less**, partly because of the extra cognitive effort (Des19, full text).
  Feelings of fluency track ease of processing, not learning (A1 §6; Bjork B4a §1).
- **Exposing question:** "What did the delayed test say — not how did it feel?"
- **Unit:** B4b §8. Status: verified (2026-11-16, Des19 full text).


### M74 — Flashcard apps are retrieval practice, so if flashcards disappoint, retrieval practice doesn't work
- **Why it feels true:** "active recall" is popularly taught *as* Anki; when a big deck becomes
  tedious and grades don't rise, the whole idea gets blamed.
- **Correction:** a spaced-retrieval *system* of isolated fact cards is one narrow format.
  Retrieval practice in research includes free recall, practice tests, and solving problems; it
  improves delayed tests over rereading, and higher-order prompts improve higher-order tests while
  fact prompts do not (Ag19; KB11, RK06). The disappointing part is the prompt level, not retrieval
  (BK22; Sung's own correction, JS-CO).
- **Exposing question:** "What did your cards ask you to *do* — name a fact, or use, compare or
  explain it?"
- **Unit:** B5 / `justin-sung-audit.md` §1. Status: verified (2026-11-16, Ag19 full text + BK22 captions read in full + JS-CO — S12d-2).

### M75 — Start at the top of Bloom's taxonomy and the facts will fill themselves in
- **Why it feels true:** experts reason at the top levels and seem to know the facts "for free";
  higher-order questions do work better for higher-order exams.
- **Correction:** higher-order and mixed retrieval quizzes beat fact-only quizzes on higher-order
  tests (Ag19) — but the same study **directly refutes "the facts fill themselves in"**: in Exp. 1
  (N = 48, 2-day delay) the fact test after higher-order quizzes was 53%, no better than studying
  once or twice (54%), while fact quizzes gave 78% (d ≈ 1.2) (Ag19 full text, S11a-rest). Benefits
  matched the quiz type; only **mixed** quizzes helped both tests (Exp. 3, 142 sixth-graders: mixed
  quizzes raised the fact test by 27 points over no quiz). Novices facing
  high-interactivity material need worked guidance first (KSC06, KACS03); expert reasoning rests
  on stored knowledge (A5). Bloom is not a strict ladder either way (M28).
- **Exposing question:** "Can you evaluate two options if you can't yet recall what each one is?"
- **Unit:** B5 / `justin-sung-audit.md` §2 (J23). Status: **verified** (Ag19 full text, S11a-rest).

### M76 — Feeling confused means you're learning efficiently
- **Why it feels true:** good strategies do feel harder, and learners wrongly read that effort as
  failure (misinterpreted-effort hypothesis, KGF19) — so "confusion is good" sounds like the cure.
- **Correction:** effort is a by-product of some useful processing, not a measure of it. Overload
  and badly designed material (extraneous load) also feel confusing and do not help (M16; S10).
  Feelings during study are weak monitors (BDK13); a delayed retrieval test is the check (RT11).
- **Exposing question:** "Was it hard because you were relating ideas — or because the material
  was a mess? What did a test a few days later show?"
- **Unit:** B5 / `justin-sung-audit.md` §4 (J26). Status: written.

### M77 — Once knowledge is well connected you don't need spaced retrieval any more
- **Why it feels true:** well-understood material does feel stable, and connected knowledge does
  fade more slowly than isolated items.
- **Correction:** deeper, related processing improves retention (CL72; Be07), but it does not
  stop forgetting; spaced retrieval to a criterion (successive relearning) is what keeps items
  available (RD11, C06). Relating and retrieving are complements — the "both" position both sides
  of the Sung–Keep exchange reached (BK22, JS-CO).
- **Exposing question:** "If you understood it well last month, can you produce it now without
  looking?"
- **Unit:** B5 / `justin-sung-audit.md` §2 (J2). Status: written.

### M78 — Breaks ("diffuse mode") are themselves how the brain learns hard material
- **Why it feels true:** insights really do arrive in the shower or on a walk, and the
  "focused/diffuse mode" story ties it to a named brain network, which sounds like proof.
- **Correction:** the testable version is the incubation effect: a positive effect, but **longer
  preparation before the break gives a bigger effect**, and it is strongest for divergent-thinking
  problems (SO09, meta-analysis). The break helps a problem you have already worked hard on; it does
  not replace the work, and it is a problem-solving effect, not a memory method. The default-mode
  mapping is a metaphor on correlational imaging (OS19 cites resting-state papers, not learning
  experiments).
- **Exposing question:** "What did you try on the problem before you took the break?"
- **Unit:** B5 §3 (Oakley). Status: written.

### M79 — Cold-calling makes students anxious and shuts down participation
- **Why it feels true:** being put on the spot is uncomfortable in the moment, and teachers
  remember the one student who froze.
- **Correction:** in university classes with high cold-calling, *voluntary* participation was
  higher and rose over the term, and students' comfort participating went **up**; in low
  cold-calling classes comfort did not change (DHP13); same pattern for women and men (DHP19,
  full text). Caveats: observational (instructors chose how much to cold-call), one business
  course; participation and comfort, not learning, were measured — and the learning tests are
  null-to-mixed (LB14 randomized: no midterm gain; Car15: some students nervous, one group lower).
  So the honest correction is narrow: cold calling does not *have* to shut participation down;
  it is not shown to raise learning. Tone and routine matter (B5 §4).
- **Exposing question:** "In classes where anyone might be asked, did fewer or more people
  volunteer by the end of term?"
- **Unit:** B5 §4 (Lemov). Status: verified (2026-11-16, DHP19 full text for the DHP13 design + LB14 full text; DHP13 itself abstract — S12d-2).

### M80 — A one-person learning challenge shows the method will work for you
- **Why it feels true:** a dramatic, well-documented personal result (33 MIT courses in a year)
  is vivid and concrete; statistics are not.
- **Correction:** n = 1, no comparison condition, a highly motivated self-selected learner, and
  **self-graded** exams (pass = ≥ 50%; the author says stricter grading would fail five finals —
  SY-MIT). It shows what one person did, not what caused it or whether it transfers. Same logic
  as M61 (a famous teacher's results).
- **Exposing question:** "Who else tried it, what did they get, and compared to what?"
- **Unit:** B5 §2 (Young). Status: verified (2026-11-16, SY-MIT project page read in full, the primary for every fact in the correction — S12d-2).

### M81 — If I can explain it simply, I've mastered it — no practice problems needed
- **Why it feels true:** a smooth explanation feels like full understanding, and the "Feynman
  Technique" is sold as the deep-understanding method ("understand an idea in 20 minutes, that
  will stick with you for years", SY-FT11).
- **Correction:** explaining builds and checks *understanding* (self-explanation g = 0.55, Bi18),
  but procedures and conditional knowledge need practice on problems (A4 rows 4–5); the
  technique's author: "If you're cutting out practice in favor of self-explanations, you may end
  up worse off!" (SY-FT24). An explanation written with the book open is not a test (A1, fluency).
- **Exposing question:** "You can explain it — now solve an unseen problem on it, closed-book."
- **Unit:** B5 §2 (Young). Status: verified (2026-11-16, Bi18 full text + SY-FT11 full text + SY-FT24 author post — S12d-2).

### M82 — Spaced reviews only work on an expanding schedule
- **Why it feels true:** "review just before you forget" sounds like it needs ever-growing gaps,
  and popular schedules (1-3-7-30; AA18, TF19; SRS apps) all expand, so the shape looks like the
  active ingredient.
- **Correction:** KBa11 (*JEP:LMC* 2011): increasing the **total** spacing of repeated tests
  produced large retention gains, but **expanding vs equal vs contracting** schedules made no
  clear difference; expanding gaps made practice harder without better long-term retention.
  MMK16 (full text, independent lab): fixed expanding = fixed equal at a 1-week test (0.31 vs
  0.30); what beat both was spacing **adapted** to each item's performance (0.42).
  What matters: real gaps, actual retrieval, enough total spread (C06). Expanding schedules are
  convenient, not magic.
- **Exposing question:** "If you kept the same total time but used equal gaps, what would change
  on the final test?"
- **Unit:** B5 §8 (Abdaal/Frank); mechanism A4. Status: verified (2026-11-16, MMK16 full text + KBa11 abstract — S12d).

### M83 — Review something enough times and you stop forgetting it
- **Why it feels true:** well-practised facts (your phone number) feel permanent; popularizers
  say the forgetting curve "no longer applies" after enough spaced reviews (AA18).
- **Correction:** each successful spaced retrieval slows forgetting (A2; RD11), so retention can
  last years — but forgetting does not switch off; unused knowledge still decays, just slowly.
  This is why the engine keeps re-testing mastered items at expanding but finite intervals.
- **Exposing question:** "Something you knew perfectly for an exam three years ago — can you
  still do it now, unaided?"
- **Unit:** B5 §8; mechanism A2. Status: written.

### M84 — The more you already know, the more you will gain from the next lesson
- **Why it feels true:** experts learn new things in their field fast (A5), and "prior knowledge
  is the most important factor" is a famous line (Ausubel, B2).
- **Correction:** SFD22 (meta, 8,776 effect sizes): prior knowledge strongly predicts **later
  knowledge** (r = .53 — those who know more still know more) but barely predicts **gain**
  (r = −.06), with a huge spread (−.69 to .62). It helps, hinders, or does nothing depending on
  whether it is relevant, active, accurate and well organized (EF26) — inaccurate deep knowledge
  is a misconception that blocks learning.
- **Exposing question:** "Did the students who knew most at the start *gain* most, or just end
  highest?"
- **Unit:** B5 §7a (Furst); mechanism A5. Status: written.

### M85 — Motivation comes first: get students motivated and achievement will follow
- **Why it feels true:** motivated students do work harder and score higher, and the
  correlation is read as motivation → achievement; motivation talks are easy to give.
- **Correction:** GC16 (1,478 children, Grades 1–4, cross-lagged): maths **achievement predicted
  later intrinsic motivation; motivation did not predict later achievement** at any point. In
  that sample the arrow ran from success to motivation. Practical rule: build early success
  (well-sequenced, ~high success rate) and motivation tends to follow (Mccrea's expectation
  principle). One age group and subject — not a law for all ages (`contested` beyond it): GC16's
  own review (full text) lists earlier studies finding motivation → achievement, reciprocal links,
  or neither, and MPLV13 (N 3,530, grades 5–10) found motivation predicted later achievement
  **growth**. Honest summary: the arrow is two-way, and success-first is the reliable lever to pull.
- **Exposing question:** "Which came first in your best subject — liking it, or being good at
  it?"
- **Unit:** B5 §7c (Mccrea); mechanism A7. Status: verified (2026-11-16, GC16 full text + MPLV13 abstract — S12d).

### M86 — A very high reliability figure from a marking tool means the marks are trustworthy
- **Why it feels true:** reliability is a technical-sounding number; 0.9+ looks like proof.
- **Correction:** BW15: with adaptive comparative-judgement pairing, the reliability statistic
  (SSR) is inflated — "high values of SSR (above 0.8) can even be obtained from random data."
  Non-adaptive CJ is reliable with enough comparisons (VBDD19: ~10–14 per script for .70).
  Ask how the figure was computed, not just how high it is.
- **Exposing question:** "Would this reliability figure still be high if the judgements were
  random?"
- **Unit:** B5 §6 (Christodoulou). Status: verified (2026-11-16, BW15 full 3-pp. paper read; VBDD19 abstract for the non-adaptive figure — S12d-2).

## Full entries

### M1 — Time spent studying = learning
- **Why it feels true:** hours are visible and countable; effort feels like progress; schools and
  timetables reward time-on-task.
- **Correction:** studying is input; learning is "the more or less permanent change in knowledge
  or understanding", inferred later (BB11). Hours spent on low-yield methods (rereading) can
  produce performance with little learning (SB15, RK06).
- **Exposing question:** "If you sat 5 hours yesterday, what can you produce *now*, without notes,
  that you couldn't before?"
- **Unit:** A1 §1. Status: written.

### M2 — If it feels fluent, I've learned it
- **Why it feels true:** fluency (smooth reading, instant recognition) is a real signal; it just
  measures the wrong thing, *current retrieval strength* (BB92).
- **Correction:** fluency during study is a weak guide to long-term learning; conditions that
  feel harder often produce more lasting learning (BB11, SB15).
- **Exposing question:** "Would it still feel easy tomorrow, with the page closed, if the question
  were worded differently?"
- **Unit:** A1 §3, A7. Status: written.

### M3 — Forgetting means the session was wasted
- **Why it feels true:** blanking on something you studied feels like losing it.
- **Correction:** retrieval strength drops while storage strength persists (BB92); relearning is
  faster (savings, MD15). Partial forgetting makes the next retrieval a *bigger* learning event
  (BB92). This is why spacing works.
- **Exposing question:** "Second time through, did it take less time than the first?"
- **Unit:** A1 §3, A2 §5. Status: written.

### M4 — Working memory holds 7 ± 2 items
- **Why it feels true:** famous number (Miller 1956), repeated in textbooks; phone numbers seem to fit.
- **Correction:** 7 mixed in rehearsal and chunking; the core limit is about 4 chunks (C01). What
  grows with expertise is chunk *size*, not slot count.
- **Exposing question:** "Is a 7-digit phone number 7 items or 2–3 chunks for you?"
- **Unit:** A2 §2. Status: written.

### M11 — Retrieval practice only checks memory; it doesn't build understanding
- **Why it feels true:** tests are associated with *measuring*; flashcards often target isolated
  facts; popular framing ("active recall fights the forgetting curve", J4).
- **Correction:** retrieval is itself a learning event. In KB11 it beat concept mapping on
  inference questions and even on a concept-map final test; benefits transfer to new questions
  (PR18, d ≈ 0.40, `uncertain` exact). Scope caveat: depth of the *prompt* matters (A1 §5).
- **Exposing question:** "After free-recalling a chapter, could you answer an inference question
  you never practised?"
- **Unit:** A1 §5, A6. Status: written.

### M16 — Any difficulty is a "desirable difficulty"
- **Why it feels true:** "harder = better" is the slogan version of the research.
- **Correction:** difficulty helps only when it triggers successful encoding/retrieval; "if,
  however, the learner does not have the background knowledge or skills to respond to them
  successfully, they become undesirable difficulties" (BB11, verbatim). Confusion and noise are
  just difficulties.
- **Exposing question:** "Can the learner succeed at this with effort — or not at all yet?"
- **Unit:** A1 §4, A4. Status: written.

### M18 — If I can't recall it, I never learned it
- **Why it feels true:** recall failure is the only signal you can see.
- **Correction:** recall depends on retrieval strength; storage strength can remain high (BB92).
  Relearning shows savings (MD15). Cues can bring back "lost" items (TT73).
- **Exposing question:** "Can you recognise it, or relearn it much faster than the first time?"
- **Unit:** A1 §3. Status: written.

### M19 — Doing well right after studying proves the method works
- **Why it feels true:** immediate scores are real numbers, and they look like evidence.
- **Correction:** immediate tests favour massing and restudy; in RK06 restudy won at 5 minutes
  and lost at 2 days and 1 week. Only delayed tests measure learning (SB15).
- **Exposing question:** "What would the score be in a week?"
- **Unit:** A1 §2. Status: written.

### M20 — For deep understanding, elaborating (e.g. concept mapping) beats testing yourself
- **Why it feels true:** elaboration *looks* deep (connections drawn, effort visible); retrieval
  looks like rote.
- **Correction:** KB11: retrieval practice beat elaborative concept mapping on verbatim and
  inference questions; students predicted the opposite. Elaboration still helps (A6), but it is not
  automatically superior. Combining (elaborate, then retrieve) is the open design question.
- **Exposing question:** "Which of the two produced more on a test a week later?"
- **Unit:** A1 §5, A6. Status: written.

### M21 — I can tell which study method works for me by how it feels
- **Why it feels true:** people trust their own experience; easy methods feel productive.
- **Correction:** KoB08: >80% rated massing equal or better while only 15% did better with it, and
  the judgment survived seeing their own results. Same pattern in KB11.
- **Exposing question:** "Have you ever compared the two methods on a delayed test, not by feel?"
- **Unit:** A1 §6, A7. Status: written.

### M22 — Brain-training games expand working memory and make you smarter
- **Why it feels true:** scores on the game improve steadily; marketing claims; "the brain is a muscle".
- **Correction:** WM training improves trained-like tasks briefly but shows no convincing transfer
  to reasoning, reading or arithmetic vs active controls (MLH13 + 2016 update). Expertise expands
  *effective* capacity through chunks (A5), not through slot training.
- **Exposing question:** "Did the training improve anything other than tasks that look like the training?"
- **Unit:** A2 §2. Status: written.

### M23 — You forget ~70% of everything within a day (the forgetting curve)
- **Why it feels true:** the Ebbinghaus curve is widely shown with dramatic percentages.
- **Correction:** the curve describes nonsense syllables learned once, by one person (Ebbinghaus;
  replicated N = 1, MD15). Meaningful, connected, retrieved material decays far more slowly; the
  curve is the worst case, not a law for everything.
- **Exposing question:** "Was the material meaningful, linked to what you know, and retrieved since?"
- **Unit:** A2 §5. Status: written.

### M24 — Memory works like a recording you play back
- **Why it feels true:** vivid memories feel like replays.
- **Correction:** retrieval is cue-dependent (TT73) and modifies the memory itself (A1 §5); memory
  is spread across multiple systems with different rules (Sq04). What you can get out depends on
  how it went in and what cues you have now.
- **Exposing question:** "Why can you recall it with one cue and not another, if it's a recording?"
- **Unit:** A2 §4. Status: written.

### M25 — Sleep is time lost from studying; all-nighters add study time
- **Why it feels true:** more waking hours = more input.
- **Correction:** sleep is when recent memories are reactivated and consolidated (RB13); waking is
  optimised for encoding, sleep for consolidation. Cutting sleep trades consolidation for more
  input that will not be kept. Spacing across nights uses this.
- **Exposing question:** "How much of last night's all-nighter material can you produce today, unaided?"
- **Unit:** A2 §4. Status: written.

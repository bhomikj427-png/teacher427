# B3 — Legendary classroom teachers (case evidence)

> Part B unit. Same entry format as B1/B2: who → what they actually did → mechanism (Part A) →
> **serves** (A3 row) · **grade** · **use as learner** · **use as teacher**. These four are famous
> for *results* or *reputation*, not for tested methods. A single teacher's success is a **case**:
> no comparison group, a teacher who cannot be separated from the method, and selection (we hear
> about the ones who succeeded — M61). So the **method grade is T almost by definition**; the
> useful question is which *parts* match evidenced mechanisms. A8 §3 test kit applied to each.
>
> Written S8b (2026-11-15).

## 1. Richard Feynman (1918–1988) — explain from first principles

**What he actually said about teaching (primary: Feynman 1963, "The Problem of Teaching Physics in
Latin America", keynote at the First Inter-American Conference on Physics Education, Rio de
Janeiro; *Engineering and Science* 27(2), Caltech archive — read at full text, S8b).**
- His target is **verbal memorization without understanding**: students who can answer "What are
  the four types of telescopes?" — "There is no more telescope, no lenses, no stars, no eyes, no
  light – just words memorized without requiring understanding. The examination is passed."
- "Memorization of laws does not permit one to make applications of these laws to new
  situations" — a **transfer** claim (A5).
- But: "I must say immediately that I am not against memorizing. Some things, even many … may be
  learned by heart."
- On method he is modest: teaching is "part of the problem of teaching anything anywhere – a
  problem for which there is no known satisfactory solution"; his practical advice is to free
  inspiring teachers to work "along with children, suggesting experiments and trying them freely."
- The same Brazil experience is told as a story in *Surely You're Joking* (not open-access; not
  re-read here).

**The "Feynman Technique."** Already settled at A8 §2 myth 7: the named four-step method (pick a
concept → explain it in plain words as if to a child → find the gaps → simplify) appears c. 2011
in Scott Young's writing (SY-FT); no Feynman text describing it was found. This sprint adds: the
1963 keynote — Feynman's longest statement on teaching found in open sources — describes no such
procedure. Attribution stays `uncertain` → leaning **not his**. The often-quoted lines ("if I
can't reduce it to the freshman level, we don't really understand it" — Goodstein's anecdote; the
*Lectures* preface's pessimism about how well he did by the students) are second-hand or not read
this sprint → `uncertain` (backlog). **Update S10b-1:** attribution `settled` — the method is Scott
Young's (SY-FT11, SY-FT24; B5 §2). Goodstein's anecdote read first-hand (Gd89: "I couldn't reduce
it to the freshman level. That means we really don't understand it."; freshman attendance
"started dropping alarmingly"). The preface wording is still seen only in secondary quotes.

**Mechanism (Part A).** What Feynman argued *against* is exactly A1's fluency/verbatim problem:
verbatim prompts build verbatim knowledge (A1 §5); an exam that asks for lists gets lists. What
the later "technique" does is **self-explanation** (Bi18 g = 0.55, S) + **teaching / explaining
to a novice** (FM16, M; Ko19) + **gap detection** = calibration (A7). Note the boundary he himself
draws: some things *should* be memorized (A3 row 1 facts — retrieval practice is right there).

**Serves:** A3 row 3 (principles, models) and row 6 (knowing what you don't understand).

**Grade:** as a teacher — **T** (case; the *Lectures* are famous, but their effect on Caltech
freshmen was never measured against an alternative). The "Feynman Technique" — attribution
`uncertain`; its parts **S** (self-explanation) / **M** (learning by teaching). His anti-rote
critique — consistent with S-grade evidence (A1, A5).

**Use as learner.** After studying, explain the idea in plain words without notes; wherever you
reach for a memorized phrase instead of a reason, that is a gap — go back. But keep using
retrieval practice for the facts; Feynman did not argue against memorizing them.

**Use as teacher.** Check whether your exam questions can be passed by memorized words
("list the four types…"). Ask for an application to a new case instead (A1 §5 relational
prompts).

## 2. Jaime Escalante (1930–2010) — AP Calculus at Garfield High, East Los Angeles

**What happened (contemporaneous journalism — T3; the definitive source, Jay Mathews, *Escalante:
The Best Teacher in America*, 1988, not read → backlog).** In 1982, 18 Garfield students took the
AP Calculus exam and all passed; ETS suspected copying, and the retest — under supervision — was
passed again (Christian Science Monitor, 24 Mar 1988). By 1987, the CSM reports **87 Garfield
students passed** (other accounts give 73 AB + 12 BC, or 85 — sources disagree; `uncertain`).
After his principal-supporter Henry Gradillas left (1987) and Escalante left Garfield (1991),
accounts report the number of passes fell sharply (`uncertain` figures).

**What he did (CSM 1988).** Learning **contracts** signed by parents: daily quizzes, weekly exams,
90 minutes of nightly study; a solved homework problem required at the door; constant practice
("you must practice with your homework problems" — Jerry West shooting the same shot 400 times);
long hours (early morning to evening), calling parents about absences; team rituals, jokes, props
for memory; very high expectations stated bluntly. The film *Stand and Deliver* (1988) dramatizes
this; it compresses years into one and shows students starting from almost nothing, which is the
film, not the record.

**Mechanism (Part A).** (a) **Daily quizzes + weekly exams** = retrieval practice at high
frequency, spaced across a year-long course (A4 S). (b) **90 minutes of nightly practice with
problems** = massive procedure practice → fluency (A3 row 4; A82). (c) **Extended time**
(summer and Saturday sessions are widely reported; `uncertain` here) = more learning time — the
mastery-learning lesson (B2 §2): time is the variable that varies. (d) **Contracts, parents,
team identity** = commitment and relatedness (SDT, engine `research/05` Part B) that keep
the practice going. "High expectations" alone is not the mechanism; the expectations were
enforced through a practice regime.

**Serves:** A3 row 4 (procedures — calculus technique) and row 1 (facts/rules), at exam level.

**Grade:** **T** (single case, no comparison, selection into his classes; results collapsed when
the supporting system left — the method depended on the person and the principal). Mechanisms
**S** (retrieval, spaced practice, time on task).

**Use as learner.** The replicable part is boring: a quiz every day, a test every week, a fixed
practice block every night, and someone you are accountable to.

**Use as teacher.** Don't copy the charisma; copy the schedule (daily retrieval, weekly
cumulative tests, protected practice time) and build the support system (parents, school
leadership) that lets it survive you.

## 3. Marva Collins (1936–2015) — Westside Preparatory School, Chicago (1975–2008)

**What happened.** Collins left Chicago public schools and opened Westside Prep in her home in
1975 with a handful of students; national fame followed (CBS *60 Minutes*, a 1981 TV film). Her
methods, as described in her own book (*Marva Collins' Way*, 1982 with Civia Tamarkin — not read
this sprint) and press accounts: **intensive phonics**, reading of classics (Shakespeare,
Emerson) with young children, daily recitation and memorization, constant questioning, very high
expectations and personal warmth.

**Evidence.** No independent evaluation of Westside Prep's outcomes was found this sprint
(claims of large test gains are self-reported or journalistic; `uncertain`). One documented
public test of her approach: Chicago's **Beidler Elementary**, where Collins was external
partner from February 1997 (Chicago Reporter, 2005, T3): students at or above national norms in
reading rose from **10.2% to 32.5%** in one year, then later fell to **13.5%**; her assistant
credited her phonics emphasis, some teachers credited the teachers in the tested grades, the
council chair "a combination". Pre/post, one school, no comparison — and a rise from a very low
point is exactly where **regression to the mean** predicts some rebound anyway.

**Mechanism (Part A).** **Systematic phonics** for early reading is the best-evidenced element
(not reviewed in this KB — beginning reading is outside A3's scope; `uncertain` here, flagged for
a later look). **Recitation and memorization** = retrieval practice (A4 S) — for facts and texts.
**Constant questioning** = elicited responses (as in DI, B2 §6). High expectations + warmth =
relatedness (SDT). Reading classics with young children — no evidence either way found.

**Serves:** A3 row 1 (facts, decoding) and row 4 (reading as a skill); attitudes.

**Grade:** **T** (case; no controlled data; the one public test is pre/post and did not hold).
Mechanisms **S** (retrieval, frequent responses) where present.

**Use as learner.** Recite and recall from memory daily — Collins' students did, and that part
has evidence.

**Use as teacher.** Systematic decoding instruction + frequent individual responses + high
expectations is a coherent package; judge it by data *with a comparison group*, not by one
year's rise from a low base.

## 4. Sal Khan (b. 1976) — Khan Academy: short videos + mastery-based practice

**What he built.** Short explanatory videos (from 2006), then an online practice platform with
skill levels and **mastery** progression (a learner advances a skill by answering correctly),
teacher dashboards, and recently an AI tutor (Khanmigo, 2023). The PNAS authors describe CAL as
"an affordable way to implement a mastery learning approach in the classroom" (EB26).

**Evidence.**
- **Randomized (FFL20, full text read S8b; preregistered AEA RCT 0002456).** Brazil, 157 public
  schools, ≈15,000 students in grades 5 and 9 (2017): once a week, math class moved to the
  computer lab and was taught with Khan Academy *instead of* the usual class. **"We find no
  average treatment effect on students' math proficiency"** on the national exam; attitudes to
  math improved; effects depended on infrastructure (positive where students had individual
  computers, negative where they shared). Program run with Khan Academy and the Lemann
  Foundation (support acknowledged).
- **Quasi-experimental, real-world use (EB26, PNAS 2026, 123(1); abstract).** Three-year panel,
  > 200,000 students in districts licensing Khan Academy's MAP Accelerator; causal effect
  identified from within-teacher and within-school changes in classroom practice time: **6.6 h a
  year (≈ 11 min/week) → +0.031 SD** in math scores; roughly linear, **projected +0.085 SD at the
  recommended 30 min/week**; higher-achieving students benefit most (they practise more). Two of
  five authors are in Khan Academy's Efficacy Research team (COI); design is stronger than a plain
  correlation but not randomized.
- **Vendor efficacy reports** ("~20% higher-than-expected gains" at 30+ min/week) are
  correlational and produced by Khan Academy (search lead only; not used for grading).

**Mechanism (Part A).** Practice items with immediate correctness feedback = **retrieval +
feedback** (S); mastery progression = the **prerequisite gate** (B2 §1–2); videos = explanation
(worked examples for procedures, A4) — but watching alone is passive (A4 bridge "Avoid: watching
only"; A1 fluency). The PNAS finding that gains scale with *practice* time, not access, fits
this: the active part does the work.

**Serves:** A3 rows 1 and 4 (facts, procedures in maths).

**Grade:** **T–M**: the one RCT is null on average (as a replacement for a weekly class, with
infrastructure problems); the best real-world estimate is **small** (≈0.03–0.09 SD at typical
to recommended use; developer co-authors). Mechanisms S. Khanmigo / AI tutoring: no outcome
evidence reviewed (→ B4/B5 if needed).

**Use as learner.** Use platforms like Khan Academy for the **practice and mastery checks**, not
the videos alone; 30 minutes a week is the dose the evidence was measured at, and the effect at
that dose is small — it is a supplement, not a course.

**Use as teacher.** Count minutes of *practice* (items answered), not videos watched. Make sure
every student has their own device before replacing class time with it (FFL20).

## Summary (B3, all 4)

| Teacher | Core move | Serves (A3) | Grade (method / mechanism) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Feynman | explain in plain words; against memorized words | 3, 6 | T (case); "Feynman Technique" = Young's (`settled` S10b-1) / self-explanation S, teaching M |
| Escalante | daily quizzes, weekly exams, nightly practice, contracts, extra time | 4, 1 | T (case; collapsed after he left) / retrieval + practice + time S |
| Marva Collins | phonics, recitation, classics, questioning, expectations | 1, 4 | T (case; Beidler pre/post 10.2% → 32.5% → 13.5%) / retrieval S |
| Sal Khan | videos + mastery practice platform | 1, 4 | T–M (RCT null FFL20; +0.03–0.09 SD EB26) / practice + feedback S |

**Pattern across B3.** (1) Every legend's *replicable* core is an S-grade mechanism already in A4 —
retrieval on a schedule (Escalante, Collins), frequent responses and practice (Escalante, Khan),
explaining in your own words (Feynman). (2) The parts that make them legends — charisma,
expectations, a film, a quotable line — are not separable from the person and have no outcome
evidence. (3) **Results did not survive the person or the system**: Garfield's passes fell after
Escalante and his principal left; Beidler's gain did not hold; Khan's platform shows small effects
once tested in ordinary use. A legend is a hypothesis about mechanisms, not proof of a method
(M61). (4) Two attribution problems: the "Feynman Technique" is not demonstrably Feynman's, and
*Stand and Deliver* is not the record.

## Misconceptions owned (B3)
M61 (a great teacher's results prove their method), M62 (high expectations alone produced
Escalante's results), M63 (time on a learning platform = learning; Khan Academy is proven to raise
scores a lot) — full entries in `misconceptions.md`. A8 myth 7 (Feynman Technique) is reinforced,
not re-owned.

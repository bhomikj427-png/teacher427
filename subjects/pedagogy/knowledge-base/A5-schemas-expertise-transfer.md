## 1. Chunks and schemas: how experts get around the bottleneck `settled`

- **Chess (CS73, building on de Groot):**
  - Masters reproduce a real game position after a brief look far better than weaker players.
  - On **random** boards the advantage disappears.
  - So masters don't have bigger memories. They store positions as a few large, meaningful
    chunks (familiar piece patterns) and fit them into the same ~4-slot working memory (C01, A2).
  - Caveats: N = 3 players; later work revised chunk sizes (Gobet's templates) but kept the core idea.
- **Schema** = a stored, organised structure that treats many elements as one and says what to do
  with them.
  - In cognitive-load terms, schemas reduce element interactivity: what is many interacting
    elements for a novice is one element for an expert (CKS17).
  - This is why the same material needs different teaching at different levels (A4 §3 rule 1).

## 2. Novices see surfaces; experts see structure `settled`

- **Physics problem sorting (CFG81):**
  - Novices grouped problems by **surface features** ("inclined plane problems", "spring problems").
  - Experts grouped them by **deep principle** ("conservation of energy", "Newton's second law").
  - The category you put a problem in decides the method you retrieve. Surface categories retrieve
    the wrong method whenever surfaces mislead.
- **Consequence for teaching (links to A4 rows 2, 5):**
  - Vary surfaces while holding the principle fixed.
  - Mix problems whose surfaces look alike but whose principles differ, so the learner must see
    structure to choose.
  - This is the mechanism behind interleaving's advantage with similar-looking categories (BR19).

## 3. Transfer: why it fails, and what helps

**Definitions:**
- Near transfer = applying learning to situations close to the practice.
- Far transfer = applying it to situations that differ in content or context.

**It is hard and not one thing (BC02)** `settled`
- After a century of research, "does transfer happen?" has no single answer.
- Barnett & Ceci argue a single effect size for far transfer is misguided, and classify transfer
  distance along **content** dimensions (what is transferred) and **context** dimensions (where, when,
  in what social and physical setting, in what format).
- Exact dimension list: `uncertain` (3 content + 6 context per a secondary source; verify S11).

**The analogy bottleneck is noticing, not reasoning (GH80)** `settled` (pattern) / `uncertain` (percentages)
- Duncker's radiation problem: a tumour must be destroyed by rays that would also burn healthy
  tissue on the way in.
- Few people solve it unaided (≈10%).
- After reading a story with the same solution (an army splits up and converges on a fortress),
  only ≈30% use it spontaneously.
- With a hint to use the story, ≈75–92% solve it.
- So people *had* the analogy and failed to *retrieve* it: a cue/encoding-specificity failure (TT73, A2).
- **Schema induction (Gick & Holyoak 1983):**
  - Reading **two** analogous stories and comparing them to extract their shared structure raised
    spontaneous transfer (≈52%).
  - Comparison strips surface details and stores the structure. That structure is the retrieval
    cue that new problems can match.

**What helps transfer (synthesis)**
1. Multiple varied examples that share structure, *compared* explicitly (GH80/83).
2. Retrieval practice, with benefits that transfer, especially when the response format matches
   (PR18).
3. Self-explanation of the principle behind each step (Bi18; A4).
4. Practice choosing the method, i.e. interleaving (A4 row 5).
5. Testing in varied contexts and wordings (TT73).

## 4. Expertise and deliberate practice `contested` (size of the effect)

- **Ericsson's deliberate practice:** effortful, targeted practice on specific weaknesses, with
  feedback, at the edge of current ability.
- **How much does it explain? (MHO14)** Across 157 effect sizes:
  - games 26% of performance variance
  - music 21%
  - sports 18%
  - **education 4%**
  - professions <1%
- **Read honestly:**
  - Practice is necessary and helps.
  - The popular "10,000 hours makes anyone an expert" is an overstatement (M34).
  - Ericsson's group disputes that the included studies measured *deliberate* practice rather than
    just practice time. That dispute is unresolved.
  - For learning a subject, the *quality* features (targeted, feedback, at the edge) matter more
    than hours. Those features are the same ones the A4 methods build in.

## 5. Uses
- **As a learner:**
  - Ask "what principle is this an example of?" for every solved problem.
  - Compare two problems with the same principle and different surfaces.
  - Mix problem types in revision.
  - Judge readiness by fresh problems, not familiar ones.
- **As a teacher:**
  - Teach with two or more contrasting analogs and make students state the shared structure.
  - Deliberately set surface-similar, principle-different problems.
  - Expect transfer only where it was practised.
- **For the engine:** INTERLEAVE and varied CHECK questions already serve this. A5 adds:
  **teach-back should name the deep principle, not the procedure steps** → S12 note.

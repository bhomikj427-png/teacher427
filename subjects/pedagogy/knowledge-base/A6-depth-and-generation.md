# A6 — Depth of Processing and Generative Learning

> **Information type:** A3 rows 1–3 and 5 (facts, concepts, principles, conditional knowledge).
> Generation helps facts *when there is something to connect them to*; constructive activities
> mainly help rows 2, 3 and 5. **Threshold concept 4** (retrieval is itself an encoding event) is
> completed here, together with A1 §5.
> Confidence tags: `settled` / `contested` / `uncertain` / `suspect`. Source IDs → `sources.md`.

---

## 1. Levels of processing: meaning beats repetition `settled` (core) · `contested` (as a theory)

- **Claim (CL72):** how well something is remembered depends on how *deeply* it was processed
  (its meaning, its links to what you know), not on how long it sat in a short-term store.
- **Mechanism:** processing for meaning connects the item to existing knowledge. That gives it
  more retrieval routes and makes it more distinct from similar items. Shallow processing (how a
  word looks or sounds) creates a trace with few links.
- **Limits:**
  - "Depth" was never defined independently of the memory result it predicts (the circularity
    critique, noted in CL72's reception).
  - **Transfer-appropriate processing** (MBF77; bib confirmed S11b-1, result seen only in
    consistent secondary summaries → `uncertain` until the paper is read): processing helps when it
    matches what the test asks. Semantic study beat rhyme study on a standard recognition test, but
    rhyme study did better on a rhyme recognition test. "Deep" is best read as "matched to the use
    you need" — the same pattern Ag19 found for fact vs higher-order quizzes (A4 §1, full text).
- **Bridge:** this is why ICAP (§3) warns that shallow tests hide the benefit of deep activities.

## 2. The generation effect: producing beats reading `settled` (lab)

- **Finding (Be07, full text):** 86 studies, 445 effect sizes, N = 17,711. Items people
  *generated* (e.g. completing *cold – h_t*) were remembered better than items they only read:
  **d = .40**, "almost half a standard deviation".
- **Moderators that teach the mechanism:**

| Condition | d | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Words | .41 | — |
| Numbers | .87 | Generating a result you can check against rules is powerful |
| **Nonwords** | **.05** | Nothing in memory to connect to → generation does almost nothing |
| Anagrams | slightly negative | Effort spent on letter-shuffling, not meaning |
| Tested immediately / 1 min–1 day | .41 / .41 | — |
| **Tested after > 1 day** | **.64** (k = 30) | Benefit grows with delay (fewer studies) |
| Cued recall / recognition / free recall | .55 / .46 / .32 | Varies with the test |

- **Mechanism:** generating forces you to search memory and use what you know to produce the
  item. That links it to existing knowledge and makes it distinctive. The nonword result shows
  generation needs prior knowledge to work with.
- **Boundary:** the paradigm is word lists and paired associates. School-scale generative
  strategies (§3) are the classroom version, and they are tested separately.
- **Retrieval is generation from memory.** This is the bridge to threshold concept 4: a recall
  attempt is a generation event, which is why testing builds learning (A1 §5) rather than only
  measuring it.

## 3. Generative learning and ICAP: what the learner *does* decides what changes `settled` (core) · `contested` (edges)

- **Generative learning (FM16):** learners select relevant information, organize it, and
  integrate it with prior knowledge. Eight strategies have evidence, each with boundary
  conditions: summarizing, mapping, drawing, imagining, self-testing, self-explaining, teaching,
  enacting.
- **ICAP (CW14, full text):** sorts activities by what the learner *visibly does*, and links each
  to a knowledge-change process:

| Mode | Learner does | Knowledge change | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **P**assive | Receives (listens, reads) | **Store**, isolated | "Inert": retrievable only with the same cue or context |
| **A**ctive | Manipulates (underlines, copies, pauses, repeats) | **Integrate** with activated prior knowledge | Fills gaps in an existing schema; at least shallow understanding |
| **C**onstructive | Generates beyond the material (self-explains, maps, asks questions, compares) | **Infer** new knowledge | Richer, more coherent schema; supports transfer |
| **I**nteractive | Dialogue where *both* partners construct | **Co-infer** | Each builds on the other's inferences |

- **Prediction:** I > C > A > P.
- **The authors' own overrides (CW14 pp. 235–236):**
  1. **Shallow tests hide differences.** Easy multiple-choice items were equal across all four
     modes (Menekse et al. 2013). Self-explanation helped only on hard and transfer items
     (Aleven & Koedinger 2002).
  2. **Arbitrary rules:** explaining *why* an English article is correct gave no benefit
     (Wylie 2011), because there is no reason to generate. → For arbitrary facts (A3 row 1),
     use retrieval and spacing, not elaboration.
  3. **No schema to infer from:** for very unfamiliar ideas (emergence), learners cannot
     self-explain their way in → guidance first (A4 §3 rule 2).
- **Contested at the edges — one direct test went the other way (Cap25, full text, S11b-1):**
  - A replication of ICAP itself: 161 undergraduates read a text on decision-making theories
    (13 min), then spent **15 min** in one of four matched activities (passive = re-read a printout).
  - Mean order on comprehension and inference tests: **passive > active > interactive >
    constructive** — the reverse of the prediction. The overall ANOVA was *not* significant
    without a covariate (comprehension p = .107, inference p = .068). With course history
    (prior knowledge) as covariate it was (partial η² .05 / .06), and passive beat constructive on
    the Bonferroni post-hoc tests.
  - Authors' reading: high cognitive load and task difficulty in the generative tasks, and low
    prior knowledge, outweighed the generation benefit.
  - Weight: **T3** (an MA thesis, one lab study, no delayed test reported, not peer-reviewed). It does not
    overturn ICAP. It does agree with CW14's own override 3 and A4 §3: generative tasks need
    enough prior knowledge and manageable load.
- ICAP is a strong first cut at the *conceptual* rows, not a law for every row.

## 4. Organizing: concept maps `settled` (benefit) · `settled` (loses to retrieval)

- **Sch17 (ERIC abstract):** 142 effect sizes, n = 11,814: learning with concept maps g = 0.58.
  **Constructing maps g = 0.72 > studying given maps g = 0.43**. The benefit holds in STEM and
  non-STEM subjects.
- **The older meta (NA06, full text S11b-1)** gives the same order: all g = .60 (k 67),
  constructing .82 (k 27), studying .37 (k 40), with large heterogeneity. Its .82 shrinks to .47 when
  only U.S./Canada studies are counted (7 African studies average 1.45), and the model is
  fixed-effect. So read **"constructing beats studying"** as `settled`. The size depends on the meta
  and subset (≈0.47–0.82 for constructing: NA06 U.S./Canada .47, Sch17 .72, NA06 all .82); "moderate"
  is the engine's reading of the two metas, not a figure either paper states.
- **But (KB11):** retrieval practice beat concept-map construction, even on inference questions
  and even when the final test was to draw a map. Students predicted the opposite.
- **Reading both together:**
  - The comparison conditions in the mapping meta-analyses are often weak (reading, lecture).
  - Mapping is a good *constructive* activity. It is not a substitute for retrieval.
  - Best use: **build the map, then reproduce it from memory**. This turns organizing into
    retrieval of relationships (A4 row 3).
- Constructing beats studying for the same reason as §2: the learner generates the links.

## 5. Pretesting and errorful generation: guess first, then study `settled` (specific) · `settled` (no general effect)

- **Unsuccessful retrieval helps (KHB09):** across six experiments, guessing *wrongly* before
  seeing the answer beat reading question and answer together for the same total time. RKK09
  extended this to a science text.
- **Size and reach (SCA24, preregistered meta, full text):**
  - **Specific effect** (on the questions you were pre-asked): **g = 0.54** (k = 97;
    bias-adjusted 0.45).
  - **General effect** (on the rest of the material): **g = 0.04** (k = 91), "nonexistent".
  - Factual prequestions g = .58 vs conceptual .28 (not significantly different; conceptual
    still beat control).
- **Mechanism (attention account, SCA24):** a prequestion tells you what will matter, so
  attention catches that answer when it appears. Searching memory for an answer also activates
  related knowledge, ready to link to the answer.
- **Conditions:** the correct answer must follow — pretesting helps "if there is an opportunity to
  study the correct answers afterwards", and works with texts, videos, lectures or feedback despite
  many wrong first answers (PC23 abstract, S11b-1) `settled`. *How soon* the answer must follow is
  not stated in the abstract → `uncertain`. The size "may vary due to differences in procedure or
  how learning is assessed" (PC23). Pre-asking does not raise learning of untested parts.
- **Advance organizers** (Ausubel: a short, more general frame given before the lesson, to link
  new material to what the learner knows) are a *different* pre-study move. `contested` / thin:
  - 135 studies: a **small** facilitative effect on learning and retention; the retention
    advantage grows with time (LAA80, abstract). Neither meta's abstract gives a number; the
    only figure held is Bloom's secondary listing, about .20 (Bl84 Table I). Own ES `uncertain`.
  - Stone's meta (1983, JEE 51(4); ERIC ED220476): supported overall, but four results go
    *against* the theory — weaker for written-only and abstract organizers, *stronger* for
    non-subsuming ones, no special help for low-ability or low-knowledge learners, weaker for
    older learners (abstract, S11b-1).
  - All pre-1980 studies, older methods. Grade **T–M**: plausible, small, not modern-tested.
  - Concrete, comparative organizers (an analogy to something known) look stronger than abstract
    summaries (Kozlow 1978 via ERIC; `uncertain`). This links to A5 §3 (analogies).

## 6. Elaboration and self-explanation (summary; full evidence in A4 §1)
- Self-explanation g = 0.55 (Bi18, full text S11a-rest; 0.67 vs no explanation, 0.35 vs a
  given explanation). Elaborative interrogation rated moderate (D13, list confirmed at full text
  S11a).
- Both are *constructive* in ICAP terms and work through the same mechanism as §2: produce the
  link yourself. Both need enough prior knowledge to produce good explanations.

## 7. Encoding vs retrieval, settled for this KB (threshold concept 4)
- Generation (§2), constructive activity (§3), map-building (§4) and guessing (§5) all work by
  making the learner **produce** something that links new to known.
- Retrieval practice is the same kind of event, with memory as the source. The *prompt* decides
  its depth:
  - "What is X?" → generation of an isolated fact (row 1)
  - "Why does X cause Y?" or "Draw the map from memory" → generation of relations (rows 2–3, 5)
- So "encoding methods" and "retrieval methods" are not rivals. Both are generation; choose the
  prompt by A3 row (A4 §4).

## 8. Uses
- **As a learner:**
  - Before a chapter, answer its end-of-chapter questions cold; expect the gain only on those
    questions (§5).
  - Turn reading into constructive work: explain each step, compare cases, build a map.
  - Then close the book and reproduce the map or explanation (§4, §7).
  - For arbitrary facts, skip the "why" and use spaced retrieval.
- **As a teacher:**
  - Open with prequestions on the points that matter most.
  - Design tasks one ICAP level up (copying → explaining → explaining to a partner who must add).
  - Check with deep questions; shallow quizzes will not show the difference.
- **For the engine:** ACTIVATE already asks for a prediction (pretesting). SCA24 adds a
  precision: the gain is specific to what is asked, so **prequestions should target the
  lesson's key points**. → S12 proposal candidate (no edit).

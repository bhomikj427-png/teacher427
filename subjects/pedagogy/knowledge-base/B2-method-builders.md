## 1. Fred Keller (1899–1996) — Personalized System of Instruction (PSI, "Keller Plan")

**What he built.** A college course design (Keller 1968, "Good-bye, teacher…", *JABA* 1(1) 79–89;
bib only this sprint): the course is cut into small written units; students work at their own pace;
each unit ends in a short test that must be passed at a mastery level before the next unit
(retakes allowed, no penalty); advanced students ("proctors") score tests and give immediate
feedback; lectures are few and used for motivation, not for content. ERIC/Kulik describe it as "an
individually paced, mastery-oriented teaching method" (KKC79 record). The five-feature list above
is the standard description; Keller's own wording → `uncertain` until the 1968 text is read
(PMC copy is a scanned image; backlog).

**Evidence (the package).** Kulik, Kulik & Cohen 1979 (KKC79), 75 comparative studies of college
courses: PSI "generally produces superior student achievement, less variation in achievement, and
higher student ratings" (abstract). The numeric mean is not in the abstract → backlog (the often
quoted ≈0.5 SD is unverified). Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns 1990 (KKB90), 108 controlled
evaluations of mastery programs (Keller and Bloom types): positive effects on examination
performance in colleges, high schools and upper elementary grades; **"stronger on the weaker
students"**; effects vary with mastery procedures, design and content; mastery programs "may
increase student time on instructional tasks", and **"self-paced mastery programs often reduce
the completion rates in college classes"** (abstract, full sentence). Slavin 1987 (Sl87, full
text) reports the earlier Kulik et al. 1986 means as 0.52 (pre-college) and 0.54 (college).

**Mechanism (Part A).** (a) Every unit ends in a **test the learner must produce answers on** =
retrieval practice (A4, S), repeated across the course = a crude form of successive relearning.
(b) **Immediate feedback** from a proctor (A4; Skinner B1 §10). (c) **The mastery gate** stops a
learner building on a missing prerequisite — the A5 schema argument (new knowledge attaches to
existing knowledge). (d) Self-pacing removes the time constraint on slow learners — which is also
why completion drops: the procrastinators never reach the end.

**Serves:** A3 rows 1 and 4 (facts, procedures) — anything that can be cut into small units with
a clear pass criterion; weaker fit for row 3 (principles) where "passing a unit" can be met by
verbatim answers (A1 §5).

**Grade:** **M–S** for achievement in college courses on the course's own exams (two metas,
consistent direction; old studies, pre-registration did not exist — effect sizes are upper bounds,
`research/07`). Mechanisms S (retrieval, feedback). `contested` edge: most outcome measures were
the course's own tests (see the Slavin critique under §2).

**Use as learner.** Cut a subject into units small enough to test in 10 minutes. Write the unit
test *before* studying the unit; do not move on until you pass it from memory. Set your own
deadlines — self-pacing without them is how PSI students failed to finish.

**Use as teacher.** Short unit tests with a pass mark and unlimited no-penalty retakes; feedback
the same day (peers can mark against a key). Add hard deadlines or a minimum pace — the evidence
says pure self-pacing loses students.

## 2. Benjamin Bloom (1913–1999) — mastery learning and the "2 sigma problem"

**What he built.** Group-based **mastery learning**: the class is taught as usual, then a
*formative* test (for feedback, not marks); students below the criterion get **corrective
instruction** and a **parallel second test**; the class moves on when most have reached mastery
(Sl87's definition, full text: criterion level, frequent assessment, corrective instruction, then
"later parallel assessments"). Also the taxonomy (→ A3, K02 revision).

**The 2-sigma paper (Bl84, read at full text from the scanned journal pages).** Two University
of Chicago doctoral students (Anania 1982/1983; Burke 1984) compared three conditions —
conventional class of about 30, **mastery learning** class of about 30, and **tutoring** ("a
good tutor for each student (or for two or three students simultaneously)"). Crucially, the
tutoring condition **also** "is followed periodically by formative tests, feedback-corrective
procedures, and parallel formative tests as in the mastery learning classes". Students were
randomly assigned; four samples at grades 4, 5 and 8; two subjects (probability, cartography);
**"the instructional treatment was limited to 11 periods of instruction over a 3-week block of
time."** Result: the average tutored student was "about two standard deviations above the average
of the control class (… above 98% of the students in the control class)"; mastery learning
"about one standard deviation" (84th percentile). The "2 sigma problem" is a *research
challenge*: find group methods that match tutoring, since tutoring "is too costly for most
societies to bear on a large scale." Bloom's Table I (adapted from Walberg 1984) lists
"tutorial instruction 2.00", "reinforcement 1.20", "feedback-corrective (ML) 1.00", "advance
organizers .20"; some entries are "averaged or estimated from correlational data".

**Later evidence.**
- **Mastery learning in real schools (Sl87, full text).** Best-evidence synthesis of group-based
  mastery learning, elementary and secondary, studies **≥ 4 weeks**: on **standardized**
  achievement tests the median effect across 7 studies was "essentially zero (ES = +.04)"; on
  **experimenter-made** tests, median +.255 across 8 studies (immediate posttests), "with little
  evidence that effects maintained over time." Slavin's explanation: the brief experiments Bloom's
  side relied on often used procedures hard to run in practice ("such as providing 1 hour of
  corrective instruction for every hour of initial instruction"), and experimenter-made tests
  match what the mastery class was focused on. Bloom replied in the same journal (1987); the
  dispute is about which studies count, not about the arithmetic.
- **Mastery programs overall (KKB90):** positive on course exams; larger for weaker students (§1).
- **Human tutoring (VL11, ERIC abstract):** VanLehn reviewed experiments comparing human tutoring,
  computer tutoring and no tutoring: the widely believed d ≈ 2.0 for human tutors "was much lower:
  **d = 0.79**"; step-based intelligent tutoring systems **0.76**, "nearly as effective as human
  tutoring."
- **Tutoring at scale (NOQ24, AERJ 61(1) 74–107, abstract):** systematic review and meta-analysis
  of preK–12 tutoring **randomized** field experiments: pooled **ES = 0.288 SD** (SE 0.029);
  largest with teachers or paraprofessionals as tutors, earlier grades, **≥ 3 days per week**, and
  during school hours.

**Mechanism (Part A).** Mastery learning = formative **retrieval** + **feedback** + **corrective
re-teaching** + a second test (= relearning to criterion) + a prerequisite gate (A5). Tutoring adds
**calibration to the individual** (A1: desirable difficulty at the learner's edge; engine
principle 4) and immediate correction of misconceptions. Why 2σ shrank: (1) Bloom's tutors were
*also* running the mastery cycle, so 2σ is tutoring **plus** mastery, not tutoring alone; (2) 3
weeks, 11 lessons, experimenter-made tests — short studies on tests aligned to the treatment
produce larger effects than semester-long studies on standardized tests (Sl87 pattern); (3)
dissertations, one group's procedure, small samples.

**Serves:** all A3 rows in principle (the gate is content-free); in the studies, rows 1, 3 and 4.

**Grade:** mastery learning **M** on aligned tests, **T** on standardized tests in practical
school use (Sl87); human tutoring **S** that it helps (NOQ24, randomized) but **≈0.3 SD at scale,
≈0.8 SD in lab-style comparisons** — 2σ is **X** as a general claim (M15). Feedback-corrective
mechanism S.

**Use as learner.** Run Bloom's cycle on yourself: test → find the gaps → re-learn only those →
take a *different* test on the same material. Most of tutoring's gain you can get from honest
self-testing and correction; what you cannot easily self-supply is someone who spots a
misconception you don't know you have — ask for that specifically.

**Use as teacher.** Formative tests that do not count for marks, followed by corrective work on
exactly what was missed, then a parallel test. Watch coverage: Slavin's "coverage vs mastery
dilemma" — time spent re-teaching is time not spent on new material. If you can buy tutoring,
the evidence says: trained tutors, ≥ 3 sessions a week, during school (NOQ24).

## 3. Robert Gagné (1916–2002) — types of learning outcome and the nine events of instruction

**What he built.** (a) The **five learned capabilities** — verbal information, intellectual skills
(discriminations → concepts → rules → higher-order rules), cognitive strategies, motor skills,
attitudes — each needing *different* conditions of learning (G84, full text, read S3; already the
Gagné column of A3 §3). (b) **Learning hierarchies**: an intellectual skill is taught after its
prerequisite skills. (c) The **nine events of instruction**, usually listed as: gain attention;
inform learners of the objective; stimulate recall of prior learning; present the content; provide
learning guidance; elicit performance; provide feedback; assess performance; enhance retention and
transfer. Wording of the nine from standard secondary listings → `uncertain` (Gagné, Briggs &
Wager, *Principles of Instructional Design* — archive copy is lending-only; backlog).

**Mechanism (Part A).** The nine events are a checklist that already contains several S-grade
moves: "stimulate recall of prior learning" (retrieval + activation, A6), "elicit performance"
(retrieval / practice, A4), "provide feedback" (A4), "enhance retention and transfer" (spacing,
varied practice — though Gagné's lists do not specify *spacing* across days). Learning hierarchies
= the prerequisite gate (A5). The **five capabilities** are the important part: they are the first
taxonomy built on the claim this subject rests on — **the best method depends on the kind of
thing being learned** (A3 §1; KLI makes the same claim with a different cut).

**Serves:** A3 as a whole (Gagné is one of the three column-sources of A3 §3); the nine events
serve rows 1–4.

**Grade:** **T** for the nine events as a package — no controlled test of the sequence was found
this sprint (the events are a design checklist, widely used in instructional design). **M–S** for
the components it contains (retrieval, feedback, worked guidance). The capability taxonomy is a
framework, graded by usefulness, not effect size (A3 decision S3).

**Use as learner.** Before a session ask "which of Gagné's five is this?" — verbal information
(self-test), intellectual skill (worked examples → practice), cognitive strategy (try it on varied
problems), motor (physical practice with feedback). The type decides the method (A4 bridge).

**Use as teacher.** Use the nine events as a completeness check on a lesson plan, not as a script.
The two most often skipped are "stimulate recall of prior learning" and "elicit performance" —
both are retrieval, which is where the evidence is strongest.

## 4. David Ausubel (1918–2008) — meaningful learning and advance organizers

**What he built.** "The most important single factor influencing learning is what the learner
already knows" (epigraph of *Educational Psychology: A Cognitive View*, 1968 — widely quoted;
wording `uncertain`, not read this sprint). **Meaningful** learning = new material linked to
relevant existing ideas, vs **rote** learning = stored arbitrarily. Tool: the **advance
organizer**, a short introductory frame "at a higher level of abstraction, generality and
inclusiveness" than the lesson (Ausubel 1960, *J. Educ. Psychol.* 51(5) 267–272, bib verified;
text not read).

**Evidence → already written at A6 §5 (do not duplicate).** LAA80 (135 studies): a **small**
facilitative effect on learning and retention, advantage growing with delay; Stone's meta: the
pattern does not follow the theory (no special benefit for low-knowledge learners); concrete
comparative organizers look stronger than abstract ones. Bloom's own Table I (Bl84) lists
advance organizers at **.20** — the smallest learner-facing entry. Grade there: **T–M**.

**Mechanism (Part A).** Activating the right prior schema before new input gives the new
information something to attach to (A5 schemas; A2 encoding). The modern versions of this idea
are stronger than the organizer itself: **pretesting** (commit to answers first — SCA24 g = 0.54
on asked items, A6 §5) and **concept-map construction** (Sch17). Ausubel's *principle* (prior
knowledge decides what is learnable) is `settled` (it is the expertise-reversal / schema result,
A5); his *tool* is weak.

**Serves:** A3 row 3 (principles/models), row 2 (concepts).

**Grade:** organizer **T–M**; prior-knowledge principle **S** (as a mechanism).

**Use as learner.** Before a chapter, spend 3 minutes writing what you already know about its
topic and what you expect it to say — that is the organizer and a pretest in one, and the pretest
part is the bit with evidence.

**Use as teacher.** Prefer a concrete analogy to something the class already knows over an
abstract overview; better still, open with 2–3 questions they must answer before the lesson.

## 5. Jerome Bruner (1915–2016) — spiral curriculum, enactive → iconic → symbolic, discovery

**What he built.** (a) The **spiral curriculum** (*The Process of Education*, 1960): return to
the same core ideas at increasing depth across years; the claim usually quoted is that "any
subject can be taught effectively in some intellectually honest form to any child at any stage of
development" (`uncertain` wording — book not open-access; backlog). (b) **Three modes of
representation** — enactive (action), iconic (image), symbolic (language/notation) — which
Singapore maths turned into **CPA** (concrete → pictorial → abstract). (c) Advocacy of
**discovery learning** (1961). (d) Co-author of the scaffolding paper (WBR76 — see B1 §9).

**Mechanism and evidence (Part A).**
- Enactive → iconic → symbolic = **concreteness fading** (FN18 definition, B1 §6). Fyfe, McNeil,
  Son & Goldstone 2014 (FMSG14, ERIC abstract read S8a) argue for "beginning with concrete
  materials and then explicitly and gradually fading to the more abstract", combining the
  advantages of both; it is a systematic review, not a meta-analysis, and the authors list
  theoretical benefits — the numeric strength is not established (`uncertain` size; B1 §6 grade M).
- The spiral = **spacing** at the scale of years plus **revisiting with elaboration** (A4 S for
  spacing). What the spiral adds over plain spacing — re-encoding at greater depth — is plausible
  but not separately tested (`uncertain`).
- **Discovery learning**: pure discovery underperforms guided instruction for novices (KSC06;
  LH16 d = 0.50 for *guided* over unguided inquiry — B1 §11). Bruner's name is attached to both
  the evidenced part (fading) and the unsupported part (minimal guidance).

**Serves:** A3 rows 2–3 (concepts, principles) in maths and science; row 4 via CPA.

**Grade:** spiral **T** (as a package) / spacing mechanism **S**; CPA / concreteness fading **M**
(`uncertain` size); pure discovery **X** for novices (`contested` edge for guided discovery, M).

**Use as learner.** For a new abstract idea, find (or draw) a concrete instance first, then a
diagram, then the notation — and *drop* the concrete version once the notation makes sense
(staying concrete is the trap; B1 M51). Revisit a core idea months later at a harder level.

**Use as teacher.** Plan topics to recur across the course with increasing demand; run
concrete → pictorial → abstract inside each lesson and fade explicitly. Do not read "discovery"
as "don't tell them".

## 6. Siegfried Engelmann (1931–2019) — Direct Instruction (DI)

**What he built.** Scripted, fast-paced, small-group lessons (DISTAR, from the late 1960s) based
on a theory of "faultless communication": sequences of examples and non-examples designed so only
one interpretation is possible; choral responses; immediate correction; mastery before moving on.
Stockard et al. separate **Direct Instruction** (capital D, the Engelmann–Becker programs) from
"**little di**" — "a broad set of educational programs that incorporate elements of systematic or
explicit instruction" (St18, full text, p. 480).

**Evidence (St18, full text — author repository copy).** 328 studies, 413 designs, 3,999 effects
(1966–2016), all designs for which an effect could be computed (not only randomized). Initial
estimates: **total 0.54** [95% CI 0.49, 0.59]; reading 0.51, maths 0.55, language 0.54, spelling
0.66; joint-model estimate for the total **0.60** [0.54, 0.66]; affective outcomes 0.14 (not
significant); "effects showed little decline during maintenance", and larger with more exposure;
publication characteristics "were not systematically related to effect estimates." DI was one of
the models in **Project Follow Through** (late 1960s onward; DI chosen by 19 sites). **Conflict of
interest, stated by the authors:** "some of the work on this article was completed while the
authors were employed on a part-time basis by the National Institute for Direct Instruction" (a
nonprofit that supports schools implementing DI), and the search began from NIFDI's bibliography.

**Mechanism (Part A).** (a) Example/non-example sequences built to isolate the defining feature =
**concept induction with contrasting cases** (A3 row 2; A4 bridge row 2). (b) Very high response
rates = many **retrieval/production** events per lesson. (c) Immediate correction = **feedback**.
(d) Mastery before moving on = the **prerequisite gate** (A5). (e) Explicit modeling for novices
= the **guidance** result (KSC06; worked examples). Every element maps to an S-grade mechanism —
which is why the package works where it is used as designed.

**Serves:** A3 rows 1, 2, 4 (facts, concepts, procedures) — especially early reading and
arithmetic. Not designed for row 3 open-ended understanding or row 6.

**Grade:** **S** for DI programs on academic achievement (large, consistent, maintained; caveats:
developer-affiliated authors, mixed designs → upper bound). Little-di (explicit instruction in
general): **S** for novices (A4). Affective outcomes: not shown.

**Use as learner.** When learning a category, collect **non-examples** that look close but fail
one feature — not only examples. Answer out loud or in writing every few minutes; never let a
lesson run 20 minutes without you producing something.

**Use as teacher.** Sequence examples so the only thing that varies is the feature you are
teaching; include near-miss non-examples; ask for a response from everyone (choral or
mini-whiteboards) every minute or two; correct immediately; do not move on until the group
is firm.

## 7. Toru Kumon (1914–1995) — self-paced worksheets "at the right level"

**What he built.** Daily worksheets in very small steps; the starting level is set by a
diagnostic test "slightly lower than the student's concurrent maximum capacity"; instructors "do
not conduct lectures; they simply observe students' progress" and move the level up or down;
worksheets "contain example questions with hints"; progress is tracked in a record book; the
target is speed + accuracy (SMS24 description, full text of the 2020 working-paper version).

**Evidence (SMS24 — Sawada, Mahmud, Seki, Le & Kawarazaki; *Economic Development and Cultural
Change* 72(4) 1893–1921, 2024; read at full text in the CIRJE-F-1127 revised working paper).**
Randomized trial in BRAC non-formal primary schools, Bangladesh, grades 3–4; 974 students in 34
schools at the start; 30 minutes a day **inside school time**, no homework; 8 months (Aug 2015–
Apr 2016). Effects: **2.073 SD** on the Kumon diagnostic test **scored per minute** (speed +
accuracy); **0.501–1.212 SD** on time-unadjusted test scores; catch-up on self-esteem for
lower-ability pupils; a longer-term gain on the national Primary School Certificate exam;
benefit-cost ratio > 1 under the authors' assumptions. **Caveats:** one trial, one country,
very low baseline; the largest number is on Kumon's *own* timed test (aligned with what was
practised); the Kumon Institute supplied the materials; 5 schools dropped for baseline
mismanagement.

**Mechanism (Part A).** Practice at the learner's level with many short items = **calibration to
the edge** (engine principle 4; ~80% success); daily practice = **spacing**; repeated production
to speed = **memory & fluency** (KLI) / automaticity (A82: declarative → procedural); small steps
with an example = **worked-example → completion** (A4).

**Serves:** A3 row 4 (procedures — arithmetic) and row 1 (number facts) to automaticity. Not a
method for row 3 understanding; it does not claim to be.

**Grade:** **M** (one strong RCT with big effects in a low-baseline setting; independent academic
authors but Kumon-supplied materials; no second RCT found). Mechanisms S.

**Use as learner.** For a procedure you must do fast (algebra manipulation, KCL equations,
Verilog syntax), do short daily timed sets at a level where you get most right, and step the
level up only when both accuracy and speed hold.

**Use as teacher.** Place each student by a diagnostic, not by age; keep daily practice short;
move levels by performance; track accuracy *and* time.

## 8. Japanese lesson study (*jugyō kenkyū*, Meiji era →)

**What it is.** A small group of teachers co-plans a lesson against a shared goal, one teaches it
while the others observe the *pupils*, then the group analyses what pupils did and revises
(ED581145 description). It is a method for **teacher** learning; pupil gains are indirect.

**Evidence.**
- **Positive RCT (LP17, abstract):** 39 educator teams across the US randomly assigned to
  locally managed lesson study **with a fractions resource kit** vs 2 control conditions, 3 months:
  "significantly greater improvement of educators' and students' fractions knowledge." The
  effect is of lesson study **plus research-based content** (the kit used linear/number-line
  representations).
- **Null RCT (MWW18 / ED581145, ERIC):** England, 181 primary schools (12,747 pupils), Edge Hill
  programme (lesson study + "Talk for Learning"), two years: students of treated teachers
  "perform no better on national tests a year after the program ended." Caveats: some control
  schools ran similar activities; a UCL critique disputes the methodology.

**Mechanism (Part A).** For the teachers: observing pupils' responses = feedback on teaching;
co-planning = elaboration. The difference between the two trials is consistent with A4's lesson
that **process without the right content** does little — the US trial supplied research-based
content; the English one supplied a generic talk focus. `uncertain` interpretation (two trials).

**Serves:** teacher learning (outside A3 rows); indirectly, whatever content the lesson targets.

**Grade:** **T–M** (one positive RCT with content kit, one large null RCT without). Same reading
as the general professional-development literature: content-focused PD helps more than
process-only PD (not verified this sprint → `uncertain`).

**Use as learner.** Study groups work like lesson study only if someone brings the *right content*
(a good explanation, a solved example); pooling confusion does not produce understanding.

**Use as teacher.** If you run lesson study, attach it to a specific, evidence-based content
resource and observe what pupils *produce*, not what the teacher does.

## 9. The Oxford/Cambridge tutorial (19th century →)

**What it is.** One to three students write an essay or problem sheet each week, then defend it
to a tutor in a one-hour discussion.

**Evidence.** No controlled outcome study of the tutorial was found. Ashwin 2005 (Ash05, ERIC
abstract) interviewed 28 Oxford students and found **four qualitatively different conceptions**
of the tutorial, "from the tutor explaining to the student what the student did not know" to
tutor and student "exchanging different points of view and both coming to a new understanding";
students' conceptions related to how they prepared. That is evidence about *experience*, not
learning outcomes.

**Mechanism (Part A).** The weekly essay is **generation** and **retrieval** under a deadline
(A6, A4); the defence is **elaborative interrogation / self-explanation** under questioning
(A4 S/M) with immediate expert **feedback**; small group = tutoring dosage (NOQ24's ≥ 3 sessions a
week is far more than the tutorial's one, though each tutorial is preceded by long independent
work).

**Serves:** A3 row 3 (principles, models, argument) and row 5 (choosing approaches).

**Grade:** **T** as a package (no outcome evidence; selective students confound any comparison);
mechanisms **S/M**.

**Use as learner.** Recreate it: write a one-page answer to a hard question each week, then have
someone (or the engine) attack it, and write the revision.

**Use as teacher.** The value is in the student's *produced* work and the questioning of it; a
tutorial that becomes the tutor explaining (Ashwin's first conception) loses the mechanism.

## 10. Paulo Freire (1921–1997) — critical pedagogy, against the "banking" model

**What he wrote (Fr70, *Pedagogy of the Oppressed*, ch. 2, read at full text, 30th-anniversary
English edition).** "Education thus becomes an act of depositing, in which the students are the
depositories and the teacher is the depositor … This is the 'banking' concept of education, in
which the scope of action allowed to the students extends only as far as receiving, filing, and
storing the deposits." The alternative is **problem-posing** education through **dialogue**, in
which "the teacher is no longer merely the-one-who-teaches, but one who is himself taught in
dialogue with the students." The context is adult literacy among the poor in Brazil, and the
book's aims are political (liberation), not retention.

**Mechanism (Part A).** Freire's target — students who only receive, file and store — matches
what cognitive science also rejects: passive reception without **generation** or **retrieval**
(A1, A6). His remedy — dialogue about problems in the learners' own lives — adds **relevance /
autonomy** (SDT, engine `research/05` Part B) and **elaboration** on prior knowledge (A5). But
the problem-posing model contains no mechanism for practice, spacing or feedback on accuracy, and
read as "the teacher must not transmit knowledge" it collides with the guidance evidence (KSC06).

**Serves:** motivation and the A3 row 6/attitudes edge (row 9 in A3, out of method scope); adult
literacy.

**Grade:** **T** — no controlled outcome evidence for problem-posing pedagogy on learning was
found; the book makes political and philosophical arguments, not empirical ones. The anti-banking
critique is consistent with S-grade findings (generation, retrieval), so the *critique* is better
supported than the *replacement*.

**Use as learner.** Freire's useful question: am I just receiving, filing and storing? If yes,
switch to producing (questions, answers, explanations).

**Use as teacher.** Connect material to problems that matter to the learners and talk *with*
them — and still teach the content explicitly and practise it. Dialogue is a frame, not a
substitute for instruction.

## Summary (B2, all 10)

| Builder | Core move | Serves (A3) | Grade (method / mechanism) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Keller (PSI) | small units, unit test to mastery, proctors, self-pace | 1, 4 | **M–S** on course exams (KKC79, KKB90) / retrieval + feedback S; self-pace lowers completion |
| Bloom | formative test → correctives → parallel test; 2σ | all | ML **M** aligned tests, **T** standardized (Sl87); tutoring S but ≈0.3 SD at scale (NOQ24), 0.79 lab (VL11); **2σ X** |
| Gagné | 5 capabilities; hierarchies; nine events | A3 hub | nine events **T** / components S; taxonomy = framework |
| Ausubel | prior knowledge first; advance organizers | 2, 3 | organizer **T–M** (LAA80, Table I .20) / prior-knowledge principle S |
| Bruner | spiral; enactive→iconic→symbolic; discovery | 2, 3, 4 | spiral T / spacing S; CPA **M**; pure discovery **X** for novices |
| Engelmann (DI) | faultless example sequences, scripted, choral, mastery | 1, 2, 4 | **S** (St18 0.54–0.60; author COI) |
| Kumon | daily worksheets at the right level, speed + accuracy | 4, 1 | **M** (one RCT, SMS24: 0.50–1.21 SD) |
| Lesson study | teachers co-plan, observe pupils, revise | teacher learning | **T–M** (LP17 + kit positive; MWW18 null) |
| Oxbridge tutorial | weekly essay, defended | 3, 5 | **T** / generation + questioning S/M |
| Freire | anti-banking; dialogue, problem-posing | motivation | **T** (critique fits evidence; replacement untested) |

**Pattern across B2.** (1) This is the first generation whose methods were **tested as
packages**, and the packages that won (PSI, DI, Kumon, mastery learning on aligned tests) are
the ones built from **frequent production + immediate feedback + a mastery gate** — the same S
mechanisms B1 found scattered across 2,400 years, now systematized. (2) The **size** of an effect
depends heavily on how it is measured: short studies on tests aligned to the treatment (Bloom's
2σ, Kumon's timed test, PSI course exams) give big numbers; long studies on standardized tests
give small ones (Slavin's +.04; tutoring 0.29 at scale). Always ask "which test, over how long?"
(A8 §3 kit). (3) The methods that are mostly *philosophy* (Freire, Bruner's discovery, the

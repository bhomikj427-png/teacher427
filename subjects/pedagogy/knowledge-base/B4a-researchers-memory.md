# B4a — Researchers who turned memory & practice findings into methods

> Part B unit. These people *are* the evidence base of Part A, so each entry does not re-argue
> the mechanism (pointer to the A file) — it records **what they turned into a method a learner
> or teacher can run**, the best test of that method *in real use*, and the boundary. Format as
> B1–B3: what they built → mechanism (pointer) → **serves** (A3 row) · **grade** · **use as
> learner** · **use as teacher**. Grade = the method as used in practice / the mechanism.
>
> Written S9a (2026-11-15).

## 1. Robert A. Bjork & Elizabeth L. Bjork — desirable difficulties

**What they built.** (a) The **new theory of disuse** (BB92): every item has a *storage strength*
(how well learned) and a *retrieval strength* (how accessible now); retrieving an item when
retrieval strength is low produces the largest gain in storage strength. (b) From it, the
practical rule **"desirable difficulties"** (BB11): conditions that slow apparent progress during
study — spacing, interleaving, testing instead of restudy, varying the conditions of practice,
generating rather than reading — tend to improve long-term retention and transfer. (c) The
**learning ≠ performance** distinction (SB15): performance during training is a poor index of
learning, and learners and teachers are misled by it.

**Mechanism.** → A1 §2–§4 (storage/retrieval strength, learning vs performance) and A2.

**Boundary.** "Desirable" is the operative word: a difficulty is desirable only if the learner can
eventually overcome it (BB11). Difficulty that blocks retrieval altogether, or overloads a
novice, is just difficulty (M16; A1).

**Serves:** all A3 rows (it is a theory of *when* practice builds memory); strongest for rows 1, 2,
4, 5.

**Grade:** **S** — the four core difficulties (spacing, retrieval, interleaving, generation) each
have meta-analytic support (A4 §1); the framework itself is a theory, graded by its predictions.

**Use as learner.** If a study session feels smooth and fast, suspect it. Space the next session,
test instead of rereading, mix problem types. Judge a method by a test days later, not by how the
session felt.

**Use as teacher.** Expect — and tell learners to expect — lower practice scores from spaced,
mixed, tested practice, with better results on the delayed exam. Do not grade the practice phase
as if it were the outcome.

## 2. Henry L. Roediger III, Jeffrey D. Karpicke, Mark A. McDaniel — test-enhanced learning

**What they built.** Low-stakes quizzing as a **learning** tool, not an assessment tool: lab
demonstrations (RK06: restudy wins at 5 minutes, testing wins at a week; KB11: retrieval beats
concept mapping) carried into real classrooms, and the popular synthesis *Make It Stick* (Brown,
Roediger & McDaniel, 2014 — book, not read).

**Classroom evidence (both read at abstract this sprint).**
- **McD11** (McDaniel, Agarwal, Huelser, McDermott & Roediger 2011): 8th-grade science, multiple-
  choice quizzes **with feedback** on some course content but not other: **13–25% gains** on unit
  exams for quizzed content; **review quizzing** before the unit exam produced the greatest gains;
  benefits "persisted on cumulative semester and end-of-year exams."
- **RAMM11** (Roediger, Agarwal, McDaniel & McDermott 2011): 6th-grade social studies, three
  experiments, within-subject: quizzed items beat both non-quizzed and twice-presented (restudied)
  items on chapter and **semester** exams, including when the exam required short answers after
  multiple-choice quizzes.

**Mechanism.** → A1 §3 (retrieval strength), A4 retrieval-practice row (ATS17, D13 "high").

**Serves:** A3 rows 1 and 3 (facts; principles when the questions ask for explanations — A1 §5,
verbatim prompts build verbatim knowledge).

**Grade:** **S** — lab + randomized-within-class classroom studies on real grades, maintained to
end of year. Caveat: the classroom studies come from one research group (Roediger/McDaniel
labs, often with Agarwal) — replication by other groups is in ATS17's 272 effects.

**Use as learner.** Quiz yourself after each section and again before the exam (review quizzing
was the strongest placement). Check the answer every time.

**Use as teacher.** Short, low-stakes quizzes with immediate feedback; schedule a review quiz
before each unit test; multiple-choice works if feedback follows (M64).

## 3. John Dunlosky & Katherine A. Rawson — technique ranking and successive relearning

**What they built.** (a) The **utility ranking** of ten common study techniques (D13): practice
testing and distributed practice **high**; elaborative interrogation, self-explanation and
interleaved practice **moderate**; summarization, highlighting, keyword mnemonic, imagery for
text, rereading **low** (list verified at A4; moderate-list wording at full text → S11 backlog).
(b) **Successive relearning** with a concrete prescription (RD11, 3 experiments, 533 students,
> 100,000 hand-scored short-answer recalls, retention measured 1–4 months later): the initial
criterion and relearning are subadditive — extra initial recalls help less once relearning is
added; "Relearning had pronounced effects on long-term retention with a relatively minimal cost";
prescriptive conclusion: **"practice recalling concepts to an initial criterion of 3 correct
recalls and then to relearn them 3 times at widely spaced intervals."** (c) Classroom version
(RDS13 — course exams; numbers → S11 backlog).

**Mechanism.** → A1 (retrieval + spacing); A4 successive-relearning row.

**Serves:** A3 rows 1 and 3 (key-term definitions and concepts were the RD11 material).

**Grade:** **S** (well-replicated lab + classroom; the 3 + 3 rule is one lab's prescription for
concept definitions — a strong default, not a law).

**Use as learner.** For each concept: recall it correctly 3 times in the first session (drop it
from the session after the third correct recall), then come back to it in 3 later sessions spread
over weeks, each time until one correct recall. This is exactly the engine's RELEARN step.

**Use as teacher.** Build relearning into the course: the same key items reappear in quizzes on
later weeks. Teach the ranking explicitly — students default to the "low" techniques (A7).

## 4. Doug Rohrer, Shana K. Carpenter, Sean H. K. Kang — spacing and interleaving in real classrooms

**What they built.** Interleaved **mathematics homework**: "an interleaved assignment is arranged
so that no 2 consecutive problems require the same strategy", forcing the student to *choose* the
strategy from the problem itself (RDS15 abstract). Plus spacing reviews (C06 co-authored by
Rohrer; Carpenter's classroom spacing work).

**Evidence.**
- **RDS15** (Rohrer, Dedrick & Stershic 2015): 126 7th-graders, same problems over 3 months,
  arranged blocked or partly interleaved, then review, then an unannounced test 1 or 30 days later:
  interleaved higher, **d = 0.42 (1 day), 0.79 (30 days)** — the advantage *grew* with delay.
- **RDHC20** (Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig & Cheung 2020): **preregistered cluster RCT**, 54 7th-grade
  maths classes, 4 months of periodic interleaved vs blocked assignments, then an interleaved
  review for both; unannounced test one month later: **61% vs 38%, d = 0.83**; teachers
  implemented it without training and supported it in a survey taken before they knew the results.
- Meta-level: BR19 (g = 0.42 overall; maths small but significant; reversed for expository text
  — A4).

**Mechanism.** → A4 interleaving row; A3 row 5 (conditional knowledge: *which* method applies).
Blocked practice lets the student know the strategy before reading the problem; interleaving
exercises the choice.

**Serves:** A3 row 5 (when to use what) and row 2 (discriminating similar categories).

**Grade:** **S** for interleaved maths practice in classrooms (preregistered RCT, large effect,
replicates the earlier experiment) / **M** for interleaving in general (BR19 moderators). The
engine's "block first, then interleave" is consistent: in both studies, new skills were still
introduced in the lesson; interleaving rearranged the *practice* problems.

**Use as learner.** After learning two or more methods (e.g., nodal and mesh analysis), practise
from a mixed set where you must decide which method each problem needs — and do it again a month
later.

**Use as teacher.** Keep the lesson's introduction blocked; make a portion of each homework set
problems from earlier units, in mixed order. Expect lower homework scores and higher test scores.

## 5. Piotr Woźniak — SuperMemo and the SM-2 algorithm

**What he built (primary: Woźniak 1990, master's thesis excerpt "Application of a computer to
improve the results obtained in working with the SuperMemo method", SM-2 "Algorithm 3.2", read at
full text).** A program (first version December 1987) that schedules each flashcard separately:
first interval **1 day**, second **6 days**, then each interval = previous × the item's
**E-Factor** (easiness factor, start 2.5, minimum 1.3), updated after each review from the
learner's self-graded response quality (0–5). Two design observations from the source: intervals
grew "by an approximately constant factor", and items that kept falling below E-Factor 1.3
"always seemed to have inherent flaws in their formulation (usually they did not conform to the
minimum information principle)" — i.e. bad cards, not bad memory. SM-2 is the ancestor of Anki's
scheduler.

**Evidence for the method.** The mechanism is spacing + retrieval (S). Whether *adaptive*
scheduling beats a simple fixed spacing schedule was tested by **Lin14** (Lindsey, Shroyer,
Pashler & Mozer 2014): in a semester-long middle-school foreign-language course, personalized
review (a statistical memory model per student and item) gave **16.5%** better retention on a
post-semester cumulative exam than massed study and **10.0%** better than one-size-fits-all spaced
review, time-matched. That is a model-based scheduler, not SM-2 itself. Two more controlled tests
point the same way (S12c-rest, 2026-11-16): **MMK16** (Mettler, Massey & Kellman 2016, full text) —
72 undergraduates learned 24 map items, 4 presentations each in one session; an adaptive scheduler
using response time + accuracy beat fixed **expanding** and fixed **equal** spacing at a 1-week
delayed test (0.42 vs 0.31 vs 0.30 accuracy), with **no** difference between expanding and equal; in
Experiment 2, fixed schedules *copied* from adaptive learners (same average spacing) still did worse,
so the gain came from fitting each item and learner, not from longer gaps. Lab task, short material,
the developers' own algorithm. **UZST21** (Upadhyay et al. 2021, abstract): randomized trial with
~50,000 users of a driving-licence app — machine-learning item selection gave ~69% longer retention
than random or difficulty-ordered selection after controlling study time, but more users quit in the
first days. Neither tests SM-2. **No controlled test of SM-2 against a fixed schedule was found**
(searched S9a and S12c-rest) — `uncertain`; Anki-style adaptive scheduling is supported *by
analogy* to these adaptive schedulers, not directly.

**Serves:** A3 row 1 (facts), and row 3 if cards ask for explanations, not labels.

**Grade:** spaced-retrieval flashcards **S** (mechanism); personalized scheduling **M** (Lin14
classroom study + MMK16 lab + UZST21 field RCT, all from the schedulers' developers); SM-2's specific formula **T** (heuristic by the author's own
account: "constructed heuristically").

**Use as learner.** Use a spaced-repetition app for facts and definitions; grade yourself
honestly; if a card keeps failing, **rewrite it smaller** (Woźniak's minimum-information
observation) rather than drilling it harder.

**Use as teacher.** Give students a ready deck of the course's key items and the instruction to
review daily; check card quality (one fact per card).

## 6. Andy Matuschak & Michael Nielsen — the mnemonic medium (*Quantum Country*)

**What they built (primary: Matuschak & Nielsen 2019, "How can we develop transformative tools for
thought?", read S9a).** An essay on quantum computing with **112 spaced-repetition questions
embedded in the text**; readers answer them while reading, then get emailed review sessions at
growing intervals.

**Evidence (their own data, stated as preliminary).** Six months after release, 195 users had
shown one month of retention on ≥ 80% of the cards; ≈ 54 days of demonstrated retention per
question typically took about **95 minutes** of total review; in a small two-week experiment
(16 regular-review vs 25 delayed-review users), regular review moved accuracy 89% → 96%, delayed
91% → 87%. Their own caveats: "preliminary results, and need more investigation"; feedback
"should be taken with a grain of salt"; one claim later "retracted" after an analysis error. The
rest of the essay (read to the end, S12c-rest) adds no further data — only user interviews without
numbers and the aim to "increase effective human memory by an order of magnitude", stated as a goal,
not a result ("very early days").

**Mechanism.** Retrieval + spacing (A4 S), placed *inside* the explanation so the questions
target the text's ideas — a form of embedded prequestions/adjunct questions plus successive
relearning.

**Serves:** A3 rows 1 and 3 (facts and concepts from expository text).

**Grade:** **T** as a medium (self-selected users, no comparison with the same essay without
prompts, the authors' own data); mechanism **S**.

**Use as learner.** When reading a technical text, write 5–10 recall questions per section as you
go and put them into your spaced-repetition system — your own mnemonic medium.

**Use as teacher.** Embed recall questions in notes and readings, and schedule them for review;
it costs little and rides on S-grade mechanisms, even if the medium itself is not yet tested.

## 7. K. Anders Ericsson (1947–2020) — deliberate practice

**What he built.** **Deliberate practice**: effortful practice designed (ideally by a teacher) to
improve specific weaknesses, with feedback, at the edge of ability — distinguished from mere
repetition or play (EKT93, *Psychological Review* 100(3) 363–406). The seminal claim, from
retrospective practice estimates of student violinists: "individual differences in ultimate
performance can largely be accounted for by differential amounts of past and current levels of
practice" (quoted in MM19).

**Evidence.**
- **MHO14** meta (A5 §4): deliberate practice explains a minority of performance variance — 26%
  in games, 21% music, 18% sports, **4% in education**, < 1% professions.
- **MM19** (Macnamara & Maitra 2019, preregistered-style replication of EKT93, double-blind):
  **"We did not replicate the core finding that accumulated amounts of deliberate practice
  corresponded to each skill level"**; the effect was "substantial, but considerably smaller than
  the original study's"; teacher-designed practice was perceived as *less* relevant by the
  violinists than practice alone.
- Ericsson's group disputes MHO14's definition of deliberate practice (A5 §4); the dispute is live.

**Mechanism.** → A5 §4; A4 deliberate-practice row. Practice targeted at weaknesses with feedback
= calibration to the edge (principle 4) + feedback (A4).

**Serves:** A3 rows 4 and 8 (procedures, motor skills) — the domains where it was studied.

**Grade:** **M** for skill domains (practice matters, its share is modest and smaller on
replication) / **T** as an explanation of academic achievement (4%). The popular "10,000 hours"
version is **X** (M34).

**Use as learner.** Practice the part you are worst at, with a way to know immediately whether
each attempt was right — but do not expect practice hours alone to predict where you end up.

**Use as teacher.** Design practice around diagnosed weaknesses, not around the syllabus order;
give feedback on each attempt.

## 8. Philip J. Kellman (and Christine Massey) — perceptual learning modules (PLMs)

**What they built.** Short computer modules where learners make **many fast classification
judgments** — e.g., match an equation to its graph, pick which of several fraction representations
is equivalent — with immediate feedback, across many varied examples, often with adaptive
sequencing (Kel08, ERIC; KMS10). The aim is the "seeing" part of expertise (A3 row 7): extracting
structure and fluency, which declarative and procedural teaching neglect.

**Evidence.**
- **Kel08** (lab/school experiments, fractions and algebra; abstract): "significant and persisting
  learning gains for students using PLMs" — the developers' own studies.
- **Massey, Porter, Desimone & Kellman 2016** (IES efficacy RCT, conference poster — not
  peer-reviewed; MPDK16): within-teacher random assignment of 6th-grade classes, two cohorts,
  four PLMs: treatment better in **three of four modules (cohort 1) and two (cohort 2)**; largest
  **ES = .48** (area measurement); **fraction modules mixed**; significant effect on a composite
  **one-year delayed** posttest. Co-investigators from Penn (Porter, Desimone) add some
  independence; peer-reviewed report not found (`uncertain`).
- Medical PALMs (histopathology, ECG, dermatology — Krasne & Kellman lab) report large gains in
  small studies (search leads only; not read).

**Mechanism.** Perceptual learning = induction from many classified examples with feedback (A3 row
7, KLI "induction & refinement"); overlaps with interleaving of confusable categories (BR19:
paintings g = 0.67).

**Serves:** A3 row 7 (perceptual / pattern recognition), also row 2 (categories).

**Grade:** **M−** (`uncertain`): one randomized classroom efficacy study with modest-to-moderate,
durable but mixed effects (poster), plus developer studies. This partly closes the KMS10 backlog
row (A3 row 7 can move from `uncertain` toward M− at S11 if the peer-reviewed report is found).

**Use as learner.** For anything you must *recognize* (circuit topologies, waveform shapes,
which theorem a problem needs), drill fast classification of many varied examples with instant
answers — dozens per session — instead of reading more explanations.

**Use as teacher.** Build short "which one is it?" drills with many examples and immediate
feedback; mix the categories students confuse.

## Summary (B4a, all 8)

| Researcher(s) | Method they made usable | Serves (A3) | Grade (method / mechanism) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bjork & Bjork | desirable difficulties; storage vs retrieval strength | all (1, 2, 4, 5) | **S** / theory |
| Roediger, Karpicke, McDaniel | low-stakes classroom quizzing with feedback | 1, 3 | **S** (McD11, RAMM11 to end of year) |
| Dunlosky & Rawson | technique ranking; relearning 3 + 3 | 1, 3 | **S** (RD11, RDS13) |
| Rohrer, Carpenter, Kang | interleaved maths homework; classroom spacing | 5, 2 | **S** maths (RDHC20 d 0.83, prereg.) / M general (BR19) |
| Woźniak | SM-2 spaced-repetition scheduling | 1, (3) | flashcards S; adaptive scheduling M (Lin14, MMK16, UZST21); SM-2 formula T, no SM-2-vs-fixed test |
| Matuschak & Nielsen | mnemonic medium (prompts inside text) | 1, 3 | **T** / mechanism S |
| Ericsson | deliberate practice | 4, 8 | **M** skills, T academic (MHO14 4%; MM19 non-replication); 10,000 h **X** |
| Kellman & Massey | perceptual learning modules | 7, 2 | **M−** `uncertain` (MPDK16 poster, ES ≤ .48) |

**Pattern across B4a.** (1) The strongest methods in this whole subject are the simplest:
**quiz with feedback, space it, relearn it, mix it** — and they now have *classroom* evidence on
real grades (McD11, RAMM11, RDHC20), not just lab evidence. (2) The tools built on them (SM-2,
mnemonic medium, PLMs) inherit the mechanism's strength but are themselves thinly tested —
grade the tool lower than the mechanism. (3) Deliberate practice is the one famous result here
that **shrank** on replication (MM19) and has a small share in academic learning (MHO14).
(4) A consistent boundary across all eight: practice *scores* during training mislead (Bjork's
learning ≠ performance; Rohrer's homework scores) — the delayed test is the verdict.

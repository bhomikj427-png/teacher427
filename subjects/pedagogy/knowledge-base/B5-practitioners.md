## 1. Justin Sung (iCanStudy) — relational learning, encoding first

**Full audit:** `justin-sung-audit.md` (J1–J28, each claim verdicted; S10a). This entry only
summarises §5 of the audit — it does not re-argue it.

**What he offers.** Studying ≠ learning; learn by *relating and organizing* ideas (non-linear
notes, mind maps, "priming" before study), aim at higher-order thinking early, and treat
spaced-repetition *systems* (flashcard apps) as secondary to encoding. Paid course (iCanStudy).

**Best real-use test.** None published. His outcome data (ICS22, self-published report) are not
peer-reviewed; no independent study of the iCanStudy method was found (Crossref, Europe PMC, web;
S10a). The public expert response (BK22, Keep 2022) and Sung's joint reply (JS-CO, 2023) ended
at "do both": relate *and* retrieve, spaced.

**Mechanism (pointers).** What is sound matches Part A: depth of processing and generation (A6),
organizing knowledge into schemas (A5), retrieval + spacing (A4 S). Where he over-reaches:
"retrieval only fights forgetting" (J4, withdrawn by him in JS-CO; A1 §5, Ag19), effort or
confusion as a signal of learning (J3, J26; KGF19; M76), trainable working-memory capacity (J25;
A2, M22), start at the top of Bloom (J23 beyond Ag19; M75).

**Serves:** A3 rows 3 (principles/models) and 2 (concepts) — relating ideas; row 6 (metacognitive)
for the studying-vs-learning message.

**Grade:** as a **translator** of mainstream findings **M**; **his own method T** (testimonial,
unpublished, commercial conflict of interest). Unusually willing to correct himself in public —
raises trust in his framing, is not evidence for the method.

**Use as learner.** Take the "relate, don't transcribe" habit (draw how ideas connect, ask "how
does this link to X?") — then still test yourself on it after a delay. Ignore "if it feels
confusing it's working."

**Use as teacher.** His studying-vs-learning framing is a good hook for students. Pair every
"organize it" task with a later retrieval task; never let a mind map stand in for a test.

## 2. Scott H. Young — *Ultralearning*, the MIT Challenge, the "Feynman Technique"

**What he offers.** Self-directed, intense learning projects ("ultralearning"; the 2011–12 MIT
Challenge, 1 Oct 2011 – 26 Sep 2012: 33 listed MIT computer-science classes self-studied from
public materials, "passed" = ≥ 50% on the final exam, **graded by himself** against MIT solutions;
he notes stricter grading would drop five finals below passing (SY-MIT, his project page, read)),
and the **"Feynman Technique"**.

**The Feynman Technique — origin now settled (S10b-1).** Young's own 2011 bootcamp transcript
(SY-FT11, "Never Forget an Idea Again with The Feynman Technique", PDF dated 12 Aug 2011 — read in
full) gives the four steps verbatim: "Step One: Choose Your Concept" (write it at the top of a blank
page) → "Step Two: Pretend You're Teaching the Idea to a New Student" (write out the explanation) →
"Step Three: Whenever You Get Stuck, Go Back to the Book" → "Step Four: Simplify and Create
Analogies". The name comes from an anecdote (Feynman challenging mathematicians to explain ideas in
simple terms), not from any method Feynman described. In 2024 Young wrote that it is "the most
popular piece of studying advice I've ever come up with" and "only loosely based on Richard
Feynman's practices" (SY-FT24, read). **So: the named method is Young's (2011), inspired by a
Feynman story** — `settled` (his own words). This upgrades A8 myth 7 from `uncertain` to `settled`
on attribution.
- What Feynman's side actually shows (first-hand, Gd89 — David Goodstein, *Physics Today*, Feb
  1989, read): Feynman used "could I give a freshman lecture on it?" as his test of whether
  something was understood — asked to explain why spin-½ particles obey Fermi-Dirac statistics, he
  came back with "I couldn't reduce it to the freshman level. That means we really don't understand
  it." The same article reports that attendance by the freshmen at the *Feynman Lectures* "started
  dropping alarmingly" while faculty filled the room. So the explain-simply test is genuinely
  Feynman's; the four-step study procedure is not. (Gleick's *Genius* "Notebook of Things I Don't
  Know About" — seen only in excerpts, `uncertain` wording; book not read.)

**Claims to check inside the technique.** The 2011 transcript promises you can "deeply understand
an idea in 20 minutes, that will stick with you for years" — **no test; X as stated**. Its useful
move is the last one: go through it "without looking at your reference materials … a really good
way to self-test" — that is retrieval practice (A4 S). Young's own later limits (SY-FT, SY-FT24):
slow ("takes too much time"), and "If you're cutting out practice in favor of self-explanations, you
may end up worse off!"

**Best real-use test.** No controlled study of the named four-step technique was found. Its parts
are tested: self-explanation g = 0.55 (Bi18, 64 reports), learning-by-teaching / explaining to
others (FM16, generative strategy), retrieval when done closed-book (A4). *Ultralearning* and the
MIT Challenge are a single self-report (n = 1, no comparison, own grading of exams) → testimonial.

**Mechanism (pointers).** Self-explanation exposes gaps (illusion of explanatory depth, A7);
writing an explanation from memory is generation + retrieval (A6, A1 §5); simplifying and
analogy = elaboration (A6). Step 3 ("go back to the book") is where it slides into re-reading if
the page is open the whole time.

**Serves:** A3 row 3 (principles/models) mainly; row 6 (metacognitive: finds what you don't
understand). Weak for rows 1 and 4 (facts, procedures) — Young's own "not a replacement for
practice problems".

**Grade:** Feynman Technique **M–S via its parts** (self-explanation S, closed-book retrieval S;
the packaged method untested); ultralearning / MIT Challenge **T** (n = 1, self-reported);
as a translator **M** (he states the limits himself).

**Use as learner.** Do it closed-book: explain the idea on a blank page from memory, mark the
exact point you got stuck, look *only* that up, then redo it later from blank. Follow it with
practice problems — it does not replace them.

**Use as teacher.** "Explain it to a new student" makes a good CHECK / teach-back prompt (engine
loop step 7). Don't call it Feynman's method; credit the parts (self-explanation, retrieval).

## 3. Barbara Oakley — *A Mind for Numbers*, *Learning How to Learn* (MOOC); focused/diffuse modes, chunking

**What she offers.** A learner-facing package: two thinking modes — **focused** (concentrated work
on a problem) and **diffuse** (relaxed, unfocused; step away and let it settle) — with the advice
to alternate them; **chunking** (practise until steps fuse into one unit); plus mainstream items
(interleaving, deliberate practice, low-stakes testing, sleep, exercise, procrastination).
Delivered through *Learning How to Learn* (LHTL, with Terrence Sejnowski), "nearly 2.5 million
registered learners in its first 4 years" (OS19, full text read via Europe PMC).

**How she grounds it (OS19).** "LHTL begins by describing the difference between the brain's task
positive and task negative ('default mode') networks", citing Immordino-Yang et al. 2012, Fox et
al. 2005 and Raichle — i.e. resting-state neuroscience, not learning experiments. Chunking is cited
to Guida, Gobet et al. 2012 (chunks / long-term working memory in expertise). The paper itself is
about *why the course is popular* (multimedia design), not whether it causes learning.

**Best real-use test.**
- *The course:* the one effectiveness study OS19 cites is a 2019 AERA conference paper (Chen &
  Oakley, not a journal article): after LHTL, students "reported cramming less … spacing
  repetitions more often" and scored "higher in the final proficiency test" — self-report plus one
  outcome, design not given in OS19 → `uncertain`. Popularity is measured by enrolment and reviews
  — not learning (A8 §3 Q1).
- *Diffuse mode:* the nearest testable claim is the **incubation effect** — setting a problem aside
  before trying again. SO09 meta-analysis (*Psychological Bulletin* 2009; abstract read): a
  **positive incubation effect**, larger for divergent-thinking tasks than for insight problems;
  **longer preparation before the break gives a bigger effect**; filling the break with demanding
  tasks shrinks it; low-demand tasks beat pure rest for linguistic insight problems. So "work hard
  first, then step away" has support **for stuck problem-solving**; it is not evidence that a
  "mode" is how material gets learned or remembered. Mapping "diffuse mode" onto the default mode
  network is a metaphor built on correlational imaging (`uncertain` as mechanism).
- *Chunking:* well supported as a description of expertise (CS73; A5; C01 for limits). Her
  practice advice (master sub-steps, then combine) matches worked example → fading (A4 S).

**Mechanism (pointers).** Chunking = schemas cut working-memory load (A2, A5). Incubation:
mechanism debated (forgetting misleading cues vs unconscious work; SO09 finds different types by
problem). Sleep between sessions = consolidation (A2). The rest of her package is A4's S-list.

**Serves:** chunking → A3 rows 4 (procedures) and 3; incubation → row 3 (non-routine problems);
study-habit advice → row 6.

**Grade:** chunking **S** (mechanism) / **M** as her practice advice; diffuse mode **T as stated**
(a brain-network story), **M** for the narrower "prepare hard, then take a low-demand break when
stuck" (SO09); LHTL as a course **T** (one conference study, self-report); as a **translator M**
(mostly mainstream findings, well delivered — OS19's design principles are Mayer's, B4b §3).

**Use as learner.** When stuck after a real attempt, take a break doing something easy (walk,
chores), then return — don't take the break *instead of* the attempt. Build chunks: drill a
sub-step until it runs without thinking, then combine.

**Use as teacher.** Teach "step away when stuck" as incubation for problem-solving, with SO09's
conditions; don't teach "diffuse mode" as brain science. LHTL videos are fine as motivation —
test the content afterwards.

## 4. Doug Lemov — *Teach Like a Champion* (cold call, check for understanding, at bats)

**What he offers.** A named catalogue of classroom techniques (49 in the 2010 first edition; later
editions revised and expanded), e.g. **Cold Call** (call on students whether or not their hands
are up), **No Opt Out** (a student who can't answer comes back and gives the right answer),
**Check for Understanding**, **At Bats** (many practice repetitions), **Right Is Right** (hold out
for the fully correct answer). Uncommon Schools / charter-school origin.

**Where the techniques come from.** Observation of high-performing teachers. The *Education Next*
review of the first edition (Po11, Pondiscio 2011, read): Lemov "has invested thousands of hours in
classroom observations"; effective teaching defined as "getting big test gains in low-income
schools"; the network's results are "not proof positive of effectiveness"; teachers are "not
waiting for gold-standard research to validate" the techniques. So the method is a **craft
taxonomy from observing outliers** — the same design as Rosenshine's process-product strand (B4b
§1): good for generating candidates, correlational as evidence.

**Best real-use test.**
- *The package:* no controlled trial of *Teach Like a Champion* training on student achievement
  was found (search S10b-1; a promotional Houston pilot, no outcome data; a Kenyan RCT of a
  scripted model, NewGlobe, is sometimes linked to it but is not a test of these techniques).
- *Cold Call:* DHP13 (*Journal of Management Education* 2013, abstract read; university classes
  compared high vs low cold-calling): more students answered **voluntarily** in high cold-calling
  classes, this grew over the term, and students' **comfort** participating rose — the feared
  discomfort did not appear. Outcome = participation and comfort, **not learning**.
- *How such techniques are usually adopted* — coaching with practice: KBH18 meta (*Review of
  Educational Research* 2018, 60 causal studies; abstract read): coaching changes instruction
  0.49 SD and achievement 0.18 SD, but large effectiveness trials get "only a fraction" of
  small-trial effects. Relevant to how TLAC is trained, not a test of TLAC itself.

**Mechanism (pointers).** Cold Call + No Opt Out = every student must prepare an answer → retrieval
for all, not just volunteers (A4 S; A1 §5); Check for Understanding = formative checking (B4b §6,
package ≈0.20 KN11); At Bats = practice volume with feedback (A4 deliberate practice M); Right Is
Right = feedback precision (engine feedback protocol). The techniques are good *delivery* of
Rosenshine's principles 3, 5, 6 (B4b §1).

**Serves:** A3 rows 1 and 4 (facts and procedures, practised in class); row 3 when the questions
are "why" questions.

**Grade:** the package **T** (observational, no controlled test, self-studied by the network that
uses it); the components it delivers **M–S** via their mechanisms; Cold Call **M** for participation
(DHP13), untested for learning.

**Use as learner.** In a group or class, answer every question in your head before anyone speaks
(you are "cold-called" every time); if you were wrong, say the right answer to yourself afterwards
(No Opt Out on yourself).

**Use as teacher.** Cold Call with a warm, routine tone (DHP13: comfort went up, not down); always
loop back to a student who couldn't answer. Treat the book as a menu of ways to make everyone
retrieve and practise — not as a tested program.

## 5. Tom Sherrington — *Rosenshine's Principles in Action* (2019), *WalkThrus*

**What he offers.** A teacher-facing translation of Rosenshine (B4b §1). SH19 (John Catt 2019,
illustrations by Oliver Caviglioli; introduction + strand 1 read at full text, publisher sample
PDF) condenses the ten principles into **four strands**: *Sequencing concepts and modelling* (2, 4,
8) · *Questioning* (3, 6) · *Reviewing material* (1, 10) · *Stages of practice* (5, 7, 9). His stated
reason: ten items is a long list for a conference talk, and "I was continually skipping back and
forth to make these connections." *WalkThrus* (with Caviglioli) turns such techniques into
step-by-step illustrated routines used for coaching.

**As a translator — checked against the primary (Ros12).** Faithful: the ten principles and the
17 "instructional procedures" are reproduced from Rosenshine; the three evidence sources are
reported correctly, including "observational studies of 'master teachers'". What the translation
**adds** is confidence: the principles are "rooted in evidence that has stood the test of time";
convergence of the three sources "gives us faith"; "it turns out that they are not that complex: we
can formulate a coherent evidence-based model". What it **does not say** (in the part read): that
the master-teacher strand is correlational, so principle 7's "80%" is a description of effective
classrooms, not a tested target (B4b §1). Agreement between three sources is reassuring, but two
of them can share a bias; convergence is not a trial.

**Best real-use test.** None for the book or for *WalkThrus* as a programme (searched S10b-2: the
WalkThrus pages are resource hubs with no outcome data). The nearest evidence is coaching in
general — KBH18: instruction +0.49 SD, achievement +0.18 SD, shrinking at scale (§4).

**Mechanism (pointers).** As B4b §1: review = retrieval + spacing (A4 S); small steps = WM limits
(A2); questioning = retrieval for all + formative checking; stages of practice = worked example →
guided → independent (fading, A4 S). The strand grouping itself is a memory aid for teachers
(chunking ten items into four — A2), not a new claim about learning.

**Serves:** A3 rows 1 and 4 (facts, procedures) most directly; row 3 through questioning.

**Grade:** as translator **M–S** (accurate to the primary; slightly overstates certainty by not
flagging the correlational strand); own method (*WalkThrus* package) **T** — untested; its parts
inherit Rosenshine's M–S.

**Use as learner.** The four strands work as a self-study checklist: did I *review* (start with
recall), take *small steps with models*, *question* myself, and move from guided to *independent
practice*?

**Use as teacher.** Use the four strands to plan a lesson and to remember the ten. Read Rosenshine's
2012 article itself (12 pages) alongside; treat "80%" as a band, as in B4b §1.

## 6. Daisy Christodoulou — *Seven Myths About Education* (2013/2014); assessment & comparative judgement

**What she offers.** (a) *Seven Myths* (e-book 2013; Routledge 2014): seven beliefs she argues are
contradicted by cognitive science, verbatim (EAP17 review): "Facts prevent understanding";
"Teacher-led instruction is passive"; "The twenty-first century fundamentally changes everything";
"You can always look it up"; "We should teach transferable skills"; "Projects and activities are
the best way to learn"; "Teaching knowledge is indoctrination." Her core argument: factual knowledge
in long-term memory is what skills are made of. (b) *Making Good Progress?* (2016) and No More
Marking (she is its Director of Education): **comparative judgement (CJ)** — judges see two pieces
of work and pick the better one; many such pairs are fitted to a scale (Thurstone paired
comparisons), replacing absolute marking against a rubric.

**As a translator.** The seven myths line up with Part A: knowledge-in-LTM as the base of skill
and the limits of WM (A2; A5 schemas/expertise), domain-specific transfer (A5 — "transferable
skills" taught in the abstract transfer poorly), minimal guidance vs explicit instruction for
novices (A4; B4b Kirschner/Sweller), "look it up" fails because searching and understanding need
prior knowledge in LTM (A2/A5). Direction **correct** on all seven at the level of the research
Part A reviewed. Caution: a myth framing hides boundary conditions — e.g. projects and discovery
can work for learners with enough prior knowledge (expertise reversal, A5; productive failure, B4b
Kapur). Full chapter-by-chapter source check not done (book not read this sprint) → `uncertain` at
detail level, backlog.

**Best real-use test — of CJ (her own method).**
- *Reliability:* VBDD19 meta (*Assessment in Education* 26(5) 541–562, 2019; 49 CJ assessments;
  publisher-record abstract read): "CJ is generally associated with high levels of reliability";
  reliability rises with the number of comparisons — about **10–14 comparisons per piece for .70,
  26–37 for .90**; the plateau comes sooner with expert or peer judges than with novices.
- *Caveat — inflated reliability:* BW15 (Bramley & Wheadon 2015, AEA-Europe paper; abstract read
  at full text): with **adaptive** pairing, the reliability statistic (SSR) is "substantially"
  inflated — "high values of SSR (above 0.8) can even be obtained from random data." Wheadon is
  from No More Marking, so the company itself published the problem; non-adaptive designs avoid it.
- *Learning:* CJ is an **assessment** method. No test was found of whether using CJ improves
  student learning; its claim is more reliable measurement (and less marking time), which is what
  VBDD19 checks.

**Mechanism.** People judge *relative* quality more consistently than *absolute* quality against a
written descriptor (the Thurstone/Laming rationale; Laming's book not read → `uncertain` as
stated). Rubric descriptors ("uses a range of vocabulary") are read differently by different
markers; a pair comparison asks a simpler question.

**Serves:** *Seven Myths* — A3 rows 1, 3 and 4 (the case that facts underpin principles and
skills). CJ — assessing complex performances (extended writing; rows 3–4 outputs) where rubrics
are unreliable; also row 6 for teachers (calibrating one's own sense of quality).

**Grade:** as translator **M** (direction matches the research; detail unchecked; one-sided by
design); own method CJ **M–S for reliability** (VBDD19, with the adaptivity caveat BW15), **T /
untested for learning** (it measures, it does not teach).

**Use as learner.** Judge your own work by comparison: put your answer next to a model answer (or
two of your own attempts) and ask "which is better, and why?" — easier and more honest than giving
yourself a mark against a rubric.

**Use as teacher.** For essays and open answers, rank pairs instead of scoring against
descriptors; use enough comparisons per script (≈ 10–14 for .70), and distrust very high
reliability from adaptive tools. Use *Seven Myths* to argue for teaching knowledge — but keep the
boundary conditions (expertise reversal) in view.

## 7. The teacher-translators — Efrat Furst, Adam Boxer, Peps Mccrea, Blake Harvard (group entry)

> Roster §5 grades the group **M** (translation). Rule for this entry (hand-off): check **one
> load-bearing claim each** at the research it rests on. None of the four sells a tested package;
> each is graded as a translator.

**7a. Efrat Furst** (cognitive neuroscientist; Harvard Learning Incubator postdoc; blog/newsletter
*Making Sense of Learning Science*). *Claim checked* (EF26, 28 May 2026, post read): prior knowledge
"can help, hinder, or make little difference, depending on how it interacts with new ideas";
she separates **deep** prior knowledge ("multiple interconnected concepts, well organised into
larger, practised structures") from **shallow** ("sparse, newly learned concepts"), and calls
inaccurate deep knowledge a misconception. *At the research:* SFD22 meta (*Educational
Psychologist* 57(1) 31–54; 8,776 effect sizes; abstract read): prior knowledge correlates strongly
with **later knowledge** (r = .53) but almost not at all with **knowledge gain** (r = −.06), with a
very wide prediction interval (−.69 to .62) — "falsifies general statements such as 'knowledge is
power' as well as 'the effect of prior knowledge is negligible.'" Furst reports this correctly and
draws the right conclusion (it depends on the kind of prior knowledge). The deep/shallow split
itself is her teaching frame (citing Willingham 2003, not read) → `uncertain` as a mechanism.
*Grade:* translator **S** for this claim (accurate, cites the meta, keeps the nuance).

**7b. Adam Boxer** (chemistry teacher, Head of Science, CogSciSci editor; *Teaching Secondary
Science: A Complete Guide*, John Catt). *Claim checked* (via LB-AB review, T3 — book not read):
task complexity and abstraction raise working-memory load, background knowledge and teacher
support lower it → explicit instruction for novices. *At the research:* ZS24 (Zhang & Sweller,
*European Journal of Psychology of Education* 39(4) 3791–3801, 2024; N = 85 middle-school students,
randomised; abstract read): explicit instruction first helped **less** knowledgeable students;
investigation first helped **more** knowledgeable ones (a crossover). The direction matches Boxer's
framing and A5's expertise reversal. Caveat: ZS24 is one small study by CLT's founder, and the
debate with de Jong et al. (2023) is live — it supports the *conditional* claim ("explicit first
for novices"), not "explicit always". *Grade:* translator **M** (direction right; book not
checked line by line).

**7c. Peps Mccrea** (Ambition Institute; *Memorable Teaching*, *Motivated Teaching* 2020). *Claim
checked:* from the book's opening chapter (PM20 extract, Ambition Institute, read) — "Motivation
influences the behaviour, learning and life chances of our pupils"; and, summarised by reviewers
(not his verbatim), an *expectation* principle: pupils are drawn to what they expect to succeed
at, so "sow success". *At the research:* GC16 (*Child Development* 87(1) 165–175; 1,478 children,
Grades 1–4; abstract read): **achievement predicted later intrinsic motivation in maths; intrinsic
motivation did not predict later achievement at any time.** So the "success → motivation" half is
supported (in early-primary maths); the opening line's "motivation → learning" half is the
direction this study did **not** find. Older students differ: MPLV13 (N 3,530, grades 5–10;
abstract) found motivation and learning strategies predicted achievement *growth*, and GC16's own
review (full text, S12d) lists reciprocal findings — so the arrow runs both ways across ages. Mccrea's extract also cites "compelling evidence" that
motivation declines through compulsory schooling without naming it. GFG01 (Gottfried et al. 2001,
longitudinal; abstract read S12d) supports it: academic intrinsic motivation becomes more stable
with age and its **mean declines**, most in maths and not significantly in social studies — so the
decline is real but subject-dependent (whether this is the study Mccrea means is unconfirmed). *Grade:* translator **M** (the usable advice —
build success first — is supported; motivation-as-cause holds for older students (MPLV13) but not
in early-primary maths (GC16), and the extract states it without that boundary).

**7d. Blake Harvard** (AP Psychology teacher, Alabama; blog *The Effortful Educator*; *Do I Have
Your Attention?*). *Claim checked* (LB19 interview, 2019, read): "Research tells us that we forget
things very rapidly"; retrieving after some forgetting strengthens memory; "Rereading your notes is
not the most effective way to study." *At the research:* the forgetting curve (A2), retrieval >
rereading (A1, A4 S; RK06), and retrieval after forgetting = desirable difficulty (BB11, B4a §1) —
all S. He also reports a **classroom comparison**: one AP section with flexible seating vs a
parallel traditional section — the flexible-seating class "did worse" on unit exams, and he dropped
it, saying the method was imperfect. That is a useful **habit** (test your own changes against a
comparison) at **T** strength as evidence (two intact classes, no randomisation). *Grade:*
translator **S** for the claim checked; own classroom tests **T**.

**Mechanism (pointers).** Prior knowledge as the frame for new learning (A5 schemas; A2 LTM ↔ WM),
element interactivity and expertise reversal (A3 col.; A5), success → expectancy → effort (A7
self-efficacy; GC16), retrieval and forgetting (A1–A2, A4).

**Serves:** Furst — rows 2–3 (concepts, principles: how new ideas attach); Boxer — rows 3–4 in
science; Mccrea — row 6 (motivation, how a learner keeps going); Harvard — rows 1 and 6 (facts;
knowing which study methods work).

**Grade (group):** translators **M–S** (Furst S, Boxer M, Mccrea M, Harvard S on the claims
checked); no own-method packages to grade.

**Use as learner.** (Furst) Before a new topic, ask: is what I already know here *deep* (connected,
practised) or *shallow*? Shallow → build it first. (Mccrea) Start a hard subject with tasks you can
succeed at; motivation tends to follow success. (Harvard) Let some forgetting happen before you
re-test.

**Use as teacher.** Diagnose the *depth* of prior knowledge, not just its presence (SFD22: presence
alone doesn't predict gain). Sequence explicit-then-investigate for novices, the reverse only for
knowledgeable classes (ZS24, conditional). Engineer early success to build motivation (GC16). When
you change a practice, compare against a parallel class — and say how weak that test is.

## 8. Ali Abdaal and Thomas Frank — study-YouTube popularizers (included where a claim maps to research)

> Roster rule: **include only claims that map to research; drop the rest with a reason.** Both
> were caption-checked at one representative video each (auto-captions; numbers not trusted
> without the paper).

**8a. Ali Abdaal** (then a Cambridge medical student; channel later moved to productivity/business).
Video checked: AA18, "How to Study for Exams – Spaced Repetition | Evidence-based revision tips"
(uploaded 9 Apr 2018; 6,926-word auto-caption).

| Claim (his words, caption) | At the research | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Spacing beats cramming; crammed material is gone "by the next day or the following day" | Spacing effect (A4 S; C06) | **keep** — correct |
| Review "ideally by active recall" at spaced intervals | Successive relearning (RD11, B4a §4) | **keep** — correct |
| "Every time we interrupt the forgetting curve it then takes longer for us to forget"; illustrative "forget half … now only 25%" | Direction right (A2; spacing slows forgetting). The halves/quarters are an illustration, not data → not a fact | keep the direction, **drop the numbers** |

**Pattern (B5).** (1) Each practitioner wraps sound mechanisms (retrieval, spacing,
self-explanation, chunking, every-student-answers, explicit-first for novices) in a branded package;
the **package is untested, the parts are not** — grade the parts. (2) The weak links are the vivid
extras: a brain-network story (diffuse mode, "diffused mode" walks), a 20-minute promise (Feynman
Technique), n = 1 (MIT Challenge), observed outliers (TLAC), a schedule shape (1-3-7-30), a
reliability number computed the flattering way (adaptive CJ). (3) Named after a famous person ≠
that person's method (the Feynman Technique is Young's, 2011). (4) **Translators differ mainly in
what they leave out**: the better ones keep the boundary conditions (Furst on prior knowledge;
Abdaal and Frank labelling their own untested extras); the common omission is that a source strand
is correlational (Sherrington on Rosenshine's 80%) or one-directional (Mccrea's "motivation →
learning", where GC16 found the reverse). (5) Teacher-translators (§5–7) score higher as
translators than study-YouTubers (§8) and the paid-course seller (§1), but none of them has a
tested package of their own.
